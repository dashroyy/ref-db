## Why this matters

A motor is made one time and then it waits, often for years and sometimes for decades. It must operate on its last day
in storage exactly as on its first day. During this time it changes. Seals get a compression set, adhesives and
bondlines absorb moisture and oxidise, bolt preload relaxes, coatings break down at edges, and greases become dry. The
part that ages fastest sets the service life of the programme. To claim a service life, you need two things: a
**prediction** from accelerated tests, and **evidence** from surveillance of real aged hardware.

### What ages, and what to monitor

| Item | How it ages | Design response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Elastomer seals | Compression set, oxidation, loss of low-temperature resilience | Include the set in the minimum squeeze ([O-ring design](../../seals/o-ring-design/)); record cure dates; keep surveillance samples |
| Adhesive and bonded joints | Moisture absorption, plasticisation, interface degradation | Humidity-aged allowables; protect the edge of the bondline; witness coupons |
| Liner and insulation bonds | Plasticiser migration to and from the propellant; hardening | Compatibility tests; bond-strength surveillance with the energetics team |
| Bolted joints | Preload relaxation, embedding, creep of coatings and gaskets | Long grips, no soft layers in the clamp, a retorque policy if permitted |
| Coatings and platings | Breakdown at edges and damage; [galvanic attack](../../materials/galvanic/) | Also coat the cathode; inspect edges and fastener heads |
| Lubricants and greases | Drying, separation, migration onto seals | Compatible with the elastomer; specify the quantity, not "lightly" |
| Connectors and cabling | Contact corrosion; insulation becomes brittle | Sealed connectors; continuity and insulation checks in surveillance |

## Ageing is chemistry, and chemistry is temperature

Most ageing is a chemical reaction: oxidation, hydrolysis or cross-linking. Reaction rates follow the Arrhenius law,
$k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}$. Life is inversely proportional to rate. Thus the **acceleration factor** between a test
temperature and the service temperature is:

$$
AF = \exp\!\left[\frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_\text{service}} - \frac{1}{T_\text{test}}\right)\right]
\qquad R = 8.314 \ \text{J/(mol·K)},\ T \text{ in kelvin}
$$

The **activation energy** $E_a$ sets how quickly life decreases with temperature:

- The old rule "ageing doubles each 10 °C" is only one value of $E_a$: approximately **53 kJ/mol** near room
  temperature. At 80 kJ/mol, the rate doubles each 6.5 K.
- $E_a$ is a property of the **mechanism**, not of the material name. Values for polymer oxidation are approximately
  between 50 and 120 kJ/mol, and the result is very sensitive to the value. Measure it. Do not take it from a different
  source.

<Analogy title="Milk on the kitchen counter">
  Milk stays good for a week in the fridge, and it becomes bad in a day on a warm counter. A few degrees make a very
  large difference to the chemistry. Now think of a fridge that becomes warm for a few hours each afternoon. The milk
  becomes bad sooner than in a fridge kept steadily at the same average temperature, because the warm hours cause much
  more damage than the cold hours prevent.
</Analogy>

## The average temperature is the wrong average

A store that changes between 12 °C and 38 °C does not age hardware as a store kept at the average does. With
$E_a = 79$ kJ/mol, one hour at 38 °C causes the same ageing as 16 hours at 12 °C. The correct average is the **mean
kinetic temperature (MKT)**. This is the constant temperature that causes the same total ageing as the real, changing
record:

$$
T_\text{MKT} = \frac{E_a/R}{-\ln\!\left[\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} e^{-E_a/(R\,T_i)}\right]}
$$

This is for $n$ readings at equal intervals, in kelvin. The MKT is always equal to or more than the arithmetic mean. The
difference increases with the size of the temperature changes and with $E_a$. The method comes from pharmaceutical
storage (Haynes, 1971), where it is standard practice. **You cannot calculate the MKT without a temperature log.** This
is a good reason to require a log in the storage specification.

## Predicting service life from accelerated tests

ISO 11346 gives this method for rubbers:

1. **Select the property and its end-of-life limit.** Examples are the compression set at which a seal does not have
   sufficient squeeze, or the bond strength below which a joint does not meet its load.
2. **Age samples at three or more increased temperatures,** in the correct condition (seals compressed, bonds at the
   correct humidity). Record the time that each sample takes to get to the limit.
3. **Plot ln(life) against 1/T.** One mechanism gives a straight line, with a slope of $E_a/R$.
4. **Extrapolate to the MKT of the service conditions,** and compare with the necessary life, with the margin of your
   programme.

The possible errors:

- **One mechanism only.** If the test temperature is too high, a different chemistry starts. You then extrapolate an
  incorrect result with high precision. Points that are off the line at the hot end are the warning.
- **Thick sections age from the outside in.** At high test temperatures, the surface uses oxygen faster than the
  oxygen can diffuse inward. Thus the skin ages and the core does not. This never occurs in service. Thick parts need
  moderate test temperatures, or a check for this effect.
- **Long extrapolations make all errors larger.** A 10% error in $E_a$ can change a predicted life by a factor of 1.5
  or more. The calculator shows the effect for your data.
- **Field ageing can be different from the laboratory.** Long-term studies of rubbers stored for decades found lives
  shorter than the Arrhenius extrapolation predicted. Treat a prediction as a hypothesis that surveillance must test.

## Worked example: a seal in a hot store

The test data for an elastomer seal are illustrative. The time to get to its compression-set limit is 1400 h at 80 °C,
330 h at 100 °C and 90 h at 120 °C. These are the starting values of the calculator.

1. **Fit.** ln(life) against 1/T gives a slope of 9526 K. Thus $E_a = 9526 \times 8.314 = 79.2$ kJ/mol, with
   $r^2 \approx 1.000$.
2. **Store.** This is a store without temperature control in a hot climate, read each three hours over a typical day:
   12, 18, 24, 31, 38, 29, 21 and 15 °C. The mean is 23.5 °C, and the MKT is **26.9 °C**.
3. **Life in this store.** 18.9 years at the MKT. The plain average would give 27.2 years, which is 44% too high.
4. **Uncertainty.** The coolest test is 53 K above the store temperature. Thus a 10% error in $E_a$ changes the result
   by a factor of 1.6.
5. **Test plan.** At 80 °C the acceleration factor is 119. Thus 30 days of oven ageing represents approximately 9.7
   years in this store, and 62 days represents 20 years.
6. **Against the requirement.** If the requirement is 20 years, this seal does not meet it in this store. The options
   are: store the motors at a controlled 20 °C (39.9 years), select a more resistant compound, or claim a shorter life
   now and extend it with surveillance evidence.

## Surveillance

A prediction tells what must occur. Surveillance tells what occurred. A surveillance programme:

- **Keeps representative hardware and witness samples** (seal samples, bonded coupons, sample cartons) in storage with
  the fleet, in the same conditions.
- **Removes them on a schedule and tests them:** dimensions and leak checks, NDT (radiography or CT for debonds and
  cracks), dissection, mechanical tests of bonds and seals, and, for complete motors, static firing of aged units.
- **Plots the trend of the results** against the prediction and the end-of-life limit. With this evidence, it extends
  or decreases the service life.

<SurveillanceTrend />

Design for surveillance from the start. Put witness samples in the build, make the bondlines accessible for
inspection, record cure dates and batch traceability, and require a temperature log in storage.

## Where this stops

- **One mechanism with a constant activation energy.** Several mechanisms, humidity, oxygen supply and mechanical
  stress each need their own method.
- **A representative record.** The MKT is only as good as the log. One hot day is not a year. Use records that cover the
  full storage cycle.
- **Non-energetic hardware only.** The energetics specialists are responsible for the ageing of the propellant and the
  igniter, which includes chemical stability.
- **ISO 11346** also gives fitting by time–temperature shifting, and a maximum temperature of use. This page uses the
  simplest form.

## On the drawing and in the specifications

- **Record the cure dates and the batch** of each elastomer, and apply the shelf-life rules of ISO 2230 or SAE ARP5316
  before assembly.
- **Give the storage conditions** (temperature range, humidity, protection from ozone and UV) in the storage and
  handling specification, and require a temperature log.
- **Identify the items with a life limit,** with the basis of their life, so that you can trace a surveillance result
  back to a prediction.
- **Include witness samples in the build:** coupons bonded and cured with the hardware. You can then test something
  that is not the flight article.
