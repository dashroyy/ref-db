## Why this matters

Usually, each solid motor case has a proof test before it gets its insulation and propellant. It is the last check
between manufacture and a vessel that will hold an energetic load for years. A proof test finds:

- **Workmanship and material errors:** an incorrect heat treatment, a weak weld, a wall machined too thin, or a bad
  batch of material.
- **Cracks that nobody found.** This is the less obvious result. It is the reason why proof is part of the
  fracture-control case, and not only a quality check.
- **Leaks** at welds, bosses and seals.

## What a proof test proves, and what it does not

<Analogy title="A sieve">
  Pour a bag of gravel through a sieve. You never look at one stone, but you know that each stone that went through is
  smaller than the mesh. A proof test is a sieve for cracks. Each case with a crack that is sufficiently large to fail at
  proof pressure fails on the test stand, safely. Each case that survives has only cracks smaller than the "mesh size".
  This is also true for cracks that no inspection could find.
</Analogy>

<ProofSieve />

It does **not** prove:

- **That the case has margin to burst.** The proof pressure is below burst on purpose. Analysis, and tests to failure
  of qualification articles, show the burst capability.
- **That flaws stay small.** Cracks grow with each pressurisation and, in some environments, under sustained load. The
  proof screen gives the *starting* size for a life analysis. It is not the end of the analysis.
- **That the case survives loads other than pressure:** thrust, bending, handling and external pressure.
- **That the test caused no damage.** Proof is the highest stress that the part has before flight. It can grow
  sub-critical cracks. In a susceptible material and environment, it can leave the case in a worse condition than
  before.

## Choosing the proof factor

The proof pressure is MEOP times a proof factor (see the [pressure ladder](../../motor/case-sizing/#the-three-pressures)).
Requirements in two directions set the factor:

| Increases the factor | Decreases the factor |
| --- | --- |
| Smaller screened flaw, thus more crack-growth life | The proof stress must stay below yield, with margin for local peaks |
| Must cover the strength lost at the service temperature | The test can grow cracks and cause sustained-load damage |
| Must cover the toughness lost at the service temperature | Composites: fibre damage, stress rupture and damage accumulation |
| Must screen to a size that NDT cannot reliably see | Good hardware lost in the test, schedule and cost |

The applicable standard sets the factor for the class of vessel. The fracture-control plan checks that it gives
sufficient life. This site never gives the value. The calculator below starts with this field empty. Two corrections
are usually added:

- **Temperature.** If the case is weaker at its service temperature than at the test temperature, increase the proof
  pressure by the ratio of the strengths. The ratio of proof stress to strength then covers the service condition.
- **Toughness.** The screen works only if the material is at least as tough in the test as in service. The toughness of
  high-strength steels usually decreases with temperature. Thus a warm proof test can screen *nothing* for a cold-day
  firing. The calculator below shows how quickly this occurs.

## The flaw-screening argument

For a crack of depth $a$ in a wall at a stress $\sigma$, linear-elastic fracture mechanics gives the stress intensity
$K = Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}$. $Y$ is a geometry factor (approximately 1.12 for a long, shallow surface crack). The crack
runs unstably when $K$ gets to the fracture toughness $K_{IC}$. Thus:

$$
a_\text{screen} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_{IC,\text{proof}}}{Y\,\sigma_\text{proof}}\right)^2
\qquad
a_\text{crit} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_{IC,\text{service}}}{Y\,\sigma_\text{MEOP}}\right)^2
$$

Their ratio is the basis of proof-test logic (Tiffany and Masters, 1965):

$$
\frac{a_\text{crit}}{a_\text{screen}} = R^2 \left(\frac{K_{IC,\text{service}}}{K_{IC,\text{proof}}}\right)^2
$$

Here $R$ is the proof factor. In the worked example below, $R = 1.10$ lets the remaining cracks grow by 21% before they
become critical at MEOP. A larger $R$ helps as $R^2$. A loss of toughness in service has an effect as the square of
the toughness ratio.

The crack-growth life between the two sizes comes from the Paris law, $da/dN = C\,(\Delta K)^m$. With a constant $Y$,
the integral has a closed form:

$$
N = \frac{a_\text{crit}^{\,1-m/2} - a_\text{screen}^{\,1-m/2}}{C\,\left(Y\,\Delta\sigma\sqrt{\pi}\right)^m\,(1 - m/2)}
$$

Fracture-control standards for flight pressure vessels usually require that the analysed life is at least **four
times** the service life.

## Worked example: the D6AC case

This is the case from the [case-sizing example](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example): a hoop stress of 1094 MPa at
the 10 MPa MEOP, a 1.377 mm wall, $K_{IC} \approx 70$ MPa√m, a proof factor of 1.10, and a long surface crack
($Y = 1.12$). The proof factor and the life factor are for this example only. Push **Load worked example** in the
calculator to load these inputs.

1. **Proof stress.** $1.10 \times 1094 = 1204$ MPa, which is 83% of the 1450 MPa yield.
2. **Screened flaw.** $\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{70}{1.12 \times 1204}\right)^2 = 0.86$ mm. No case that passes proof
   has a crack deeper than this.
3. **Critical flaw at MEOP.** $\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{70}{1.12 \times 1094}\right)^2 = 1.04$ mm. The ratio is
   $1.10^2 = 1.21$.
4. **Life.** For martensitic steel, the upper limit of Barsom is $da/dN = 1.35 \times 10^{-10}\,\Delta K^{2.25}$. With
   full cycles from zero to MEOP, the crack takes approximately **105 pressurisations** to grow from 0.86 to 1.04 mm.
   For one firing plus one leak check, with a life factor of 4, the requirement is 8. The margin is approximately 13.
5. **NDT.** If inspection can only find cracks deeper than approximately 1.3 mm, it cannot support this case alone,
   because the critical size is smaller than the size that NDT can find. The proof test carries the fracture case. The [NDT page](../../make/ndt/#worked-example-can-ndt-clear-the-d6ac-case)
   checks each method against this case.

The same numbers give two warnings:

- **Cold service.** If the toughness at −40 °C is 20% lower (56 MPa√m), the ratio becomes $1.21 \times 0.8^2 = 0.77$.
  Cases that passed a room-temperature proof can then have cracks that fail on a cold day. The proof screen has no
  value unless the test is done cold, or unless you show that the toughness does not decrease.
- **Deep cracks.** The critical crack is 75% of the wall. At that depth, a constant $Y$ gives a value of $K$ that is
  too low. Thus the real sizes are smaller. This is also the [break-before-leak](../../motor/case-sizing/#leak-before-break)
  case that the case-sizing page identified. A released analysis uses a correct surface-crack solution.

## Test media and safety

### Why we proof with liquid

<Analogy title="A full suitcase versus a compressed spring">
  If you sit on a suitcase full of books, it compresses only a little, and almost nothing springs back when you stand
  up. A liquid is the same: it almost does not compress, so it stores very little energy. A gas is a coiled spring. All
  the work used to compress it comes back out at the moment when the vessel opens.
</Analogy>

The energy released if the vessel fails is:

$$
E_\text{liquid} = \frac{p^2 V}{2K}
\qquad
E_\text{gas} = \frac{p V}{\gamma - 1}\left[1 - \left(\frac{p_\text{atm}}{p}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right]
\qquad
E_\text{wall} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,p\,\Delta V
$$

For the 85-litre example case at 11 MPa, water stores approximately **2.3 kJ**, and the elastic strain energy of the
steel wall adds 5.2 kJ. The total is 7.6 kJ, which is approximately 2 g of TNT. The same test with nitrogen stores
approximately **1.7 MJ**, which is approximately 0.4 kg of TNT: **more than 200 times more**. This is why proof tests
are hydrostatic unless there is a very good reason, and why a pneumatic proof test needs special approval, remote
operation and much larger exclusion zones.

**Trapped air** is gas energy inside a hydrostatic test. It is most important in large tests at low pressure, where
the liquid stores little energy. Fill the vessels from the bottom, and bleed them from the highest point until only
liquid comes out.

### The medium must not attack the vessel

- **Stress-corrosion cracking.** High-strength steels can crack under sustained stress in plain water. Thus the proof
  water is inhibited and its chemistry is controlled, the hold times are short, and the case is drained and dried
  immediately.
- **Chemistry is important for all alloys.** Titanium pressure tanks failed during tests with methanol in the Apollo
  era, and chlorides attack stainless steels. Check the test medium against the material as carefully as you check the
  propellant.
- **Temperature.** Steel cases are tested well above any ductile–brittle transition, and at a temperature that keeps
  the toughness argument above valid.

### The rig and the schedule

<ProofRig />

<ProofProfile />

- **Remote operation.** Nobody is near a pressurised vessel. The cell is cleared and barricaded before the pressure
  goes above a low leak-check level.
- **A relief valve** set above the proof pressure and below any pressure that would damage the vessel, with rupture
  discs as backup.
- **Two independent, calibrated pressure measurements.** One gauge that reads low can over-pressurise a case.
- **Pressurisation in steps** with holds, and a slow final ramp. Thus a leak or an abnormal strain reading stops the
  test early.
- **Records.** The gauge calibrations, the liquid temperature, the pressure and strain histories, and the serial number
  of the part make the acceptance record.

## Acceptance criteria

A case passes proof when it meets all the criteria of the test specification. Usually these are:

1. **No burst and no leak.** Leaks are checked visually and by the pressure hold, after the temperature correction.
2. **No significant permanent deformation.** Measure it by volumetric expansion (water jacket), strain gauges or
   dimensional checks before and after. A frequent convention for gas cylinders limits the permanent expansion to 10% of
   the total expansion at test pressure. Motor cases use limits specific to the programme.
3. **The strain follows the prediction.** Strain gauges must follow the predicted elastic response linearly. A knee in
   the strain–pressure curve shows local yield.
4. **NDT after proof,** to look for cracks that the test possibly grew.
5. **Composite cases** add acoustic-emission criteria, because fibre damage and matrix damage make noise long before
   you can see them.

### Why hold pressure drifts

A vessel full of liquid is very stiff. Thus very small volume changes cause large pressure changes. A change in liquid
temperature changes its volume:

$$
\frac{dp}{dT} = \frac{\beta_\text{liquid} - 3\alpha_\text{vessel}}{1/K + C}
$$

Here $\beta$ is the volumetric expansion coefficient of the liquid, $\alpha$ is the linear expansion coefficient of the
vessel, and $C = \frac{1}{V}\frac{dV}{dp}$ is the volumetric compliance of the vessel. For the example case it is
approximately **0.12 MPa for each kelvin**, which is more than 1% of the proof pressure for one degree. Most "leaks"
during a pressure hold are the liquid that cools.

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Screening, not a fracture-control plan">
  These tools use linear-elastic fracture mechanics with a constant geometry factor and growth data typical of the
  class. A released proof-test case uses measured toughness and crack-growth data at the correct temperature and
  environment, surface-crack and embedded-crack solutions, crack growth during the proof cycle, sustained-load
  (environment-assisted) cracking, and the factors of the applicable standard.
</Aside>

- **Thin, tough walls** can tear in a ductile way before linear-elastic fracture mechanics applies. Then you need
  elastic-plastic methods.
- **Composite cases** are not screened in this way. Their proof logic is about damage, stress rupture and acoustic
  emission, not one crack.

### Differences from REF·DB v1

<Aside type="note" title="Crack-growth constants corrected">
  v1 gave the ferrite–pearlite constants ($6.9\times10^{-12}$, $m = 3.0$) the label "low-alloy / maraging steel". D6AC,
  maraging and other martensitic case steels use the martensitic upper limit of Barsom ($1.35\times10^{-10}$,
  $m = 2.25$), which predicts faster growth at the important stress intensities. Also, the austenitic coefficient of v1
  did not agree with the metric form of the line of Barsom. It is now $5.6\times10^{-12}$, $m = 3.25$.
</Aside>
