## Why this matters

You can see a bad mesh. You cannot see a bad material card. Some examples are a density in incorrect units, a Poisson's
ratio of 0.49 for propellant, an engineering stress–strain curve entered as true values, or a temperature table that
stops below the operating temperature. Each of these runs, converges and gives smooth, plausible contours. Card errors
are some of the most frequent errors in finite-element work, and some of the easiest to find.

Each card is also a set of decisions. Which data? On which basis (typical or minimum)? At which temperature and rate?
In which form (elastic, plastic, orthotropic, hyperelastic or viscoelastic)? A motor model needs all of them: a steel
or composite case, aluminium and titanium hardware, rubber insulation and seals, and a propellant grain that is very
different from all the other materials.

## What goes on a card

| Material | On the motor | Model form | Possible errors |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Metals | Case, closures, skirts, bolts | Isotropic elastic; true-stress plastic table; all against temperature | Units; engineering against true; typical against minimum |
| Fibre composites | Wound case, skirts | Orthotropic, for each ply or for the laminate | Material axes; major and minor Poisson's ratios; moisture and temperature |
| Rubbers | Insulation, O-rings, flex bearing | Hyperelastic on a bulk modulus; viscoelastic where rate is important | Poisson's ratio; the cold end near the glass transition |
| Propellant and liner | The grain | Viscoelastic on a bulk modulus, with time–temperature shift | Rate and temperature of the load case; stress-free temperature |
| Carbon, graphite, phenolics | Nozzle | Orthotropic, with a strong temperature dependence | Data at the temperatures that the part gets to |

Thermal models also need conductivity, specific heat and density for each material. See
[Transient thermal](../../motor/transient-thermal/). Strength is not on the elastic card. Margins come from the results,
checked against [allowables](../../materials/allowables/).

## Metals

**Elastic.** Use Young's modulus $E$ and Poisson's ratio $\nu$. These give $G = E/2(1+\nu)$. Give a solver two of the
three values. Never give three values that do not agree. Stiffness is always a typical value. There is no "minimum
modulus".

**Plastic.** Solvers need **true stress against true plastic strain**, which starts at zero plastic strain. From an
engineering curve, up to necking:

$$
\sigma_t = \sigma\,(1 + \varepsilon), \qquad \varepsilon_t = \ln(1 + \varepsilon)
$$

$$
\varepsilon_p = \varepsilon_t - \sigma_t/E
$$

If you have only the tensile properties, use a Ramberg–Osgood curve $\varepsilon = \sigma/E + 0.002\,(\sigma/F_{ty})^n$.
Put it through the 0.2% yield, and through the ultimate at the uniform elongation $e_u$. This gives the shape:

$$
n = \frac{\ln\!\left[(e_u - F_{tu}/E)/0.002\right]}{\ln(F_{tu}/F_{ty})}
$$

- **Typical or minimum?** Use typical curves for stiffness and to correlate with tests. Use minimum (A- or B-basis)
  curves when the plastic analysis is the strength check. Do not use the two in one model.
- **After the last row,** many solvers keep the stress constant (Abaqus does this). The true curve continues to
  increase after necking. Thus a table that stops at the ultimate is conservative for load, but it tells nothing about
  failure.

**Temperature.** Each property that changes over the range of the model needs a table against temperature. The table
must cover the full range. Outside the range, most solvers keep the end values. They do not extrapolate.

**Thermal expansion.** Handbooks give the **instantaneous** coefficient (the slope of the expansion curve at a
temperature) or the **secant** (mean) coefficient from a reference temperature. Solvers need secant values and the
reference temperature for them (Abaqus ZERO, the Ansys reference temperature, Nastran TREF):

$$
\bar\alpha(T) = \frac{1}{T - T_{ref}}\int_{T_{ref}}^{T} \alpha\,dT
$$

## Composites

An orthotropic card has nine constants: $E_1, E_2, E_3$, $\nu_{12}, \nu_{13}, \nu_{23}$, $G_{12}, G_{13}, G_{23}$.
Symmetry relates the major and minor Poisson's ratios, $\nu_{ji} = \nu_{ij}E_j/E_i$. The constants describe a real
material only if these conditions are true (Lempriere):

- each modulus is positive, and $|\nu_{ij}| < \sqrt{E_i/E_j}$;
- the compliance determinant is positive:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta = 1 &- \nu_{12}\nu_{21} - \nu_{23}\nu_{32} \\
&- \nu_{13}\nu_{31} - 2\nu_{21}\nu_{32}\nu_{13} > 0
\end{aligned}
$$

- **Conventions are different.** Some sources and solvers give $\nu_{21}$ where others mean $\nu_{12}$. If you type the
  minor ratio in place of the major ratio, all the checks above pass, but the card is incorrect.
- **For a unidirectional ply,** the 2–3 plane is usually isotropic: $E_3 = E_2$ and $G_{23} = E_2/2(1+\nu_{23})$.
- **Orientation.** The correct constants on incorrect material axes also pass all the checks. Plot the axes in the
  model.
- **Ply or laminate.** A wound case is made from ply data and winding angles. A smeared laminate card hides the
  bending–stretching coupling and the through-thickness behaviour.

## Rubbers and propellant

It is easy to change the shape of rubber and propellant, but it is very difficult to change their volume. Their bulk
modulus $K$ is thousands of times their shear modulus. Poisson's ratio is then a derived value, and its fourth decimal
place is important:

$$
\nu = \frac{3K - E}{6K}
$$

<Analogy title="A water balloon">
  When you squeeze a water balloon, it bulges in a different location. It is easy to change its shape, but almost
  impossible to make it smaller. If you give it a Poisson's ratio of 0.49 in a model, it becomes a sponge. It is still
  soft in shape, but now it is also soft in volume: sixty times too soft for propellant. All the results that depend on
  its volume change are then incorrect, for example the pressure on a bonded grain, or a seal in its groove.
</Analogy>

- **Get $K$ from a volumetric test,** and calculate $\nu$ from it. Never round to 0.49 or 0.5.
- **Hyperelastic forms** (neo-Hookean with $C_{10} = G/2$ and $D_1 = 2/K$, Mooney–Rivlin, Ogden) are fitted to
  uniaxial, planar and equibiaxial tests together. A fit to one test only can become unstable in a different mode.
  Check the stability over the strain range of the model.
- **Viscoelastic.** Stiffness depends on rate and temperature, thus a card is for one load case. Time-dependent loads
  need a Prony series and a shift function (see [Viscoelasticity](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/)). Thermal loads
  need the stress-free temperature ([Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/)).
- **Use hybrid (mixed) elements.** Usual elements lock when the material is almost incompressible, and they are much
  too stiff.

## Where the numbers come from

| Material | Sources | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metals | MMPDS; minimums of the material specification; certificates for the lot | MMPDS gives design allowables, typical moduli, stress–strain curves and temperature effects |
| Composites | CMH-17; supplier data; tests (ASTM D3039, D6641, D7078) | Datasheet values are typical, usually at room temperature and dry |
| Rubbers | Tests: uniaxial (ASTM D412), planar, equibiaxial, volumetric | Hardness gives only an approximate modulus |
| Propellant | Tests to the ICRPG/JANNAF mechanical behaviour manual: tension over rates and temperatures, stress relaxation, bulk modulus | Aged and fresh; the capability of the grain comes from the same programme |

A "typical" value from a supplier is not an allowable. A handbook value at room temperature tells nothing about 150 °C.
Put the source, its basis, the temperature and the material condition on the card with the numbers.

## Checking a card before you trust it

1. **Units.** Use one consistent system. Check the density with the wave speed $\sqrt{E/\rho}$, which is some
   thousands of m/s for all metals. Compare the total mass of the model with the CAD or the scales. A static model does
   not show a density error. The first vibration or acceleration case shows it.
2. **Consistency.** Isotropic: $E$, $\nu$ and $G$ agree. Orthotropic: symmetry, the limits and the determinant.
   Almost incompressible: $\nu$ from a measured $K$.
3. **Basis and purpose.** Typical or minimum, as necessary for the margin calculation.
4. **Plastic tables.** True values, the first row at zero plastic strain, increasing values, the last row at necking,
   and known behaviour after it.
5. **Temperature and rate.** The tables cover the range of the model. Rubber and propellant data agree with the load
   case. Expansion is secant, with its reference temperature.
6. **Orientation** of orthotropic materials in the model.
7. **Single-element tests.** Load one element and compare the result with the card:

| Test | Load | Must give |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uniaxial stress | Stretch, sides free | $E$; $\nu$ from the lateral strain; the plastic curve |
| Pure shear | Shear | $G$ |
| Hydrostatic | Pressure on all faces | $K$ |
| Free heating | Uniform temperature change, no restraint | Thermal strain, $\bar\alpha\,\Delta T$ |
| Mass | Gravity or a frequency | Density |

8. **Traceability.** Record the source, basis, temperature, condition, revision and owner. A card is a controlled
   input, like a drawing.

## Calculator

The first tab writes a card for any material in the site's material library. Select the material, the solver and the
unit system. The tab writes the input text for Abaqus, Ansys MAPDL or Nastran, and you can copy it or download it.
**Use in the check tabs** copies the same values into the other tabs, so that you can check them there.

- The card contains the elastic constants, the density, the secant expansion, and the conductivity and specific heat
  when the library has them.
- For a metal, enter the uniform elongation from your material data to add a true-stress plastic table.
- For a metal, the tab also draws an **estimated S-N curve**: a Basquin line from 0.9 × the ultimate at 10³ cycles to
  the endurance strength at its knee, corrected for surface, loading, size and reliability (the Marin factors of
  [Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/)). Copy the points for a fatigue post-processor. It is a mean curve for
  screening, not an allowable: use test data for a fatigue clearance.
- A comment line in the card identifies each value that comes from a family default. The card leaves out each value
  that is not in the library, and the tab tells you which values these are.
- The library values are typical room-temperature values. They are applicable to stiffness, mass and correlation. For
  a strength check, use the allowables from your material specification.

### Adding a material

All the tools on the site use one material library, `src/data/materials.json`. When you add an entry to this file,
the new material appears in each tool that can use it: the material chart, case sizing, buckling, fatigue, fracture,
proof testing, differential expansion, fits, bolted joints, sealing, transient thermal, insulation, the grain screen,
the hand calcs, FEA & test, residual stress, random vibration
and this card generator. A tool shows a material only when the entry has the properties that the tool needs.

1. Copy an entry of the same family and change the values. The units are g/cm³ for density, GPa for $E$, MPa for the
   yield and the ultimate, µm/m·K for expansion, W/m·K for conductivity and MPa√m for toughness.
2. Add the optional values that you have: `nu` (Poisson's ratio), `cp` (J/kg·K), `eu` (uniform elongation, %) and
   `kz` (conductivity through the thickness of a laminate). If you do not give `nu` or `cp`, the tools use the family
   default and mark it.
3. Give the source of the values in `spec` or `summary`.
4. Run `npm test`. The tests make sure that each material has a unique name and a known family, and that the values
   are in a physical range.

## Worked example: cards for the 300 mm motor's model

The starting values in each tab of the calculator are the values of this example. The library card starts with the
D6AC case in mm, N, tonne and seconds. The uniform elongation, the ply
constants and the expansion coefficients are illustrative.

1. **D6AC case, elastic values and units.** In mm, N, tonne and seconds: $E = 205\,000$ MPa and $\nu = 0.29$, so
   $G = 79\,457$ MPa. The density is $7.85 \times 10^{-9}$ t/mm³. The wave speed is
   $\sqrt{205\,000/7.85\times10^{-9}}$ mm/s $= 5110$ m/s, which is correct for steel. If you type the density as 7850,
   the wave speed decreases to 5 mm/s, and the 10.2 kg cylinder has a mass of $1.02 \times 10^{10}$ t. A static
   pressure analysis does not show this error. It shows at the first vibration load.
2. **D6AC case, plastic values.** $F_{ty} = 1450$ MPa, $F_{tu} = 1520$ MPa and, for this example, 5% uniform
   elongation give $n = 64.9$. This is very little hardening, as in most high-strength steels. The table goes from
   1394 MPa at zero plastic strain to **1596 MPa at 4.10% true plastic strain**. The true stress at necking is 5% more
   than the engineering ultimate. One stretched element in the model must follow the table, and then keep 1596 MPa
   constant.
3. **Carbon-fibre skirt, orthotropic values.** A typical carbon/epoxy ply has $E_1 = 160$ GPa, $E_2 = E_3 = 9$ GPa,
   $\nu_{12} = 0.32$, $\nu_{23} = 0.45$, $G_{12} = 5$ GPa and $G_{23} = 3.1$ GPa. It passes: the determinant is 0.781,
   $\nu_{21} = 0.018$, and $G_{23}$ agrees with $E_2/2(1+\nu_{23}) = 3.10$ GPa. If you change $E_1$ and $E_2$ but keep
   the Poisson's ratios, $\nu_{21}$ becomes 5.7 and the determinant becomes −1.03: **this is not a material**. If you
   type the minor ratio 0.018 as $\nu_{12}$, all the checks pass, but the sideways contraction of the ply is 18 times
   too small. Only the source can show this error.
4. **Propellant.** The relaxed modulus of the grain is 3.6 MPa and its bulk modulus is 3.5 GPa
   ([Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/)). Thus $\nu = 0.499829$, $G = 1.20$ MPa, $C_{10} = 0.600$ MPa and
   $D_1 = 5.7 \times 10^{-4}$ MPa⁻¹. An estimated $\nu = 0.49$ gives $K = 60$ MPa, which is **58 times too soft** in
   volume. At 10 MPa, the volume of the grain would decrease by 16.7%, not 0.29%. The ignition-strain term that uses
   $p/K$ on the grain page would be $10/60$, not $10/3500$, and the predicted bore strain would increase from 5.7% to
   71%. If you round in the other direction, to 0.4999, $K$ is 1.7 times too stiff.
5. **Aluminium closure, thermal values.** After burnout, heat goes back into an aluminium closure and it gets to
   150 °C. The instantaneous $\alpha$ increases from $21 \times 10^{-6}$/K at −50 °C to $25 \times 10^{-6}$/K at
   150 °C. The closure is assembled at 20 °C, and this is the reference temperature. The secant coefficient to 150 °C
   is $23.7 \times 10^{-6}$/K, and the thermal strain is 3081 µε. If you enter the instantaneous value of 25 as a
   secant value, the closure grows **5.5% too much**. If the tables of the card stopped at 100 °C, the solver would use
   the stiffness, strength and expansion at 100 °C for all temperatures up to 150 °C.

## Where this stops

- **Cards, not data.** All the numbers on this page are examples. Design values come from your sources and tests.
- **Simple forms.** The plasticity here is isotropic hardening from a monotonic curve. Cyclic loads, kinematic
  hardening, rate dependence of metals, damage and failure need more.
- **Linear expansion data.** Real coefficients are curves. The calculator uses a straight line between two points.
- **Composites** need failure criteria, through-thickness data and process data that the elastic card does not have.

## On the drawing

The card is not on the drawing, but the two must agree:

- **Material and condition** (specification, temper or heat treatment) are the same as the source of the card.
- **Process-dependent properties** (fibre volume, winding angle, cure, propellant batch and age) are controlled on the
  drawing or its process specifications if the model uses them.
- **The analysis report** gives each card with its source, basis, temperature range and revision.
