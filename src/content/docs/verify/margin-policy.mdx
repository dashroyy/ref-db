## Why this matters

The margin of safety is the main result of a stress report. There is one number for each failure mode. It tells if a
part is sufficiently strong, and by how much. Reviewers do not check each calculation. They check that each margin
comes from a consistent set. Most review findings come from four errors:

- **Mixed pairs:** a yield allowable against an ultimate load, or the opposite.
- **The incorrect basis:** a typical value or a room-temperature value in a hot check with a single load path.
- **Missing factors:** a fitting factor or casting factor that the specification requires, but that is not used.
- **Too much conservatism:** a pessimistic load, times a pessimistic allowable, times a pessimistic model. This gives a
  heavy part and a margin that nobody believes.

## Load levels

<LoadLevels />

- **Limit load** is the most severe load that is expected in service. The load rules of your programme define it. For
  a pressure vessel, it is MEOP.
- **Yield design load** is the limit load × the yield factor. At this load, there must be no yield that has an effect
  on the function: no important permanent set, no leak and no misalignment.
- **Ultimate design load** is the limit load × the ultimate factor. At this load, there must be no rupture or collapse.
- **For pressure vessels,** the proof and burst factors have these functions. See [case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/)
  and [proof testing](../proof-testing/).

## The margin of safety

$$
MS = \frac{\text{allowable}}{k \times \text{applied}} - 1
\qquad
\text{pass when } MS \ge 0
$$

Here $k$ is the factor for that check. The applied value is at limit level, and the allowable has the correct basis at
the correct condition. European practice often gives the **reserve factor**, $RF = MS + 1$, which passes when
$RF \ge 1$.

The formula is correct with stresses only when the stress is proportional to the load. For **buckling, contact,
preloaded joints, large deflections and plasticity,** apply the factor to the *load*, and do the analysis at the
factored load: $MS = \text{failure load} / (k \times \text{limit load}) - 1$. It is a programme rule if thermal
stresses and preloads get a factor, and which factor. Give this rule in the report.

<Analogy title="A bathroom scale">
  A scale that shows pounds and a weight limit in kilograms can both be correct, and the comparison can still be
  incorrect. A margin is the same. The allowable, the factor and the load can each be correct, but they must be in the
  same "units": yield with yield, ultimate with ultimate, the same temperature and the same direction.
</Analogy>

### Pairing: the most common finding

The [case-sizing worked example](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example) has a hoop stress of 1094 MPa at MEOP, a
yield allowable of 1305 MPa and an ultimate allowable of 1368 MPa (with the weld efficiency), with example factors of
1.10 and 1.25:

| Pairing | Margin | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yield allowable, yield factor | $1305 / (1.10 \times 1094) - 1 = +0.08$ | Correct |
| Ultimate allowable, ultimate factor | $1368 / (1.25 \times 1094) - 1 = 0.00$ | Correct |
| Yield allowable, ultimate factor | $1305 / (1.25 \times 1094) - 1 = -0.05$ | A failure that is not real |
| Ultimate allowable, yield factor | $1368 / (1.10 \times 1094) - 1 = +0.14$ | A pass that is not real |

Always calculate yield and ultimate as **separate rows**.

## Special factors

Specifications often require additional factors for some features. These multiply the basic factor:

| Factor | Applies to |
| --- | --- |
| Fitting factor | Joints and fittings, where the stress state is not certain |
| Bearing factor | Bearing at fastener holes with clearance or vibration |
| Casting factor | Cast parts, related to the inspection level |
| Weld factor | Welded joints, related to the procedure and the inspection |
| Model-uncertainty factor | Analysis methods that a test has not validated |

If a necessary factor is missing, all the margins that follow from it are incorrect. As with the basic factors, their
values come from your specification. This site does not give them.

## Combined loading

- **The same stress component from several loads.** Add the stresses at limit level, then apply the factor to the
  total. For ductile metals under combined normal stress and shear stress, use von Mises:
  $\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2}$.
- **Different load types or failure modes,** for example a fastener in tension and shear, or a shell under axial load,
  bending and pressure. Use an interaction equation:

$$
\sum_i R_i^{\,e_i} \le 1,
\qquad
R_i = \frac{k \times \text{applied}_i}{\text{allowable}_i}
$$

Linear interaction ($e = 1$) is the most conservative. Elliptical interaction ($e = 2$) is usual for fasteners in
tension and shear. Use the form and the exponents that your applicable standard specifies. For spaceflight fasteners,
this is NASA-STD-5020.

The margin tells how much **all the loads can increase together** before they get to the curve: $MS = \lambda - 1$,
where $\sum (\lambda R_i)^{e_i} = 1$. For linear interaction this is $1/\sum R_i - 1$. For elliptical interaction it is
$1/\sqrt{R_1^2 + R_2^2} - 1$. A frequent shortcut, $1/\sum R_i^2 - 1$, is incorrect. It gives a margin that is much too
high.

## Conservatism and the heavy-part rule

Conservatism is acceptable when you know where it is. Record its location (the load, the allowable or the model), so
that you can remove it on purpose if mass becomes a problem, and do not find it only at the end. On a part where mass is
important, a large margin is also a finding. A general rule: above approximately 0.5, the part is heavier than
necessary, or a different failure mode or load case governs and you have not found it yet.

## The margin report

Use one row for each failure mode and each load case. Give all the data that a reviewer needs to check it without
questions:

| Column | What goes in it | Example: the hoop-burst row |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Item / location | The part and the location on it | Case cylinder, mid-length |
| Load case | Which limit case | MEOP |
| Failure mode | What would occur | Hoop burst |
| Applied (limit) | Limit-level value, with units | 1094 MPa |
| Factor(s) | Basic and special factors, with their source | Ultimate 1.25 (example), spec clause |
| Allowable | Value, basis, condition and source | 1368 MPa, A-basis ultimate × weld 0.9, 20 °C, MMPDS |
| MS | The margin | 0.00 |
| Reference | The calculation or analysis run | Case-sizing calc, §3 |

- **Give the lowest margin for each item** at the start of the report. Never hide a negative margin.
- **Give units in each column,** and refer to the analysis or finite-element run, so that the margin is traceable.
- **Give the status** (preliminary, design or final), because allowables and loads change during a programme.

## Worked example: a motor case and its closure bolts

The rows of the calculator are the 300 mm D6AC case from the other pages, plus its aft-closure bolts. The factors are
**example values only**: yield 1.10, ultimate 1.25, buckling 1.25, and a fitting factor of 1.15 on the bolts. Push
**Load worked example** to load them.

1. **Hoop yield at proof:** $1305 / (1.10 \times 1094) - 1 = +0.08$.
2. **Hoop burst:** $1368 / (1.25 \times 1094) - 1 = 0.00$. The margin is exactly zero, because the case was sized to
   burst.
3. **Buckling of the empty case under vacuum:** $0.1262 / (1.25 \times 0.101) - 1 = -0.0004$, shown as −0.00. This
   is a fail. A negative margin fails, however small. See
   [shell buckling](../shell-buckling/#worked-example-the-300-mm-case-unpressurised).
4. **Closure bolt, tension and shear:** 18 kN on a 60 kN allowable, and 4 kN on 40 kN (illustrative). The factor is
   $1.25 \times 1.15 = 1.44$, so $R_1 = 0.431$ and $R_2 = 0.144$. Elliptical interaction gives
   $\lambda = 1/\sqrt{0.431^2 + 0.144^2} = 2.20$, so **MS = +1.20**. Linear interaction would give +0.74. The incorrect
   shortcut $1/(R_1^2 + R_2^2) - 1$ would give +3.84.

The lowest margin is the buckling of the empty case at −0.00, so the case governs and the report has a fail. Add a
ring or a thicker wall (as on the shell-buckling page), or support the case on a mandrel during the vacuum check. The
hoop burst at 0.00 is next. The bolt margin of +1.20 is a finding under the
heavy-part rule. Either a different check sizes these bolts (preload, joint separation or thread engagement) and needs
its own row, or the bolts could be smaller. [Bolted joints](../../joints/bolted-joints/) does those checks for the same
closure.

## Where this stops

- **Factors, load definitions and margin rules** come from the requirements of your programme, for example
  NASA-STD-5001 or ECSS-E-ST-32-10 for spaceflight structures. This page tells how they fit together. It does not give
  them.
- **Linear responses only** for stress-based margins. Non-linear responses need load-based margins at the factored
  load.
- **Fatigue and fracture** use life and flaw size, with their own factors. See [Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/)
  and [Proof testing](../proof-testing/).

## On the drawing and in the report

- **The drawing gives what the margins assumed:** material specification and temper, finish, inspection class, and
  weld and casting quality levels. If a margin needed a casting factor related to an inspection level, that level must
  be on the drawing.
- **The report gives the full set:** for each row, the load, the factor and the allowable with their sources, and the
  lowest margin for each item at the start.
