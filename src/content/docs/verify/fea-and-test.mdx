## Why this matters

A finite-element model always gives a result, in colour, to four significant figures. If the result is correct depends
on decisions that the contour plot does not show: what the model does not include, how fine the mesh is where it is
important, what holds the model, and how the loads are applied. Most incorrect analyses are not caused by a solver
failure. They are incorrect because the model answered a different question, and nobody checked.

Two words help:

- **Verification.** Are the equations solved correctly? This includes code checks, mesh convergence and equilibrium.
- **Validation.** Are they the correct equations? Only a comparison with a test can show that the idealisation is
  sufficiently good.

NASA-STD-7009 and ASME V&V 10 give the two processes for models whose results support decisions.

<Analogy title="A map">
  A map is useful because it does not show everything. A road map has no contour lines, and a hiking map has no traffic
  lights. If you use the incorrect map, all the details can be accurate but the route is incorrect. A model is a map of
  the part for one question. A converged mesh makes the map sharper. It does not make it the correct map.
</Analogy>

## From question to model

Start with the question, for example the burst margin of the case, the seal-gap opening at the aft joint, a bolt load,
or the natural frequency of a bracket. Then select the simplest model that can answer it:

| Model | Good for | Does not include |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hand calc | Sizing; far-field stresses; checks of all other models | Detail, interaction |
| Axisymmetric shell | Case, domes and skirts under pressure and axial load; junction bending | Bolts, local 3-D detail, non-axisymmetric loads |
| Axisymmetric solid | Closures, bosses, flange sections, thick parts, through-thickness stress | Discrete bolts (smeared into a ring), bending loads |
| 3-D sector with cyclic symmetry | One bolt pitch of a flange joint, with contact and preload | Loads that are not the same at each bolt |
| Full 3-D | Bending and side loads, real asymmetric detail, assemblies | Nothing, but it is the most difficult to check |

- **Symmetry also needs symmetric loads.** An axisymmetric case model cannot take a bending moment. A quarter model
  cannot take an asymmetric load.
- **Shells or solids.** Thin walls ($r/t$ more than approximately 10) are shells. Junctions, lands and thick parts are
  solids. Linear solid elements lock in bending. Use quadratic elements, or several linear elements through the
  thickness.
- **Linear or nonlinear.** Large deflection of thin shells, plasticity near burst, and contact in joints and seals each
  make the model nonlinear. Each has its own convergence and checks.

## Mesh and convergence

A finite-element result has a discretisation error, which decreases as you refine the mesh. The only way to know its
size is to solve on three meshes and look at the quantity that you will report. For element sizes $h_1 < h_2 < h_3$,
with a constant ratio $r$ and results $f_1, f_2, f_3$:

$$
p = \frac{\ln\big[(f_3 - f_2)/(f_2 - f_1)\big]}{\ln r}
$$

$$
f_{exact} \approx f_1 + \frac{f_1 - f_2}{r^p - 1}
$$

The **grid convergence index**, $\text{GCI} = 1.25\,|(f_2 - f_1)/f_1|\,/\,(r^p - 1)$, puts a band on the finest result
(Roache). The method is valid only with three conditions:

- **Monotonic convergence.** The changes decrease and keep their sign. If they oscillate or increase, refine more, or
  look for a singularity.
- **The asymptotic range.** The observed order $p$ is near the theoretical order of the element, and
  $\text{GCI}_{32}/(r^p\,\text{GCI}_{21})$ is near 1. Meshes that are too coarse for the feature give results that
  look precise but are incorrect.
- **The important quantity, at the important point.** Displacements converge on coarse meshes. Peak stresses need
  several elements across the zone where they change. For a shell edge, this zone is $\pi/\beta \approx 2.4\sqrt{rt}$
  from [Hand calcs](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/#thin-shells).

## Element choice and mesh quality

Convergence tells you that the mesh is fine enough. The element type and the shape of each element tell you if the
mesh can give the correct answer at all.

**Select the element for the task:**

| Task | Use | Avoid | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Thin walls: case, skirt, dome | Shell elements | One linear solid through the thickness | A single linear solid layer cannot bend correctly |
| Solid parts with a stress concentration | Second-order (10-node) tetrahedra, or second-order hexahedra | First-order (4-node) tetrahedra | First-order tetrahedra are too stiff (see below) |
| Bending of solid parts | Second-order or incompatible-mode hexahedra | First-order fully integrated hexahedra | Shear locking makes them too stiff in bending |
| Rubber and propellant | Hybrid (mixed) formulation | Standard displacement elements | Volumetric locking near ν = 0.5 ([FEA cards](../fea-cards/)) |
| Contact surfaces | Matched, refined meshes on the two sides | A coarse mesh on one side | Contact pressure and the gap need several elements across the contact |
| Bolts in a joint model | Beam or solid bolt with a pretension section | Rigid links that carry no preload | The joint behaviour depends on the preload ([Bolted joints](../../joints/bolted-joints/)) |

**The first-order tetrahedron problem.** The 4-node tetrahedron has a constant strain. It is much too stiff in bending,
so it predicts too little displacement and too little stress, which is the dangerous direction. Automatic meshers use it
by default because it fills any shape. A dense first-order mesh that looks correct can be 20–30% low on a bending stress.
Use second-order tetrahedra, or hexahedra where the shape permits.

**Check the shape of the elements.** Each solver reports these metrics. A single bad element at a fillet can set the
peak stress that you report. The limits below are typical starting values; your analysis procedure sets the real ones.

| Metric | Good | Marginal | Reject | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aspect ratio (longest ÷ shortest edge) | under 3 | 3–10 | over 10 in a stress gradient | Long elements cannot follow a fast change of stress across their short side |
| Jacobian ratio | over 0.7 | 0.3–0.7 | under 0.3, or negative | Distortion of the element from its ideal shape; a negative value is an inverted element |
| Corner angles, triangles and tetrahedra | 30° or more | 20–30° | under 15° | Small angles give poor stress results |
| Corner angles, quadrilaterals and hexahedra | 45°–135° | 30°–150° | outside 20°–160° | Very distorted corners give poor stress results |
| Warpage of quadrilateral faces | under 5° | 5–15° | over 15° | A warped shell or face is not flat, and bends incorrectly |
| Mid-side node position (second-order) | on the straight line or the surface | small offset | far from the middle | Curved edges that do not follow the geometry give false stresses |

Look at the metrics where the stress is high, not only as a summary for the full model. A poor element far from the
point of interest is usually acceptable; one at the peak is not.

## Singularities

At some points, the stress does not converge. At a sharp internal corner, the elastic stress is infinite. Near the
corner it increases as $r^{\lambda-1}$, with the Williams exponent $\lambda = 0.545$ for a 90° corner and 0.5 for a
crack. Each time you halve the mesh, the peak increases by $2^{1-\lambda} = 1.37$, with no limit.

<FeaConvergence />

The same occurs at point loads, point constraints, contact edges and the corners of bonded dissimilar materials. Thus:

- **Model the real radius.** If the drawing has a 0.5 mm fillet, the model must also have it, with a mesh that is
  sufficiently fine to converge. The radius then becomes a drawing requirement.
- **Read stresses where a method defines them.** Use through-thickness linearisation into membrane and bending for
  pressure-vessel codes, hot-spot extrapolation from fixed distances for welds, and stress intensity for cracks.
- **Spread loads and supports** over a realistic area. Apply them with a coupling that does not make the part stiffer,
  for example Nastran RBE3 or an Abaqus distributing coupling, unless the real part is rigid.

## Boundary conditions and loads

- **Remove all rigid-body motions, and no more.** A model with too few constraints does not solve. A model with too
  many constraints solves, but it gives stresses that the real part never has. If you fix the end of a case model
  rigidly, but the real flange grows and turns with the wall, you get the clamped-edge bending of the
  [hand calc](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/#thin-shells). This is approximately two times the hoop stress, and it is
  not real.
- **Free bodies stay free.** Nothing holds a motor in flight. Balance its loads with inertia (inertia relief). Do not
  add a support that does not exist.
- **Apply pressure to all wetted faces,** which includes the closures and the nozzle as well as the wall. Check the
  reaction.
- **Symmetry planes** need the symmetry conditions, not only a cut.

## Contact and preload

Joints and seals have contact: surfaces that can separate and slide, and that carry pressure only in compression.

- **Apply the preload first, then the load.** Put the bolt pretension in its own step. Check the clamp force against
  the [Bolted joints](../../joints/bolted-joints/) calculation before you apply the service loads.
- **Initial gaps and overlaps** in the geometry become forces or free play at the first increment. Remove them.
- **Contact must also converge.** Contact pressure needs a sufficiently fine mesh on the two surfaces. Check the
  penetration, and make sure that the contact status does not change back and forth between increments.
- **Monitor the artificial terms.** Stabilisation energy or hourglass energy must be a small fraction of the strain
  energy (a few per cent maximum). If it is not, the result comes from the numerics, not from the part.
- **Seal gaps** come from separation at the seal line, for the coldest case with the lowest preload. See
  [Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/).

## Before the results go in a report

1. **Inputs.** Geometry at the drawing revision, checked [material cards](../fea-cards/), and units.
2. **Reactions.** They are equal to the applied loads, to round-off. A static model is in equilibrium, or a load is
   missing.
3. **Deformed shape.** Animate it at a sensible scale. It must move as the part would move.
4. **Far field.** Compare membrane stresses, displacements and frequencies with the hand calc.
5. **Convergence** of each reported quantity, at its point.
6. **Singularities.** Find them, and do not read a result at one.
7. **Diagnostics.** Check solver warnings, element distortion, negative eigenvalues, and the energy balance in
   nonlinear and explicit runs.
8. **Sensitivity.** Put limits on the uncertain inputs: support stiffness, friction, gaps and material scatter.
9. **Validation.** Compare with a test if there is one ([Test correlation](../test-correlation/) is the next step).
10. **Record.** Write the question, the assumptions, the inputs and their sources, the checks and the results, so that
    a different engineer can do the analysis again.

## The model and the test

The model helps to plan the test, and the test checks the model:

- **Put gauges in small gradients,** where a small position error is not important. Compare them with the strain of
  the model averaged over the gauge length, on the same surface.
- **Put gauges on the two surfaces** where the part bends. One surface cannot separate membrane from bending.
- **Use rosettes** where you do not know the principal directions. For a rectangular rosette with gauges $a$, $b$, $c$
  at 0°, 45° and 90°:

$$
\varepsilon_{1,2} = \frac{\varepsilon_a + \varepsilon_c}{2} \pm R
$$

$$
R = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{(\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_b)^2 + (\varepsilon_b - \varepsilon_c)^2}
$$

- **A proof test is a validation test.** With gauges at the correct locations, it checks the case model at a known
  load on the real hardware. See [Proof testing](../proof-testing/).

## Worked example: checking a model of the case at its aft flange

The question is the peak stress in the wall of the 300 mm D6AC case where it meets the aft flange, at MEOP (10 MPa).
The model is the simplest one that can answer it: an axisymmetric shell. Here it is a real one-dimensional
finite-element model of the wall, which runs in the **Mesh convergence** tab. The end is clamped to represent the
flange.

1. **The free body.** The first run had pressure on the wall but not on the closures. The far-field hoop stress was
   1094 MPa, which is exactly $pr/t$, so it looked correct. But the axial reaction was **zero**. It must be
   $10 \times \pi \times 150^2 = 707$ kN. The radial growth also showed the error: 0.804 mm, which is 17% more than the
   closed-end 0.688 mm and exactly the open-end value. With the end load applied, the two values agree. (These
   first-run values are the defaults in the **Equilibrium & far field** tab.)
2. **Mesh convergence.** Elements of 16, 8 and 4 mm give peak axial stresses of 1811, 2108 and 2205 MPa. The
   convergence is monotonic, the observed order is 1.62, the extrapolated value is **2252 MPa**, the GCI is 2.7% and
   the asymptotic ratio is 1.05. You can trust this study. The exact result for this model is 2241 MPa. The 4 mm mesh
   is 1.6% low and the extrapolation is 0.5% high. A study on 32, 16 and 8 mm meshes has fewer than five elements across
   the 35 mm edge zone. It gives an order of 0.87 and an extrapolated value of 2467 MPa, which is 10% high. With coarser
   meshes, the results do not converge.
3. **Converged is not the same as correct.** The 2241 MPa is real for this model, but it is not real for the case. It
   comes from the clamp. The [hand calc](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/#thin-shells) gives the same value for a rigid
   edge, approximately two times the hoop stress. The real flange grows and turns with the wall, so the model must
   include it, or at least its stiffness. The wall also needs a land where it joins the flange. If the inside corner of
   the land were a sharp 90°, the peak would increase by 1.37 times each time you halve the mesh. Model the fillet
   radius of the drawing.
4. **The test.** At the 11 MPa proof test, a rosette is 100 mm from the flange. This is almost three edge zones away,
   in a small gradient. In this example it reads 1260, 3170 and 4950 µε at 0°, 45° and 90° from the axis. The
   **Strain gauges** tab gives 1190 MPa hoop and 603 MPa axial. The model gives 1204 MPa and 602 MPa, so the
   differences are 1.2% and 0.2%. This validates the membrane model at that location. The edge region needs its own
   gauges, or the comparison must wait for [test correlation](../test-correlation/).

## Where this stops

- **Method, not a solver manual.** Element formulations, contact algorithms and solver settings are different between
  codes. Follow the documentation of your code and the modelling guidelines of your organisation.
- **Richardson extrapolation** assumes smooth convergence in the asymptotic range. It is not applicable at singular
  points, or to quantities that change their behaviour, such as contact status or buckling modes.
- **The edge model** is linear, axisymmetric and thin-shell. It is a demonstration with a known result, not a design
  tool.
- **Validation needs tests** that are designed for it, with loads, boundary conditions and measurements that the model
  can represent.

## On the drawing

- **The radii and lands that the model used,** as minimum dimensions, so that nobody can make the part sharper than
  the analysis.
- **The drawing revision** of each model, recorded in the analysis.
- **Gauge locations, orientations and surfaces** on the test drawing or procedure, the same as the read-out points of
  the model.
