## Why this matters

All cases contain flaws. Fracture control is about what the flaws do. A crack that grows in the wall can have one of
two results:

- **Leak before break.** The crack grows through the wall and becomes a through crack that is sufficiently short to be
  stable. Gas escapes and the pressure decreases. The failure is visible and contained.
- **Break before leak.** The crack becomes critical while it is still below the surface, or it goes through the wall
  when it is already too long to be stable. The case bursts with no warning, and it releases all of its stored energy
  at one time.

Toughness, stress and wall thickness decide which result occurs. This is a design decision, also if nobody makes it on
purpose.

<Analogy title="A slow puncture or a blowout">
  A slow puncture makes a hissing noise, the tyre becomes soft, and you stop. A blowout gives no warning. The two start
  with damage in the wall. The difference is if the hole stays small when it opens. This depends on the toughness of
  the wall compared with the force that pulls it.
</Analogy>

## Two questions

1. **Does the flaw go through the wall before it becomes critical?**
2. **When it goes through, is the through crack stable?**

### 1. Growing through the wall

Real flaws are **surface cracks**, approximately half-ellipses with a depth $a$ and a length $2c$. Their stress
intensity comes from the fit of Newman and Raju to finite-element results:

$$
K = \sigma\sqrt{\frac{\pi a}{Q}}\;F\!\left(\frac{a}{c}, \frac{a}{t}, \varphi\right),
\qquad
Q \approx 1 + 1.464\left(\frac{a}{c}\right)^{1.65}
$$

$K$ is different at each point of the front. At the **deepest point**, it moves the crack through the wall. At the
**surface points**, it moves the crack along the wall. Each point grows by the Paris law, $da/dN = C\,\Delta K^m$. A
long, shallow flaw becomes more semicircular as it becomes deeper. Near the back face, the surface points grow faster
and the crack becomes longer.

**Breakthrough** occurs when the crack gets to the back face, or when the thin ligament in front of it fractures or
yields first. In the two cases, the flaw becomes a through crack with a length of approximately its surface length
$2c$. If $K$ at the surface points gets to $K_{IC}$ first, the crack runs along the wall while it is still below the
surface, and the case bursts.

### 2. Is the through crack stable?

A through crack in a flat plate has $K = \sigma\sqrt{\pi c}$. In a pressurised cylinder, its edges **bulge** outward,
which opens the crack more. The Folias factor $M$ includes this effect:

$$
K = M\,\sigma\sqrt{\pi c},
\qquad
M = \sqrt{1 + 0.6275\,z - 0.003375\,z^2},
\qquad
z = \frac{(2c)^2}{D\,t}
$$

This is the modified-B31G form, for $z \le 50$. In a tough material, the ligaments at the crack tips yield before they
fracture. Thus the crack can also fail by **plastic collapse** when $M\,\sigma = \sigma_\text{flow}$. Here, the flow
stress is the mean of the yield strength and the tensile strength. The through crack is stable while:

$$
\sigma_\text{hoop} < \min\!\left(\frac{K_{IC}}{M\sqrt{\pi c}},\; \frac{\sigma_\text{flow}}{M}\right)
$$

This gives the longest stable through crack, $2c_\text{crit}$. The design is **leak before break** if the crack goes
through the wall with a shorter length. The ratio of the two lengths is the margin.

[The simple screen of Irwin](../../motor/case-sizing/#leak-before-break) requires that a flat-plate through crack with
a half-length equal to the wall is stable. It is a quick version of the same method, and it gives the same result in
the examples below.

## Why high-strength cases usually fail the test

The stable crack length changes as $(K_{IC}/\sigma)^2$. Toughness usually decreases as strength increases, and
high-strength cases operate at high stress. Thus $c_\text{crit}$ decreases quickly. D6AC at 1094 MPa holds a through
crack of only approximately 2.6 mm, in a wall 1.4 mm thick. There is almost no space for a crack to go through before
it is already too long.

**Size also has a bad effect.** At the same hoop stress, a larger motor has a proportionally thicker wall. The crack
must be longer to go through it, but the stable length does not change. A small case that leaks first can become a
large case that bursts, in the same material at the same stress.

## What it means for a motor case

<Aside type="caution" title="A leak in a burning motor is not safe">
  In flight, a crack through the case of a burning motor releases hot combustion gas. This gas usually burns through
  and stops the flight. Leak before break does not make a flight failure safe. Fracture-control standards for
  spaceflight hardware give credit for leak before burst only where the leak itself is not hazardous.
</Aside>

Where it is useful:

- **On the proof-test stand.** A flaw that grows during proof leaks on the stand. Water that comes out of a hydrostatic
  test is easy to see. The case does not burst near the test crew.
- **As a measure of robustness.** The ratio of the stable length to the breakthrough length tells how much a flaw that
  was not found could grow before it is important.
- **In the remainder of the system.** Pressurant bottles and actuator accumulators contain gas whose leak can be
  harmless. There, leak before burst can be an important part of the fracture-control case.

Where leak before break is not true (most high-strength steel cases), safety depends on **screening and inspection**.
The [proof test](../proof-testing/) makes sure that no flaw larger than its screened size stays. NDT and the
crack-growth life then continue the protection.

## Worked example: three cases at the same MEOP

All three are the 300 mm, 10 MPa case from [case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example), each sized there with
the same factors. Each starts from a surface flaw 0.25 mm deep and 1 mm long ($a/c = 0.5$), cycled from zero to MEOP.

1. **D6AC:** 1.377 mm wall, hoop stress 1094 MPa, $K_{IC} = 70$ MPa√m. The longest stable through crack is 2.58 mm
   ($c_\text{crit} = 1.29$ mm; $M = 1.005$, so bulging is almost not important at this length; fracture governs). With
   the upper-limit growth law for martensitic steel, the flaw grows for approximately 1800 cycles. It is then 92%
   through the wall and 3.3 mm long, and its surface points get to $K_{IC}$. It runs along the case. **Break before
   leak.** The screen of Irwin agrees: $c_\text{crit}/t = 0.95 < 1$.
2. **15CDV6:** a steel with lower strength and higher toughness. When sized to the same MEOP, its wall is 2.23 mm and
   the hoop stress is 679 MPa, with $K_{IC} = 90$ MPa√m. The longest stable through crack is 10.2 mm. The flaw goes
   through the wall with a length of 5.7 mm. **Leak before break,** with a margin of 1.8 on length. The cost is a wall
   that is 62% thicker, thus a case that is approximately 60% heavier.
3. **Maraging 18Ni-250:** stronger *and* tougher ($K_{IC} = 100$ MPa√m). A 1.21 mm wall at 1242 MPa still leaks first,
   with a margin of 1.29. The case is also lighter than the D6AC case (10.6 kg against 11.8 kg). Toughness, not low
   stress, can give leak before break. The cost is in the material, not in the mass.

Within one class of steel, the trade is clear. A thinner wall decreases the mass but loses leak before break. A tougher
alloy can get it back without the mass, but the material costs more.

<Aside type="note" title="Few cycles, but the shape is still important">
  A motor case has only a few pressure cycles (proof, leak check and one firing). Thus fatigue growth in service is
  small. The growth model is here for the *shape* of the crack as it becomes deeper. The shape decides how long the
  crack is when it goes through the wall.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

- **Screening only.** This is linear-elastic fracture with a separate plastic-collapse check. For ductile materials,
  the correct assessment uses tearing resistance, with fracture and collapse together on a failure assessment diagram
  (BS 7910, R6).
- **The surface-crack fit** covers $a/t \le 0.8$ and $a/c \le 1$. Outside this range, the calculator extrapolates. Real
  breakthrough can be dynamic and make the crack longer.
- **Toughness** must be measured at the correct temperature, orientation and thickness. Welds and heat-affected zones
  are often the least tough material in the case.
- **Axial cracks** are assessed, because the hoop stress is two times the axial stress. Circumferential cracks need
  their own check where axial loads or bending loads are high.
- **Environment.** Stress-corrosion cracking and sustained-load cracking are not included.
- **Detection.** A leak is useful only if somebody finds it in time to act. This page does not calculate that part of
  the case.

## On the drawing

- **Record the fracture-control approach** (leak before break or safe life) in the drawing notes or the fracture
  control plan, so that inspection and test know what they protect.
- **Specify the minimum toughness** in the material specification, at the important temperature and orientation. The
  result depends on it, and a typical datasheet value is not a requirement.
- **Relate the NDT acceptance to the initial flaw** that the analysis assumed. If the analysis starts from 0.25 mm, the
  inspection must find 0.25 mm reliably, or the proof test must screen it.
- **Make proof testing an acceptance requirement,** with the pressure, the hold and the leak criteria. See
  [Proof testing](../proof-testing/).
