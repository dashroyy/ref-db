## Why this matters

A motor spends almost all of its life on the ground. It is lifted, put in cradles, driven, flown, shipped, stored for
years and lifted again, and sometimes it is dropped. The [duty cycle](../../fundamentals/duty-cycle/) lists these
events. Their loads are often larger than the loads in flight, and they act in directions that the flight does not
load: down on the side of the case, along the case from a rail coupling, and into the lift points at an angle.

The case is designed for pressure, which loads its thin wall evenly. A cradle loads it on a small area. For the 300 mm
motor at 3 g, the case as a beam has 1 MPa of bending stress; at the edge of a narrow cradle, the ring bending in the
same case is 94 MPa. In a drop it can yield the case. A dropped motor is also a safety event: inspect it, or scrap it.

## The intuition: a drinks can

<Analogy title="A drinks can">
  A full can of a fizzy drink holds several bar of pressure, which is a large load on its thin wall. But you can dent
  it with your thumb. The wall is strong when the pressure pulls it evenly in every direction, and weak against a load
  on a small area, because that load bends it. A motor case is the same: designed for pressure, it needs wide cradles,
  rings or pads to carry its own weight on a few points.
</Analogy>

## The loads before flight

| Event | The load | What it loads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lifts | The weight times a dynamic factor, at the lift points, with the angle of the slings | The lugs, the skirts, the case between the lift points |
| Cradles, in storage and in transport | The weight times the vertical, lateral and longitudinal load factors | The case at the cradles; the grain and its bond |
| Road, rail, air and sea | Vibration for long durations, and shocks: rail couplings, potholes, landings | Fatigue of brackets and joints; preload; the grain |
| Drops and impacts in handling | Short pulses of tens of g | The case at its supports, and the parts that ride on mounts |

The levels come from your programme and its environment specifications, for example MIL-STD-810 (Method 514 for the
vibration of transport, Method 516 for shock). This page does not give them.

## The motor as a beam

On two supports, each at a distance $A$ from its end, a motor of mass $m$ and length $L$ under $n$ g carries
$w = n\,m\,g/L$ along its length. Each support takes $Q = n\,m\,g/2$, and

$$
M_\text{support} = \frac{w A^2}{2}\ \text{(hogging)}
\qquad
M_\text{mid} = \frac{w\,(L - 2A)^2}{8} - \frac{w A^2}{2}\ \text{(sagging)}
$$

The two are equal when $A = (\sqrt{2} - 1)\,L/2 = 0.207\,L$. The stress in the case is $M/(\pi R^2 t)$, and the
compression side must not buckle in bending ([Shell buckling](../shell-buckling/)). For a motor, these stresses are
usually small: the [hand calcs](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/) find 5 MPa for the 300 mm motor at 3 g. The problem is
local, at the supports.

## Cradles: the local problem

<CradleLift />

Zick analysed a thin horizontal cylinder on two saddles, and his method is still the standard for pressure vessels. At
a saddle of contact angle $\theta$ and width $b$, three stresses matter in a shell with no ring:

$$
\sigma_\text{long} = \frac{M_\text{support}}{K_1\,\pi R^2 t}
\qquad
\tau = \frac{K_2\,V}{R\,t}
\qquad
\sigma_\text{horn} = \frac{Q}{4t\,(b + 1.56\sqrt{Rt})} + \frac{3K_6\,Q}{2t^2}
$$

The last term is $12K_6\,Q\,R/(L\,t^2)$ when $L < 8R$. $V$ is the shear force just inside the saddle.

| Contact angle $\theta$ | $K_1$: the part of the section that still works in bending | $K_2$: the shear, gathered near the horns | $K_6$: the ring bending at the horn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 120° | 0.107 | 1.171 | 0.0528 |
| 150° | 0.161 | 0.799 | 0.0317 |

- **The ring bending at the horn governs a thin case.** It grows as $1/t^2$, and the wall that the pressure sizes is
  thin. Over the saddle, the case also flattens, and only an arc at the top still carries the beam bending.
- **The levers:** a wider contact angle (150° or more), a wider cradle, a wear plate or a ring at the cradle, and
  cradles within about half a radius of a dome, where the dome stiffens the shell. A rubber pad on the cradle spreads
  the load and protects the surface.
- **A loaded motor is different from an empty case.** The bonded grain resists the ovalisation of the case, so the
  ring bending in the case is lower than Zick's value for an empty shell. The load then goes through the grain in
  compression, and at the edges of the cradle the bond sees shear. Give the pad a smooth, rounded edge. An empty case,
  in manufacture or after a proof test, has only its wall.

## Lifts

- **Lift points** go on the skirts, on a handling ring or on the closures, never on the thin membrane of the case.
  Proof-load each lifting feature to your lifting standard, and mark it. Check each lug as a lug
  ([Hand calcs](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/#lugs-and-pins)).
- **Slings at an angle** $\beta$ from the horizontal carry $T = Q/\sin\beta$ in each leg, and they push the two lift
  points together with $Q/\tan\beta$. At 30°, the push is 1.7 times the lift-point load. A spreader beam keeps the
  legs vertical and removes the push.
- **The hook must be above the centre of gravity,** or the motor tilts in the slings. Mark the centre of gravity on the
  motor and on its drawing.
- **The dynamic factor** for the crane and the lift comes from your lifting standard.

## Drops and the shock response

The [duty cycle](../../fundamentals/duty-cycle/#handling-transport-and-shock) gives the peak of a drop: a half-sine
pulse of duration $\tau$ with the peak $\pi (1 + e)\,v/(2\tau)$. That is the acceleration of the motor as a rigid body.
A part that rides on its own mount, such as a bracket, an instrument, or the nozzle on its joint, responds to the pulse
like a mass on a spring:

- **Near $f_n = 0.8/\tau$** the part sees up to 1.77 times the pulse, with no damping. For a 10 ms pulse, that is a
  part near 80 Hz.
- **Stiff parts** ($f_n \gg 1/\tau$) see the pulse itself.
- **Parts on soft mounts** ($f_n < 0.27/\tau$) see less than the pulse: the mount isolates them.

This is the **shock response spectrum** of the pulse. A cushion or a sprung cradle lengthens the pulse and lowers its
peak, but it also moves $0.8/\tau$ down. Check the parts whose frequencies are near the new value.

## The grain in transport

- **The bond carries the weight of the grain** in every direction: $\rho\,n\,g\,(b^2 - a^2)/(2b)$, the same as along
  the axis ([Bondlines](../../motor/bondlines/#the-bond-in-the-long-cylinder)). In long horizontal storage, the bore
  slumps sideways under the weight of the web.
- **Vibration:** the grain is heavy and well damped. It adds mass to the case and to its supports
  ([Random vibration](../random-vibration/)).
- **Temperature:** an unheated truck or aircraft hold can set the coldest day of the life of the grain, and with it the
  bond and bore strains ([Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/#cool-down-why-the-bore-cracks)).

## Worked example: the 300 mm motor in its cradles, in a lift and in a drop

The loaded motor has a mass of 137.8 kg. It is 1150 mm long: the 1000 mm cylinder and its domes, without the nozzle.
It sits in two cradles 240 mm from each end; for this length, 238 mm balances the moments. The case is D6AC with a mean
radius of 150.69 mm and a 1.377 mm wall. The cradles touch it over 120° and are 50 mm wide. The load factor of 3 g is
the one in the hand calcs. The criteria (a factor of 1.10 on yield, a lift dynamic factor of 1.3, and a handling limit
of 100 g for a part on the motor) are **for this example only**. Push **Load worked example**.

1. **The beam.** Each cradle carries 2.03 kN. The moment is 0.10 kN·m over the cradles and at midspan, which gives
   1.0 MPa at midspan.
2. **The cradle, at 3 g.** Over the cradle, only 10.7% of the section works, so the longitudinal stress is 9.7 MPa.
   The shear is 6.7 MPa. The ring stress at the horn is **94 MPa**, of which 89 MPa is ring bending: 96 times the beam
   stress. MS = $1318 / 93.9 - 1 = +13.0$ against the yield of 1450 MPa ÷ 1.10.
3. **The lift.** Slings at 60° from the horizontal, at the cradle positions, with the dynamic factor of 1.3: each leg
   carries 1.01 kN, and the slings push the lift points together with 0.51 kN.
4. **The drop.** The [duty-cycle drop](../../fundamentals/duty-cycle/#worked-example-the-life-of-the-300-mm-motors-case) gives
   58.3 g for 10 ms. Set the load factor to 58.3: the ring stress at the horn is **1825 MPa**, more than the yield.
   MS = −0.28. The narrow cradle dents the empty-shell model of the case.
5. **A better cradle.** Make it 150° and 100 mm wide: 1093 MPa at the horn, MS = +0.21. A cushion that lengthens the
   pulse to 19.4 ms also works: it halves the peak (see the duty-cycle example).
6. **A part on the motor.** On the **Shock response** tab, a part at 80 Hz sits at $f_n\tau = 0.8$. It sees 1.77 times
   the pulse: **103 g**, over its 100 g limit (MS = −0.03). With 5% damping in its mount it sees 96 g (MS = +0.04). A
   mount below 27 Hz would isolate it.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the example shows">
  At 3 g, nothing is close. The drop is twenty times larger, and it finds the weak point of a thin case: ring bending
  at the horn of a narrow cradle. A wider cradle fixes the case. The part on its mount needs its own check, because
  the mount can amplify the shock that the motor feels.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Screening values">
  These are first-order checks for the layout of cradles, lifts and mounts. The real loads come from your environment
  specifications, and the real stresses from FEA and tests.
</Aside>

- **Zick's analysis** here is for an empty shell with no ring at the saddle. It does not include the grain, the bond
  under the cradle, wear plates or rings (Zick and Moss give the last two).
- **Lateral and longitudinal loads,** such as rail couplings, which load the skirts and the restraints of the motor.
- **The restraint of the motor** in its cradles: straps, friction and the stops.
- **Other pulse shapes,** and parts with more than one mode. The spectrum gives the peak, not the history.
- **Fatigue from transport vibration** of brackets and joints ([Random vibration](../random-vibration/)).

## On the drawing and in the specifications

- **The lift points:** their proof load, their marking, and the centre of gravity of the motor.
- **The cradle interfaces:** the support positions, the contact angle and width, the pad material, and the zones of
  the case where no support may go.
- **The handling limits** in g for the motor and for its parts, and the temperature limits of transport and storage.
- **The transport environment:** the vibration and shock levels and their durations.
- **The inspection after a drop** or an impact above the limits, and who decides whether the motor can still be used.
