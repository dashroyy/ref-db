## Why this matters

A static margin cannot predict fatigue. A bracket that carries ten times its working load one time can crack after a
few million cycles at one third of that load. Fatigue is the most frequent cause of failure of mechanical hardware in
service.

A solid motor case has only a few pressure cycles (proof, leak check and one firing), so fatigue is seldom critical for
it. But fatigue is critical for much of the hardware around it:

- handling and transport equipment, lifting points, and the attachment points of the motor;
- the brackets and linkages of thrust-vector actuators, and the bolts in each joint;
- brackets and mounts that vibration loads during transport and flight;
- test-stand hardware and reusable test cases, which have many cycles.

<Analogy title="Bending a paperclip">
  Bend a paperclip one time and nothing occurs. Bend it back and forth twenty times and it breaks, at a load that it
  survived nineteen times. Each cycle caused a little damage that you could not see. Fatigue in hardware works in the
  same way, with smaller strains and millions of cycles.
</Analogy>

This page covers **initiation**: stress-life, for a part with no known flaw. When a crack is present, use fracture
mechanics. [Proof testing](../../verify/proof-testing/) grows a known flaw with the Paris law. If you use the incorrect
method, the error is frequent and expensive.

## The S-N curve

Fatigue strength is measured on polished coupons, cycled at constant amplitude and fully reversed. The results are
plotted as stress against cycles to failure, on log scales.

- **Steels and titanium have an endurance limit** at approximately $10^6$–$10^7$ cycles. Below this limit, clean coupons
  at room temperature do not fail. For steels, a usual estimate is $S'_e \approx 0.5\,S_{ut}$. Above
  $S_{ut} = 1400$ MPa, it stays at approximately 700 MPa.
- **Aluminium alloys have no endurance limit.** Their fatigue strength is given at $5 \times 10^8$ cycles. It is
  approximately $0.4\,S_{ut}$, but not more than approximately 130 MPa, and the curve continues to decrease.
- **Between $10^3$ cycles and the endurance limit,** the curve is almost a straight line on log scales (Basquin's law).
  It goes from approximately $0.9\,S_{ut}$ at $10^3$ down to $S_e$.
- **A life is a distribution, not a number.** At one stress, coupon lives usually have a scatter of a factor of ten.

## From a laboratory coupon to your part

The coupon is small and polished, loaded in bending, and tested in a laboratory. Your part has none of these
properties. The Marin factors correct for the differences:

$$
S_e = k_a\,k_b\,k_c\,k_d\,k_e\;S'_e
$$

| Factor | Corrects for | Typical effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $k_a$ surface | Roughness: cracks start at the surface | Machined 1280 MPa steel 0.68; ground 0.86; as-forged 0.22 |
| $k_b$ size | A larger section has more surface and more inclusions where cracks can start | 0.88 at 25 mm in bending; 1 in axial loading |
| $k_c$ loading | Axial loading has no gradient to slow an early crack | 1 bending, 0.85 axial |
| $k_d$ temperature | Strength loss and oxidation when hot | 1 at room temperature |
| $k_e$ reliability | From the median coupon life to a survival probability | 0.81 for 99%, 0.75 for 99.9% |

The surface factor decreases fastest for **high-strength steels**. The stronger the steel, the more a rough surface
decreases its fatigue strength. This is why fatigue-critical features on strong parts are ground, polished or peened.

## Notches

A hole, a fillet or a thread increases the local stress by the elastic stress-concentration factor $K_t$. Get $K_t$
from charts such as those of Peterson. The fatigue effect is a little less than $K_t$, because a small notch also has a
steep stress gradient. The notch sensitivity of Peterson gives the fatigue factor:

$$
K_f = 1 + q\,(K_t - 1),
\qquad
q = \frac{1}{1 + a/r}
$$

Here $r$ is the notch radius, and $a$ is a material length: approximately $0.0254\,(2070/S_{ut})^{1.8}$ mm for steels,
and approximately 0.64 mm for aluminium alloys. Stronger steels have a smaller $a$, thus they have almost the full
$K_t$. This gives the design rules: large radii, notches kept apart, and nothing added on a fillet (no thread run-out,
no cross-hole and no marking).

## Mean stress

A tensile mean stress decreases the life. A compressive mean stress increases it. The usual criteria change a cycle
with an amplitude $\sigma_a$ and a mean $\sigma_m$ into an equivalent fully reversed amplitude $\sigma_{ar}$:

| Criterion | $\sigma_{ar}$ | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goodman | $\sigma_a / (1 - \sigma_m/S_{ut})$ | Standard; conservative for ductile metals |
| Gerber | $\sigma_a / \left(1 - (\sigma_m/S_{ut})^2\right)$ | A parabola, nearer to test data for ductile metals |
| Soderberg | $\sigma_a / (1 - \sigma_m/S_y)$ | To yield; the most conservative |
| Smith–Watson–Topper | $\sqrt{\sigma_\text{max}\,\sigma_a}$ | Frequently used with strain-life methods and for aluminium |

Usual practice gives **no credit for compressive mean stress**. **Residual stress acts as a mean stress.** Shot peening
and rolling put the surface in compression and increase the life. Welding puts it in tension and decreases the life.
See [Residual stress](../../materials/residual-stress/).

## Variable loads: Miner's rule

Real parts have a spectrum of loads. Count the cycles into blocks of similar amplitude and mean. Rainflow counting
(ASTM E1049) is the standard method for a measured load history ([Duty cycle](../duty-cycle/#rainflow-counting)).
Then add the fraction of life that each block uses:

$$
D = \sum_i \frac{n_i}{N_i},
\qquad
\text{failure predicted at } D = 1
$$

The rule is simple and frequently used. But measured damage sums at failure have a scatter of approximately 0.3 to 3,
and the sequence of the loads is important, which the rule does not consider. For this reason, programmes apply a
factor on life. With an endurance limit, the rule gives no damage for blocks below $S_e$. But in reality, occasional
large cycles can decrease the endurance limit. The Haibach modification continues the S-N line below $S_e$ with a
smaller slope to cover this.

## Worked example: an actuator bracket

This is a machined 4340 steel bracket for a thrust-vector actuator. It has a 25 mm section in bending, with a fillet of
$K_t = 1.8$ and a radius of 2 mm, at 99% reliability. For each service life, it has transport vibration (90 MPa
amplitude on a 50 MPa mean, 5 million cycles), handling (zero to 500 MPa, thus 250 on 250, 2000 times) and firings
(180 on 60 MPa, 20,000 cycles). These are the starting values of the calculator.

1. **Coupon strength.** $S'_e = 0.5 \times 1280 = 640$ MPa.
2. **To the part.** $k_a = 4.51 \times 1280^{-0.265} = 0.677$, $k_b = 1.24 \times 25^{-0.107} = 0.879$ and
   $k_e = 0.814$. Thus $S_e = 640 \times 0.677 \times 0.879 \times 0.814 = 310$ MPa, which is less than half the
   strength of the coupon.
3. **The notch.** $a = 0.0254 \times (2070/1280)^{1.8} = 0.060$ mm, so $q = 1/(1 + 0.060/2) = 0.971$ and
   $K_f = 1 + 0.971 \times 0.8 = 1.78$.
4. **The S-N line** goes from $0.9 \times 1280 = 1152$ MPa at $10^3$ cycles to 310 MPa at $10^6$. The slope is
   $b = \log(310/1152)/3 = -0.190$.
5. **Each block** (Goodman, with $K_f$ on the amplitude and on the mean):
   - Transport: local 160 on 89 MPa → $\sigma_{ar} = 172$ MPa, below $S_e$: **no damage**.
   - Handling: local 444 on 444 → $\sigma_{ar} = 444/(1 - 444/1280) = 680$ MPa →
     $N = 10^3 (680/1152)^{-1/0.190} = 16{,}000$ → $D = 0.125$.
   - Firings: local 320 on 107 → $\sigma_{ar} = 349$ MPa → $N = 538{,}000$ → $D = 0.037$.
6. **Total** $D = 0.162$. The median life of the bracket is approximately **6.2 service lives**, before the factor on
   life of your programme.

This example has two lessons. First, the 2,000 handling cycles cause **77%** of the damage, and the 5 million vibration
cycles cause no damage. Second, if you **grind the fillet**, $k_a$ increases to 0.86 and $S_e$ to 394 MPa. The firing
loads are then below the endurance limit, and the life increases to **14.8 service lives**. This is 2.4 times longer,
only from the surface.

For a different case, try 7075-T6 with all stresses divided by three (an aluminium bracket would be sized for lower
stresses). There is no endurance limit, so the 5 million vibration cycles now cause approximately a quarter of the
damage.

## What makes fatigue worse or better

| Worse | Better |
| --- | --- |
| Tensile residual stress: welds, abusive grinding | Compressive residual stress: shot peening, cold rolling |
| Corrosion and corrosion pits; corrosive environments remove the endurance limit | Protective finishes selected for fatigue as well as corrosion |
| Fretting at clamped and bolted interfaces | Preloaded bolts: the bolt then gets only part of the alternating load |
| Hard chromium plating on steel; hard anodising on aluminium | Large radii, notches kept apart |
| Handling damage, transverse tool marks, stamped markings | Fine finish along the load direction; etched marks, not stamped marks |
| High temperature, hydrogen embrittlement | Welds kept out of the regions with the highest stress |

## Where this stops

- **High-cycle, stress-life only.** Below approximately $10^3$–$10^4$ cycles, or where the notch yields, use strain-life
  (Coffin–Manson) methods. See [stress- and strain-based failure](../../verify/random-vibration/#stress-based-and-strain-based-failure).
- **When there is a crack,** use crack growth: the Paris law on the [proof-testing page](../../verify/proof-testing/).
- **Multiaxial and non-proportional loading** need special criteria. This page uses one equivalent stress.
- **Random vibration** is changed into cycles with a spectral method. See [Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/).
- **Welded joints** are assessed with detail categories (for example BS 7608 or the IIW FAT classes), not with Marin
  factors. See [Welding](../../make/welding/#fatigue-of-welded-joints).
- **All the results here are estimates.** Optimism in fatigue causes failures. Coupon tests and full-scale tests must
  substantiate a fatigue-critical part.

## On the drawing

- **Surface finish on fatigue-critical features,** as an Ra limit and, where it is important, a lay direction along
  the load. See [Surface finish](../../drawing/surface-finish/#fatigue-critical-fillets).
- **Minimum fillet radii,** with dimensions. Do not leave them to "break sharp edges".
- **Shot peening** to a specification (for example AMS 2430 or AMS 2432), with the intensity and the coverage.
- **Bolt preload,** specified as a tension or a controlled torque. Do not leave it to the fitter.
- **Identification of fatigue-critical features,** with their inspection (penetrant or magnetic particle), and with no
  marking or stamping on them.
