## The whole idea in one picture

A solid rocket motor has no pumps, valves or moving parts. The fuel and the oxidiser are mixed into a solid,
rubber-like **grain**, which is cast directly into the **case**. After ignition, the exposed surface burns and makes hot
gas. The gas pressurises the case, and it can go out only through the **nozzle**. All the properties of the motor come
from this arrangement.

<Analogy title="A pressure cooker that is also the stove">
  The case is a pressure cooker, the grain is the fuel that burns inside it, and the nozzle throat is the valve on the
  lid. The difference from a kitchen pressure cooker is that here the "stove" burns *faster* as the pressure increases.
  The result can be a steady hiss or an explosion. One number decides which, and the remainder of this page is about
  that number.
</Analogy>

## How the propellant burns

Composite propellant burns only at its exposed surface. That surface moves back **perpendicular to itself** at the
**burn rate** $r$, which is usually 5–20 mm/s for tactical and booster propellants. The burn rate depends strongly on
the chamber pressure. Over a useful range, it follows the law of Saint-Robert (Vieille):

$$
r = a\,p_c^{\,n}
$$

- $a$ is the burn-rate coefficient, set by the propellant formulation and its initial temperature.
- $n$ is the pressure exponent, usually approximately 0.3–0.5 for AP/HTPB propellants.

<Analogy title="Toast under a grill">
  Burning propellant is similar to bread under a grill. Only the surface that faces the heat changes, and it moves in
  layer by layer. If you make the grill hotter (increase the pressure), the bread becomes brown faster, but not
  proportionally faster. The exponent $n < 1$ gives this "not proportionally".
</Analogy>

The surface moves back everywhere at the same time. Thus the **shape** of the bore decides how the burning area $A_b$
changes as the grain burns. A plain circular bore increases in area as it opens (progressive thrust). A star bore or a
slotted bore can be shaped to keep the area almost constant (neutral thrust). Grain design sets the thrust–time curve of
the motor.

## Why chamber pressure settles at one value

The burning area and the burn rate set the rate at which gas is made. The throat area and the chamber pressure set the
rate at which gas goes out through the choked throat. The pressure increases until the two rates are equal:

$$
\underbrace{\rho_p\,A_b\,a\,p_c^{\,n}}_{\text{gas made}}
\;=\;
\underbrace{\frac{p_c\,A_t}{c^*}}_{\text{gas leaving}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
p_c = \left(\rho_p\, a\, c^*\, \frac{A_b}{A_t}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-n}}
$$

Here $\rho_p$ is the propellant density, $A_t$ is the throat area, and $c^*$ is the characteristic velocity. $c^*$
measures how much hot gas the propellant makes for each unit of throat flow. The ratio $K_n = A_b/A_t$ is the main
design parameter.

<Analogy title="A bath with the tap on and the plug out">
  Water flows in from the tap and out through the plug hole. The level increases until the outflow, which increases
  with depth, is equal to the inflow. A solid motor does the same with pressure in place of water level. The difference
  is that in a motor, the tap opens more as the level increases ($p^n$). While the plug hole "wins" ($n<1$), the level
  still becomes stable. If the tap won ($n \geq 1$), the bath would overflow, for any size of plug hole.
</Analogy>

Try it. The orange curve is the gas made, and the blue line is the gas that goes out. The operating pressure is where
they cross.

## The consequence for hardware: small changes are amplified

The balance shows how a fractional change in burning area, burn rate or throat area becomes a change in pressure:

$$
\frac{\Delta p_c}{p_c} \approx \frac{1}{1-n}\,\frac{\Delta K_n}{K_n}
$$

With $n = 0.4$, a 1% change in burning area changes the pressure by approximately 1.7%. Because of this amplification,
the **maximum expected operating pressure (MEOP)**, which is the design pressure of the case, is well above the nominal
pressure. It must cover all of these items together:

- **Hot propellant.** The burn rate increases with the initial grain temperature. The pressure sensitivity
  $\pi_K = \left(\partial \ln p_c / \partial T\right)_{K_n}$ is usually approximately 0.1–0.3 %/°C. Thus the difference
  between a cold-soaked motor and a hot-soaked motor can be tens of per cent.
- **Manufacturing variation** in burn rate, grain geometry and throat diameter.
- **The ignition spike** and any **erosive burning** early in the burn, when the gas speeds along the bore are highest.

<Aside type="caution" title="Hardware designers get MEOP, they do not set it">
  MEOP is a ballistics output that already includes these effects. A frequent and expensive error is to size a case
  for the nominal pressure, and to find later that the hot-day value is 15–25% higher.
</Aside>

## From pressure to thrust

The thrust is the chamber pressure on the throat area, multiplied by the thrust coefficient $C_F$ of the nozzle:

$$
F = C_F\, p_c\, A_t
$$

$C_F$ is usually approximately 1.3–1.8. It depends on the expansion ratio of the nozzle and on the ambient pressure. It
measures how well the nozzle changes pressure into exhaust momentum. Thus the thrust follows the chamber pressure trace,
which follows the burning area, which follows the grain shape. [Nozzle design](../../motor/nozzle/) derives $C_F$, and
shows what throat erosion does to the pressure trace.

## What to take away

1. **The pressure controls itself** only because $n < 1$. Propellants are selected for a low, stable $n$.
2. **$K_n$ sets the pressure.** The grain geometry sets $A_b$ during the burn. Throat erosion slowly increases $A_t$.
3. **Changes are amplified by $1/(1-n)$.** Thus temperature, tolerances and transients must all be within MEOP.
4. **The case must withstand MEOP, proof and burst.** The page
   [case &amp; pressure vessel sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/) continues from here. For what each hardware part
   carries, how it fails and what its mass is, see [rocket motor hardware](../../motor/hardware/).
