## Why this matters

Seals, case insulation, propellant binder, adhesives and the matrix of a composite case are all polymers. Their
stiffness can change by a factor of a thousand with temperature and with the duration of the load. The
[Viscoelasticity](../viscoelasticity/) page gives the numbers: the Prony series, the WLF shift and the master curve.
This page shows the mechanism behind those numbers: chain segments that move, chains that tangle, links that tie them
together, and the bond between binder and particle that breaks.

When you can see the mechanism, you can predict the behaviour before you have the data. A plasticiser that leaves the
material makes it brittle. More crosslinks give a stiffer plateau and less strain capability. A cold seal cannot follow
a joint that opens. A filled binder makes voids before it tears.

<Analogy title="A bowl of spaghetti">
  Cooked spaghetti is a good model of a polymer. Short pieces slide past each other: tip the bowl and they flow. Long
  strands tangle: pull one and it resists, then it slides out slowly along its own path. Tie the strands together at a
  few points, and the bowl becomes one net that stretches but does not flow. Let the bowl go cold, and the strands
  stick together: the mass is stiff, and it cracks when you bend it.
</Analogy>

## What the model shows

| Part | In the model | What it does | Where you meet it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chain segment | A bead, in the colour of its chain | Carries load along the chain. How freely the segments move decides if the material is glassy or rubbery. | The backbone of every polymer: HTPB, nitrocellulose, an elastomer |
| Backbone bond | A stick between two beads | Holds the chain together. It stretches a little and does not break. | Every covalent bond along a chain |
| Entanglement | Not drawn: the chains cannot go through each other | A temporary link. A chain must snake out along its own path. This delay makes the rubbery plateau. | Long-chain thermoplastics, uncured binder |
| Crosslink | A red link between two chains | A permanent link. A network of links cannot flow at any temperature. The number of links sets the plateau modulus. | The cure of an elastomer or of an HTPB binder. Too few links and it creeps; too many and it is brittle. |
| Plasticiser | The smallest beads | Free small molecules between the chains. They give the chains room to move, which decreases T<sub>g</sub>. | Double-base propellant, plasticised PVC, seal compounds |
| Filler particle | A large grey sphere | A rigid particle in a soft binder. Many particles make a highly filled elastomer: stiffer, and with less strain capability. | Ammonium perchlorate and aluminium in a composite propellant; carbon black in rubber |
| Bond to a particle | A green line to a particle | Carries load from the binder into the particle. It is the weakest link. When it breaks (dewetting), a void opens. | The bonding agent of a composite propellant |
| Crystalline block | A purple cube | Ordered chains that stay rigid above T<sub>g</sub>, up to the melting point. | PTFE, PEEK, polyethylene, nylon |

## The curve that each structure gives

The Viscoelasticity page measures a curve. The structure of the chains decides its shape. Time and temperature do the
same thing to it (time–temperature superposition), so the time axis is also a temperature axis.

## Calculator

**Live model** runs the chains in 3D. Select a material and a temperature. Then drag to rotate the view, to push the
chains with the cursor, or to pull one bead. **Heat pulse** adds heat for 1.5 s. **Stretch 40%** stretches the
material along its length, and **Release** unloads it. The readouts are measured from the model: its temperature, how
far the chain segments and the plasticiser move, and how many bonds to the particles still hold. **Trails** draws the
recent path of some beads.

**Two temperatures** shows one chain network at a cold and a warm temperature. The shaded volume around each segment
shows how far it can move in the time that you look. The numbers come from the Prony series and the WLF shift of the
Viscoelasticity page, in real units: the modulus, the Deborah number and the state.

If motion is paused for the site, the live model starts paused. Push **Run** to start it.

## Worked example: a seal on a cold morning

Use **Two temperatures** with the seal compound of the Viscoelasticity page ($T_g = -20$ °C). Look for 0.1 s: about the
time in which a joint opens at ignition. These are the starting values of the tool.

1. **At 20 °C** the Deborah number is $2.4 \times 10^{-3}$. The segments follow easily. The modulus at 0.1 s is
   17 MPa, and the compound is rubbery.
2. **At 0 °C** every process is 555 times slower than at 20 °C. The Deborah number is 1.3, so the compound is in its
   transition. The modulus is 82 MPa, almost five times the warm value, and the seal follows the joint only slowly.
3. **At −40 °C**, 20 K below $T_g$, the compound is glassy: approximately 950 MPa. Below $T_g$ the WLF shift is only an
   indication, but the result is the same: the seal cannot follow a joint that opens. This is how the Challenger
   field-joint seals failed ([O-ring design](../../seals/o-ring-design/)).
4. **Change to the propellant** ($T_g = -70$ °C). At −40 °C and 0.1 s the Deborah number is 0.25 and the modulus is
   48 MPa. The grain is in its transition, and it is ten times stiffer than at 20 °C (4.8 MPa). For this reason the
   cold ignition is the critical load case of a case-bonded grain ([Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/)).

### In the live model: dewetting

Select **Filled binder** and push **Stretch 40%**. Each chain bead is held at the place where the average stretch
puts it. The first bonds between the binder and the particles break at approximately 15 to 25% stretch, and they
break at the poles: within 45° of the direction of the stretch. A rigid particle in a soft binder concentrates the
strain at its poles. Push **Release**. The broken bonds stay broken, and the voids stay open.

In a real composite propellant this damage increases the volume under strain, so Poisson's ratio decreases below 0.5.
A tensile test that measures the change of volume (dilatation) shows the strain at which dewetting starts.

## What to try

1. **Short linear chains** at a temperature of 1.2: select Push and move the cursor through the chains. They slide
   apart and stay where you put them.
2. **Long entangled chains**: push them in the same way. They resist, then move slowly, because each chain must snake
   along its own path to get out of the loops of its neighbours.
3. **Stiff chains with plasticiser**: turn on Trails. The small beads go two to three times as far as the chain
   segments in the same time.
4. **Thermoset**: push Heat pulse. The segments shake faster, but the network stays where it is.
5. **Semi-crystalline**: the blocks do not move. Only the chains between them move.
6. **Any material at a temperature of 0.05**: almost all motion stops. This is the glassy state of the model.

## Where this stops

- **A teaching model in reduced units.** Its temperature is not in °C and its time is not in seconds. The Two
  temperatures view uses real units, from the Prony series of the Viscoelasticity page.
- **No glass transition.** In the model, motion decreases smoothly as the temperature decreases. A real polymer has a
  narrow band of temperature, $T_g$, in which the segmental motion stops.
- **No entropic elasticity.** The bonds are springs, so a stretched network does not pull back to its shape as real
  rubber does. Real rubber elasticity comes from the entropy of the coiled chains. The model is too small to show it,
  and this page does not claim it.
- **A small model.** It has approximately 150 beads in a box. Real chains have thousands of segments, and
  entanglement needs long chains. The model shows the mechanisms, not the numbers.
- **The stretch is affine.** Each chain bead is held where the average stretch puts it, as if the rest of the material
  were outside the box. A bond to a particle breaks if it stays longer than a set amount for a set time.
- **No chemistry:** no cure, no ageing reactions and no combustion. Propellant formulation and energetic chemistry are
  outside the scope of this site.

## On the drawing and in the specifications

- **Specify the low-temperature limit of an elastomer as a test result at a rate:** TR-10 (ASTM D1329, ISO 2921), or
  $T_g$ by DMA at a stated frequency. A compound name is not a specification.
- **For a seal,** show that the compound is rubbery at the coldest service temperature, at the time scale at which the
  joint opens.
- **For a filled elastomer,** ask for stress, strain and dilatation in the tensile test, at the temperatures and rates
  of your load cases. The dilatation shows where dewetting starts.
- **Where a plasticiser can migrate** into a liner, an adhesive or a fluid, specify a barrier or a compatibility test.
  Add an ageing test that measures the change in modulus and in strain capability.
