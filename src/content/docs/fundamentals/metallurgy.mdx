## Why this matters

The same steel can be soft enough to machine, hard enough to cut glass, or tough enough to contain a motor. Only the
heat treatment is different. The same aluminium alloy can be strong and crack in a damp store, or a little weaker and
safe. The datasheet gives one condition; your part gets the condition that its processing gave it.

The designer controls this through a few lines on the drawing: the material specification and its condition, the
heat treatment, the hardness or strength to verify, and the limits on later heat. Each line makes sense once you can
see which diagram it refers to.

<Analogy title="Cooking, not chemistry">
  Bread, toast and a burnt crust are the same flour. The recipe and the oven, not the ingredients, decide which one
  you eat. A steel is the same: carbon and alloy elements are the ingredients, and the heat treatment is the recipe.
  Like cooking, the time matters as much as the temperature, and you cannot undo a burnt result by cooling it.
</Analogy>

## Metallic bonding: why metals bend

Three kinds of bond hold solids together:

- **Metallic.** Each atom gives its outer electrons to a "sea" of electrons that all the positive ion cores share. The
  bond has no direction. When a plane of atoms slides by one spacing, the electron sea holds the ions at every step,
  and the bonds re-form. This is why metals yield before they break, and why a crack in a metal blunts.
- **Ionic.** Positive and negative ions attract (alumina, zirconia). If a plane slides, ions of the same sign come
  together and repel: the crystal cleaves.
- **Covalent.** Neighbours share pairs of electrons in fixed directions (diamond, silicon carbide, silicon nitride).
  The bonds must break before the plane can slide: the crystal cracks first.

The free electrons also carry heat and current. That is why metals conduct, and why every insulation and nozzle
calculation on this site needs the conductivity of the metal behind it. Ceramics are hard and strong in compression,
but they cannot yield to blunt a crack: their strength depends on the largest flaw.

## Crystals, grains and dislocations

Metal atoms sit in a repeating crystal lattice. A part is made of many small crystals, the **grains**. Metals yield
because lines of misfit in the lattice, the **dislocations**, move through the grains. Anything that stops
dislocations from moving makes the metal stronger, and usually less ductile.

<CrystalDbtt />

| Lattice | Metals | What it means for the design |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Body-centred cubic (BCC) | Ferritic and martensitic steels, molybdenum, tungsten | Ductile-to-brittle transition: specify an impact test at the lowest service temperature |
| Face-centred cubic (FCC) | Aluminium, nickel, copper, austenitic stainless steel | No transition; tough at cryogenic temperatures |
| Hexagonal close-packed (HCP) | Titanium (α), magnesium, zinc | Fewer slip systems: properties change with direction (texture) in plate and forgings |

**The most important result:** a BCC steel that is tough at room temperature can break in a brittle way when cold.
FCC metals do not do this, which is why cryogenic hardware uses aluminium, austenitic stainless steel and nickel
alloys. Nickel at 9% moves the transition of a ferritic steel far enough down to use it for liquefied natural gas.

## The six ways to make a metal stronger

| Mechanism | How it works | Examples | Survives heat? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solid solution | Dissolved atoms of a different size strain the lattice | Mg in 5xxx aluminium, Mo and Cr in nickel alloys | Yes: it cannot over-age |
| Work hardening | Plastic strain multiplies dislocations until they block each other | H tempers of aluminium, cold-drawn bar, flow-formed cases | No: lost by annealing or in a weld HAZ |
| Grain refinement | Grain boundaries block dislocations | Normalised steels, fine-grained castings | Lost if the grains grow |
| Precipitation hardening | Fine particles formed by ageing block dislocations | 2xxx, 6xxx and 7xxx aluminium, 17-4PH, maraging steel, Inconel 718 | No: over-ages at temperature |
| Transformation (martensite) | Carbon trapped in a distorted lattice by a fast quench | Quenched-and-tempered steels: 4130, 4340, D6AC | Tempers further above its temper temperature |
| Dispersion | Stable oxide particles that do not dissolve | Oxide-dispersion-strengthened alloys | Yes, to very high temperature |

Grain refinement is the only mechanism that increases strength **and** toughness together. The yield strength follows
the Hall–Petch relation, $\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k/\sqrt{d}$, with $d$ the grain size. All the others trade
toughness or ductility for strength.

## Iron and carbon

The iron–carbon diagram is the map underneath every steel. Carbon content sets which phases can exist; temperature
sets which of them do. The important points:

- **Austenite (γ, FCC)** dissolves up to 2.1% carbon. All hardening of steel starts by heating into austenite.
- **Ferrite (α, BCC)** dissolves almost no carbon (0.022%). On slow cooling, the carbon leaves as **cementite**
  (Fe₃C), in layers with ferrite called **pearlite**.
- **The eutectoid** is at 0.77% carbon and 727 °C (A1). Structural and aerospace steels are below it, at 0.25 to 0.5%
  carbon: enough to make useful martensite, not so much that the steel cracks on quenching or welding.
- **Ac1 and Ac3** are the temperatures where austenite starts and finishes forming on heating. Alloying moves them.
  Steels are austenitised a little above Ac3, so that all the steel is austenite, but not so high that the grains grow.

The **lever rule** gives the phase fractions. A slowly cooled steel with carbon content $C$ below the eutectoid has a
pearlite fraction of $(C - 0.022)/(0.77 - 0.022)$.

## Quenching, hardenability and martensite

What a steel becomes depends on how fast it cools from austenite. A transformation diagram shows the time that
austenite needs to change at each temperature.

<TttPaths />

- **Martensite** forms when the quench misses the nose of the curve. The carbon is trapped, and the lattice distorts
  to body-centred tetragonal. It is very hard and very brittle. It starts to form at the **Ms** temperature.
- **Hardness comes from carbon.** Fully martensitic steel has approximately $35 + 50\,C$ HRC (for 0.1 to 0.6% C).
  Alloying does not increase it.
- **Hardenability comes from alloying.** Chromium, molybdenum, nickel and manganese move the curves to the right, so
  a thick section or a slower (oil or gas) quench still makes martensite at its centre. The Jominy test (ASTM A255)
  measures it. This is why 4340 hardens through a thick forging and a plain carbon steel of the same carbon does not.
- **Quenching is violent.** The surface turns to martensite first and the core later, with a volume increase each
  time. Sharp corners, sudden changes of section and holes near edges cause quench cracks and distortion.

## Tempering

As-quenched martensite is too brittle to use. **Tempering** reheats it below Ac1, so that fine carbides form and the
lattice relaxes. Strength and hardness decrease; toughness increases. Time and temperature trade against each other
through the Hollomon–Jaffe parameter:

$$
P = T\,(C + \log_{10} t)
$$

Here $T$ is the absolute temperature in K, $t$ the time in hours, and $C \approx 20$ for low-alloy steels. Two
tempers with the same $P$ give approximately the same hardness. Three results for the design:

- **Later heat tempers the steel further.** A stress relief, a coating or insulation cure, or a hydrogen bake adds
  to the temper. If its parameter is near the temper's, the strength decreases. Keep later heat a margin below the
  temper temperature (often 15 to 30 °C).
- **Some temperature ranges are brittle.** Tempering at approximately 250 to 400 °C can cause tempered-martensite
  embrittlement, and slow cooling through approximately 375 to 575 °C can cause temper embrittlement in some steels.
  The material specification sets the temper to avoid them.
- **Double tempering** changes retained austenite to martensite in the first temper and tempers that martensite in
  the second.

## Precipitation hardening

Precipitation-hardened alloys are heated to dissolve the alloying elements (solution treatment), quenched to keep
them in solution, then aged so that fine precipitates form.

<AgeingCurve />

- **The temper code is the position on the curve.** T4 is naturally aged at room temperature. T6 is artificially aged
  to the peak. T73 and T76 are over-aged for resistance to stress-corrosion cracking and exfoliation. The second and
  third digits (T7351) add the stress relief by stretching after the quench.
- **Service heat continues the ageing.** A peak-aged alloy that is held hot moves down the right-hand side of its
  curve. Elevated-temperature data after exposure come from this.
- **Steels and nickel alloys too.** 17-4PH is aged to H900 (strongest), H1025 or H1150 (tougher, more resistant to
  stress-corrosion cracking). Maraging steel is soft after solution treatment, so it is formed and welded, then aged.
  Inconel 718 ages slowly enough to weld.

## Diffusion

Atoms move through the lattice by diffusion, faster at high temperature: $D = D_0\,e^{-Q/RT}$. With a constant
concentration at the surface, the depth that the change reaches grows with $\sqrt{Dt}$:

$$
\frac{c(x) - c_0}{c_s - c_0} = \operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right)
$$

Twice the depth needs four times the time. Three processes on the drawing follow from this:

- **Case hardening.** Carburising adds carbon to the surface of a low-carbon steel in austenite, then quenches it: a
  hard case on a tough core, for gears and bearing surfaces. Nitriding adds nitrogen at a lower temperature, with
  little distortion.
- **Decarburisation.** Hot steel in air loses carbon from its surface. The surface becomes soft, with low fatigue
  strength. Heat-treat in vacuum or a protective atmosphere, leave material to machine off, and state the allowed
  depth on the drawing.
- **Hydrogen embrittlement.** Hydrogen enters high-strength steel from plating, pickling, welding or corrosion, and
  causes delayed brittle fracture under stress. Steels harder than approximately 39 HRC get a bake soon after
  plating (ISO 9588, ASTM B850), so the hydrogen diffuses out before it does damage
  ([Coatings](../../materials/coatings/#hydrogen-embrittlement)).

### Carbon and low-alloy steels

| Class | Typical grades (UK / US) | Condition | Aerospace and motor use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mild and structural | EN3B, S275, S355 | As rolled or bright drawn | Ground equipment, handling frames, test stands. Never flight hardware |
| Medium carbon | EN8 (080M40) | Normalised, or induction hardened | Shafts, studs, axles for ground equipment |
| Low-alloy, quenched and tempered | EN16T (605M36), EN19T (709M40, 4140), EN24T (817M40, 4340), 4130 and T45 tube | Hardened and tempered | Fittings, shafts, bolts, tube structures |
| Motor-case steels | D6AC, 15CDV6 | Hardened and tempered, 1400–1600 MPa | Solid motor cases |
| Ultra-high strength | 300M, maraging 250 and 300, AerMet 100 | Quenched and tempered, or aged | Landing gear, highly loaded fittings, motor cases |

- **Carbon sets the hardness; alloying sets the depth.** The as-quenched hardness depends on the carbon. Cr, Mo and
  Ni let a thick section harden through. British Standard bar is specified with a **limiting ruling section**: the
  largest section that reaches the properties of its condition.
- **Weldability falls as carbon equivalent rises.** $CE = C + Mn/6 + (Cr + Mo + V)/5 + (Ni + Cu)/15$. Above about 0.45,
  weld only with preheat, a qualified procedure and a post-weld heat treatment.
- **Hydrogen embrittlement** above about 1000 MPa: bake after plating
  ([Drawing frame & notes](../../drawing/drawing-frame/) writes the note).
- **These steels are BCC.** They have a ductile-to-brittle transition: test the impact energy at the lowest service
  temperature.

### Stainless steels

Chromium of at least 10.5% forms a thin oxide film that heals itself in air. The other elements decide the structure:

| Class | Structure | Magnetic | Hardened by | Aerospace grades | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Austenitic | FCC | No (slightly, after cold work) | Cold work only | 304L, 316L, 321, 347, 21-6-9, Nitronic 60 | Tubing, ducting, cryogenic parts, bellows |
| Martensitic | BCC/BCT | Yes | Quench and temper | 410, 431 (S80), 440C | Shafts, pins, valve seats, bearings |
| Precipitation-hardening | Martensite with precipitates | Yes | Ageing (H900 to H1150) | 17-4PH, 15-5PH, 13-8Mo, Custom 465, FV520B | Fittings, actuators, shafts, fasteners |
| Duplex | Half FCC, half BCC | Yes | Solid solution | 2205, 2507 (super duplex) | Seawater and chloride service |

- **The H number of a PH stainless is its ageing temperature in °F.** H900 (482 °C) is the strongest and least tough;
  H1025 (552 °C) and H1150 (621 °C) trade strength for toughness and stress-corrosion resistance. 15-5PH and 13-8Mo are
  cleaner, tougher versions of 17-4PH for fracture-critical parts.
- **Sensitisation.** Between about 450 and 850 °C, chromium carbides form at the grain boundaries of an austenitic
  steel, and the chromium-poor zone beside them corrodes. Use the low-carbon (L) grades or the stabilised grades (321,
  347) for welded parts.
- **Pitting resistance** grows with $PREN = Cr + 3.3\,Mo + 16\,N$ (mass %): about 18 for 304, 25 for 316, 35 for
  2205 and above 40 for 2507.
- **Passivate after machining** (ASTM A967 or AMS 2700) to remove free iron from the surface.
- **Austenitic threads gall.** Use a lubricant, or a different material for the nut or insert
  ([Threads](../../joints/threads/#plating-galling-and-lubrication)).

### Duplex and super duplex

A duplex steel is about half austenite and half ferrite. The ferrite gives about twice the yield strength of 316L and
much better resistance to chloride stress-corrosion cracking; the austenite gives toughness and weldability.

- **Keep it below about 250–300 °C.** The ferrite embrittles slowly between about 300 and 500 °C, fastest near 475 °C. Duplex is
  not a hot-structure material.
- **Weld with a qualified procedure.** The heat input and the filler must keep the phase balance near 50/50 and avoid
  sigma phase, which makes the weld brittle and less resistant to corrosion.
- **Where it fits on a motor programme:** seawater and marine test equipment, coolant and process pipework, and
  chloride environments where 316L cracks.

### Aluminium alloys

| Series | Main element | Hardened by | Typical aerospace grades | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1xxx | Pure | Work | 1050A | Conductivity, cladding |
| 2xxx | Copper | Ageing | 2024-T3/T351, 2014A, 2219-T87, 2618A | Fatigue (2024), weldable tanks (2219), hot parts (2618A, RR58). Poor corrosion resistance: clad or protect |
| 5xxx | Magnesium | Work | 5083, 5251 | Weldable sheet and plate, marine |
| 6xxx | Mg and Si | Ageing | 6061, 6082 | General brackets and housings; weldable |
| 7xxx | Zinc | Ageing | 7075, 7050, 7010, 7020 | The strongest: frames, fittings, forgings |
| Al-Li | Lithium | Ageing | 2195, 2050, 2099 | Lighter and stiffer launch-vehicle tanks |

**Tempers.** O is annealed, H is work hardened, T is heat treated. T3 and T4 are naturally aged; T6 is peak aged;
T73, T74 and T76 are over-aged for stress-corrosion resistance. A "51" after the temper (T351, T651, T7351) means that
the plate was stretched to relieve the quench stresses, so it moves less when you machine it.

- **Stress corrosion in 7xxx T6** is worst in the short-transverse direction of thick plate and forgings. Use T73 or
  T7451 where the part has sustained stress in that direction.
- **Welds lose the temper.** The weld zone of 6061-T6 has a third to a half less strength; most 2xxx and 7xxx alloys are
  not fusion welded at all (friction stir welding is the exception).
- **Galvanic corrosion** against steel, stainless steel, titanium and carbon fibre: isolate and seal the joint
  ([Galvanic](../../materials/galvanic/)).

### Titanium alloys

Titanium is HCP (α) below about 880 °C and BCC (β) above. Aluminium, oxygen and tin keep the α; vanadium,
molybdenum and iron keep the β.

| Class | Grades | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commercially pure | Grade 2 | Corrosion-resistant ducting and tanks, formed sheet |
| α and near-α | Ti-5Al-2.5Sn ELI, Ti-6-2-4-2, IMI 834 | Cryogenic tanks (5-2.5 ELI); compressor parts to 600 °C (IMI 834) |
| α-β | Ti-6Al-4V (Grade 5), Ti-6Al-4V ELI (Grade 23), IMI 550, Ti-3Al-2.5V | Half of all titanium: fittings, frames, pressure vessels, fasteners, AM parts |
| β and near-β | Ti-10V-2Fe-3Al | Large forgings that must harden through: rotor hubs, landing gear |

- **ELI (extra low interstitial)** grades have less oxygen: lower strength, much higher toughness. Use them for
  fracture-critical and cryogenic parts.
- **Alpha case.** Hot work in air makes a brittle, oxygen-rich surface layer. Remove it by machining or chemical
  milling, and check it on the drawing.
- **Do not put cadmium- or silver-plated parts on titanium.** Both embrittle titanium. Avoid chlorinated cleaning
  fluids on hot titanium.
- **Titanium galls** against itself and many metals. Lubricate threads and sliding faces.

### Nickel superalloys (Inconel, Nimonic, Waspaloy)

Nickel is FCC at all temperatures, with chromium for oxidation resistance. The two ways to make it strong when hot:

- **Solid solution:** Inconel 625 and 600, Hastelloy X, Nimonic 75. Formed and welded sheet for combustors, ducting,
  bellows and heat shields. No over-ageing.
- **Precipitation of γ′ (Ni₃(Al,Ti)) or γ″ (Ni₃Nb):** Inconel 718, Waspaloy, Nimonic 80A, 90 and C263, Haynes 282.
  Inconel 718 is the workhorse to about 650–700 °C: its γ″ forms slowly, so it welds without strain-age cracking.
  It is solution treated, then aged in two steps (about 720 °C, then 620 °C). The γ′ alloys go hotter but crack on
  welding.

The Nimonic alloys are the British line: Nimonic 75 sheet, 80A and 90 blades, bolts and springs, and C263 combustors
and casings. Monel 400 and K-500 (nickel-copper) resist seawater and burn less readily in oxygen. Invar 36 hardly
expands near room temperature.

- **Superalloys work harden as you machine them.** Use sharp tools, positive cuts and constant feed; budget for slow
  machining ([Machining cost](../../make/cost/)).
- **Inconel 718 is embrittled by high-pressure hydrogen.** Check the environment before you use it in hydrogen
  service.

### Copper beryllium and bronzes

| Alloy | UK / US designation | Condition | Typical strength | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beryllium copper (alloy 25) | CB101, C17200 | Solution treated and aged (TF00, TH04) | 1100–1400 MPa | Springs, bushes, connectors, non-sparking tools |
| Beryllium copper (alloy 3) | C17510 | Aged | 650–800 MPa, high conductivity | Welding electrodes, current-carrying springs |
| Aluminium bronze | CA104, C63000 (AMS 4640) | As supplied | About 700 MPa | Landing-gear and flap-track bushes, seawater fittings |
| Phosphor bronze | PB102, PB103 (CW451K, CW452K) | Work hardened | 400–600 MPa | Springs, contacts, bushes, bellows |
| Leaded gunmetal and bearing bronze | LG2, LG4 (CC491K, CC492K), CC493K | Cast | 250 MPa | Plain bearings, valve bodies |
| Free-machining brass | CZ121, CW614N | Drawn | 400 MPa | Turned fittings for ground equipment |

- **Beryllium copper is aged at about 315 °C** after a solution treatment near 790 °C. The temper letters give the
  condition: TB00 solution treated, TD04 work hardened, TF00 aged, TH04 work hardened and aged.
- **Beryllium dust and fume are toxic.** Machining, grinding and welding beryllium copper need extraction and controls
  under your health and safety procedures. Finished solid parts are safe to handle.
- **Bronze bushes run against steel** without galling. Aluminium bronze, beryllium copper and copper-nickel-tin are the
  usual choices for highly loaded aerospace bushes.

## Worked example: the heat treatment of the D6AC case

The 300 mm motor's case is D6AC ([Case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/)). The heat of steel has 0.46 C, 0.75 Mn,
0.22 Si, 0.55 Ni, 1.05 Cr, 1.0 Mo and 0.08 V (typical values inside AMS 6431). The temper of 565 °C for 2 + 2 h is
illustrative. The criteria for this example only: later heat stays 25 °C below the temper, and the allowed
decarburisation is 0.08 mm. Push **Load worked example** in the calculator to load these inputs.

1. **On the diagram.** Slowly cooled, the steel is 41% ferrite and 59% pearlite: soft enough to form and machine.
   Ac1 is **730 °C** and Ac3 **814 °C**, so it is austenitised at approximately 845 to 875 °C. Ms is **292 °C**. Fully
   martensitic, it has approximately **58 HRC**: this is the hardness of the as-quenched case, before tempering.
2. **The temper.** 565 °C for 4 h in total gives $P = 838 \times (20 + \log_{10} 4) = 17\,268$. This is the condition
   that the allowables of the case belong to ([Allowables](../../materials/allowables/)).
3. **The insulation cure.** The case-bonded insulation cures at 160 °C for 6 h. Its parameter is 9000: the same as a
   few microseconds at 565 °C. It has **no effect** on the temper.
4. **A stress relief after a weld repair.** At 540 °C for 2 h, the parameter is 16 508, the same as **30 minutes** more
   at 565 °C. The total parameter increases by 43, and the exposure is below the limit (the temper made 25 °C colder,
   $P = 16\,753$). The hottest 2 h stress relief inside the margin is **552 °C**.
5. **The wrong stress relief.** At 600 °C for 2 h, the parameter is 17 726: the same as **14 hours** more at 565 °C.
   The steel tempers further and its strength falls below the allowables. The case must be hardened and tempered
   again, then tested. A note on the drawing prevents this: the maximum temperature of later heat.
6. **Decarburisation.** One hour in air at 900 °C (normalising, or austenitising without protection) decarburises
   **0.34 mm** of each surface, to the depth where the carbon is back to 90% of the core value. The wall is 1.377 mm:
   a quarter of it on each side would be soft, with a low fatigue strength, and the wall is not machined after
   hardening. The 0.08 mm allowed is reached in **200 seconds**. The case is heat-treated in vacuum or a protective
   atmosphere, and a hardness traverse on a test piece shows the depth.

Every number here changes the drawing, not the analysis: the condition and its test, the maximum temperature of
later processes, and the decarburisation limit.

## Where this stops

- **The binary iron–carbon diagram.** Alloying elements move the lines and the eutectoid. Use the diagrams and
  transformation data of the alloy.
- **Andrews' formulas** are empirical, for low-alloy steels with 0.1 to 0.6% carbon, and give approximately ±20 °C.
  The heating rate also moves Ac1 and Ac3.
- **One Hollomon–Jaffe constant.** $C$ depends on the carbon content, and the parameter does not apply to secondary
  hardening steels (high Mo, V or W) or to precipitation-hardened alloys. Use the tempering curves of the alloy.
- **Diffusion into a semi-infinite solid** with a constant surface concentration and one diffusion coefficient. In
  a thin wall, both surfaces change and the profiles meet. Carbon diffusion in austenite increases with carbon
  content. Hydrogen in high-strength steel is slowed by traps.
- **No grain size, inclusions or texture.** These come from the melting and working of the material, controlled by
  the material specification ([Material specs](../../materials/specification/)).

## On the drawing

- **The material specification and its condition** at delivery and after heat treatment, for example AMS 6431, or
  7075-T7351 to AMS-QQ-A-250/12.
- **The heat treatment specification,** for example AMS 2759/2 for low-alloy steel above 1517 MPa (220 ksi), or
  AMS 2770 for aluminium, with pyrometry to AMS 2750.
- **What to verify:** hardness or tensile strength, on the part or on test pieces that go through the furnace with
  it ([Certs & trace](../../make/certs/)).
- **The decarburisation limit** and how it is checked.
- **The maximum temperature of later processes:** stress relief, cures, coatings, bakes.
- **The hydrogen embrittlement bake** after plating or pickling, with the time limit to start it.
