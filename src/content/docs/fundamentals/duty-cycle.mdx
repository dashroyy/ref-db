## Why this matters

Many structural failures found in review start with a load case that nobody wrote down: the re-proof after a repair,
the fifth reassembly of a closure, the cold night in an unheated store, the drop from a forklift. The analysis was
correct for the loads it had. It did not have these.

The duty cycle is the list of every event that loads the hardware, with its level and its count, from manufacture to
the end of its life. Agree it before detail design starts. Every margin on the drawing depends on it.

<Analogy title="A car's mileage">
  The odometer of a car counts kilometres, but the engine wears most in cold starts and the suspension in potholes. A
  car with few kilometres and many short trips on bad roads can be more worn than one with many motorway kilometres.
  Count the events that do the damage, not only the use.
</Analogy>

## What the duty-cycle sheet must have

| Input | Why it governs | Common miss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MEOP (maximum expected operating pressure) | Wall and joint sizing | Nominal pressure given, not the hot worst case |
| Pressure rise rate and ignition peak | Dynamic load on joints and seals | Treated as static |
| Temperature: soak and transient | Allowables, seal state, differential growth | Only the steady state given |
| Time at temperature | Short- or long-time allowables, creep, ageing | Never asked for |
| Vibration and shock spectra | Preload retention, fatigue, fretting | Left to qualification |
| Handling and transport | Often larger than the operating loads | Ignored |
| Assembly cycles | Thread wear, inserts, seal reuse | Assumed to be one |
| Storage life and conditions | Elastomer ageing, corrosion, compression set | Treated as packaging |

**If an input is unknown, write the assumption on the sheet with a name against it.** An assumption with an owner
gets challenged. A blank gets filled in, without a record, by whoever does the drawing.

## Count the cycles honestly

"Single use" describes the mission, not the life of the hardware.

<LifeTimeline />

| Event | Typical count | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proof test | 1 to 2 | The highest stress the part will see. A repair means a re-proof |
| Leak checks | 2 to 5 | Each disassembly adds one |
| Acceptance and functional tests | 1 to 3 | Programme-dependent |
| Environment tests | Per the test plan | Vibration, shock, thermal cycling on the qualification unit, sometimes on flight units |
| Temperature cycles in storage | Thousands | Daily and seasonal swings on seals, preload and bond lines |
| Handling and transport | Many | Lifts, cradles, road and air transport |

**Limit, proof and ultimate.** The limit load is the highest load expected in service, with no factor. The ultimate
load is the limit load times the factor from the programme specification: the part must not rupture. The proof load
is the acceptance test level on delivered hardware, from the contract or the code. The factors come from the customer
specification or the governing code, never from a handbook default ([Margin policy](../../verify/margin-policy/)).

## Equivalent cycles

Events at different levels do different damage. With Miner's rule and an S–N curve of slope $m$
($N \propto S^{-m}$), the events convert to an equivalent number of cycles at a reference level, such as MEOP:

$$
N_\text{eq} = \sum_i n_i \left(\frac{S_i}{S_\text{ref}}\right)^{m}
$$

Here $n_i$ is the count of event $i$ and $S_i$ its stress range (or pressure range, where stress is proportional to
pressure). The slope is approximately 3 for welded joints and 5 to 10 for smooth parent metal. A steep slope makes the
largest events count most: the proof test is often more than half of the damage of a single-use case, and
low-pressure leak checks are almost nothing. The scatter factor of the programme then multiplies $N_\text{eq}$ into
the life that analysis or test must show ([Fatigue](../fatigue/#variable-loads-miners-rule)).

## Rainflow counting

A measured or predicted history (a pressure trace, a strain gauge record, a temperature log) is not a list of
cycles. Rainflow counting (ASTM E1049) turns it into one.

<HysteresisLoops />

The three-point rule: take the peaks and valleys in order. When the latest range is at least as large as the range
before it, that earlier range is a cycle; remove it and continue. Ranges left at the end are half cycles. The largest
cycle always runs from the lowest to the highest value in the history, even when they are far apart in time.

The result is a list of ranges with their counts, which go into the equivalent-cycle sum or into Miner's rule with
the S–N curve. Mean-stress correction, where it matters, is applied to each cycle ([Fatigue](../fatigue/#mean-stress)).

## Temperature

Temperature changes are load cycles too:

- **Daily and seasonal swings** in storage and transport: thousands of cycles on seals, bolt preload, bond lines and
  the propellant grain ([Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/)).
- **Differential expansion** between parts of different materials changes preload, fits and seal squeeze at each
  extreme ([Differential expansion](../../joints/differential-expansion/)).
- **Time at temperature** ages elastomers, adhesives and propellant ([Storage & ageing](../../verify/storage-ageing/)).
  Record the temperature history, not only the limits.

Climatic design data (MIL-HDBK-310) and test methods (MIL-STD-810, Methods 501, 502 and 503) give typical extremes
and cycles where the programme has none.

## Handling, transport and shock

Handling and transport loads are often larger than the operating loads, and they act on the motor in directions that
the flight does not load. A drop from height $h$ onto a surface gives an impact speed $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. If the surface
or the cushion stops the part in a half-sine pulse of duration $\tau$, with rebound coefficient $e$:

$$
A_\text{peak} = \frac{\pi (1 + e)\, v}{2\tau}
$$

The peak depends on the pulse duration as much as on the height. A hard floor gives a few milliseconds and very high
accelerations; a cushion or a sprung cradle gives tens of milliseconds. Specify the handling limit (in g) that the
motor, its grain and its igniter accept, and design the cradles and containers to keep inside it. MIL-STD-810 Method
516 gives shock tests, and Method 514 vibration for transport ([Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/)).

## Worked example: the life of the 300 mm motor's case

The 300 mm motor has a welded D6AC case with a MEOP of 10 MPa ([Case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/)). The event
counts, the scatter factor of 4, the drop height and the handling limit of 30 g are **for this example only**. Push
**Load worked example** in the calculator to load them.

1. **The pressure events.** One proof test at 11 MPa (1.1 × MEOP), four leak checks at 0.7 MPa
   ([Leak testing](../../make/leak-testing/)), and one flight at MEOP. That is six pressure cycles.
2. **Equivalent cycles.** With $m = 3$ for the welds, the life is **2.33 MEOP cycles**. The proof test is **57%** of
   it; the four leak checks together are 0.0014. With $m = 8$ for smooth parent metal, it is 3.14: the steeper slope
   makes the proof count more.
3. **The life to show.** With a scatter factor of 4, the analysis must show **9.3 MEOP cycles**. The
   [NDT](../../make/ndt/#worked-example-can-ndt-clear-the-d6ac-case) page found 70 cycles of growth from the largest
   flaw that ultrasonic testing can miss: 7.5 times this requirement, at room-temperature toughness. The duty cycle and the inspection are two halves
   of one argument.
4. **Rainflow of the pressure history.** The real flight has an ignition peak of 10.4 MPa and small oscillations. The
   history $0, 11, 0, 0.7, 0, 0.7, 0, 0.7, 0, 0.7, 0, 10.4, 9.2, 10, 9.4, 10, 0$ gives **8 cycles**: 11, 10.4, four of
   0.7, 0.8 and 0.6 MPa. The equivalent life is **2.46** MEOP cycles. The ignition peak adds 0.125; the two
   oscillations add 0.0007. The largest cycles control the damage.
5. **Temperature.** Ten years in storage is approximately **3650 daily cycles**. They do not load the case wall, but
   they load the bond between the insulation and the propellant, the seals and the closure bolt preload. Each needs its
   own count on the sheet.
6. **A handling drop.** The loaded motor (137.8 kg) drops 0.3 m into a steel cradle: a 10 ms pulse with a rebound of
   0.5. The peak is **58 g**, an equivalent static load of 78.8 kN on the cradle supports. The
   [hand calc](../hand-calcs/#worked-example-hand-checks-for-the-300-mm-motors-test-campaign) used 3 g for transport:
   the drop is twenty times larger. To stay inside 30 g, the pulse must be at least **19.4 ms**: a cushion with
   approximately **15 mm** of stroke.

Six pressure cycles, thousands of temperature cycles and one bad drop: each is on the sheet, with a number and a name
against it.

## Where this stops

- **Miner's rule** sums damage linearly. The order of the loads matters in reality: a large cycle first can make
  later small cycles more damaging, or an overload can slow crack growth.
- **One S–N slope** and no endurance limit. Real curves bend, and the slope changes with the detail.
- **Rainflow without mean stress.** Apply a mean-stress correction to each cycle where it matters.
- **A rigid body on a half-sine pulse** for the drop. A part on a flexible mount can amplify the shock; use a shock
  response spectrum for components.
- **Fatigue is not fracture.** For few cycles at high stress, the life comes from crack growth from the largest flaw
  that inspection can miss ([Leak before break](../../verify/leak-before-break/)).

## On the drawing

- **A reference to the duty-cycle or environment specification,** with its revision, for each part that it loads.
- **The limits that production and handling must respect:** the number of proof tests and re-proofs allowed, the
  number of reassemblies, the handling limit in g, the storage temperature range.
- **Life-limited items:** seals, inserts and fasteners that are replaced after a stated number of assemblies.
- **The tests that add cycles,** written in the test plan with their levels, so that the analysis can count them.
