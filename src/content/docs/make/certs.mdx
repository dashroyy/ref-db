## Why this matters

Many properties of a part cannot be measured on the finished part: the chemistry, the melt practice, the tensile
strength, the toughness, the heat treatment that it had. The design analysis assumes them. The only evidence that the
part has them is a set of documents that link the part, step by step, to the tests that showed them.

If one link is missing, the evidence is lost. A correct certificate for the wrong heat, an offcut without its heat
number, or a heat treatment without its furnace record makes good material into material of unknown quality. On a
pressure part, unknown quality means scrap or a concession, however good the metal actually is.

<Analogy title="Chain of custody">
  In court, a piece of evidence counts only if its chain of custody is complete: who had it, when, and what they did
  with it, from the scene to the courtroom. If there is one gap, the evidence cannot be used, even if it is genuine.
  Material traceability is a chain of custody for metal, from the melt to the motor.
</Analogy>

## Inspection documents

BS EN 10204 gives four types of inspection document for metallic products:

| Type | Name | Content | Validated by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2.1 | Declaration of compliance | A statement that the product complies, without test results | Manufacturer |
| 2.2 | Test report | Results of non-specific tests: typical of the production, not of this batch | Manufacturer |
| 3.1 | Inspection certificate 3.1 | Results of specific tests on the delivered batch | Inspection representative of the manufacturer, independent of production |
| 3.2 | Inspection certificate 3.2 | As 3.1, and the results are also verified | The manufacturer's representative and the purchaser's representative or a third party |

Structural and pressure parts usually need 3.1 at least. Types 2.1 and 2.2 have no results for your material: they
are not evidence of its properties. The purchase order gives the type that is required. Other documents complete the
chain:

- **Certificate of conformity (CoC):** a statement that the product meets the order. It is necessary, but it does not
  replace test results.
- **Special-process certificates:** heat treatment, plating, peening, welding and NDT. Each gives the specification,
  the parameters, the identity of the parts and the result. Aerospace special processes are usually done by suppliers
  with Nadcap accreditation.
- **Test reports from laboratories,** preferably accredited to ISO/IEC 17025 for the test.
- **First article inspection (FAI)** to AS9102: a full record that the first production part meets every requirement of
  the drawing, with the material and process certificates.

## What to check on a certificate

<CertAnatomy />

1. **The type** is the type that the order requires.
2. **The specification and its issue** are those on the drawing and the order. A different issue needs a check or a
   concession.
3. **The condition and the product form** are those ordered: temper or heat treatment, bar, plate or forging, size.
4. **The heat or lot number** agrees with the marking on the material that you received.
5. **The chemistry** is in the limits of the specification, including the residual elements that it controls.
6. **The test results** are from this batch, in the test direction and location that represent your load path, and
   meet every minimum. Properties across the grain (short transverse) are usually the lowest.
7. **The signature** of the person who validates the results, as the type requires.

Also check that the numbers are consistent: yield less than UTS, a hardness that agrees with the strength, and results
that are not all exactly at the minimum. Look for alterations, missing pages and photocopies of photocopies. A
distributor certificate is complete only with a copy of the producer's certificate.

### The material certificate is not the part certificate

The producer certifies the material in its delivered condition. D6AC bar to AMS 6431, for example, is usually delivered
annealed: its certificate gives the chemistry, the melt practice and the annealed properties. The strength that the
case analysis uses comes from hardening and tempering, done later. That evidence is the **heat-treatment
certificate**: the specification (AMS 2759/2 for this steel), the furnace load, the temperatures and times with the
furnace chart, pyrometry to AMS 2750, and the tests on coupons that went through the furnace with the parts. A hardness
test of each part after heat treatment shows that each part, not only the coupon, had the treatment.

## Traceability

<TraceChain />

- **Lot traceability:** each part is linked to its heat, its heat-treatment load and its process lots. This is the
  minimum for structural parts.
- **Serial traceability:** each part has its own serial number and record. Use it for fracture-critical and pressure
  parts, so that a problem found in one part leads to the exact set of parts that shares its history.
- **Trace back and trace forward.** Back from a part to the melt, for a failure investigation. Forward from a bad heat,
  a bad furnace load or a bad batch of fasteners to every motor that contains it, for a recall. Test both directions
  on your records before you need them.
- **Keep the identity on the material.** Transfer the heat number to each piece before stock is cut, and keep material
  of different heats apart. Material without its identity goes to quarantine.
- **Keep the records** for the life of the product and the period that the contract gives. A motor in storage for
  fifteen years needs records that last longer.

### Marking the part

| Method | Where it is usually acceptable |
| --- | --- |
| Ink, label or tag (bag and tag) | Small parts, parts that cannot be marked, temporary identity during manufacture |
| Electrochemical etch | Most metals; shallow, no stress raiser; check the effect on corrosion |
| Laser marking | Most metals; check the depth and the heat effect on highly stressed or coated surfaces |
| Dot peen or vibro-peen | Non-critical surfaces; makes small indentations |
| Low-stress impression stamp | Thick sections away from stress; never on fracture-critical or thin parts |

Mark after final machining, at a location shown on the drawing, away from high stress, seal faces and fatigue-critical
surfaces, and where the mark stays readable after the finish. SAE AS478 describes the methods; MIL-STD-130 gives
unique identification for US defence items.

## Suspect and counterfeit material

Suspect material is material whose identity or properties are in doubt. Counterfeit material is misrepresented on
purpose. Fasteners, electronic parts and high-value alloys are the usual cases. The signs:

- The certificate does not agree with the marking on the material or the head marking on a fastener.
- The numbers are not consistent: hardness against strength, chemistry against the alloy, identical results for
  different heats, or every result at a round number.
- Altered, illegible or incomplete documents; no producer certificate behind a distributor certificate.
- An unknown source, a price much lower than the market, or a delivery much faster than the usual lead time.

Buy from the producer or from approved distributors. When material is suspect, quarantine it, report it under your
quality procedure, and do not return it to the supplier before it is investigated: it can come back into the supply
chain. AS9100 requires the prevention of counterfeit parts, and AS6174 gives the method for materiel.

## Receiving inspection and sampling

Receiving inspection checks the documents for each delivery, and tests a sample where the risk justifies it: a
hardness check, a chemistry check by a portable analyser, or a test at an independent laboratory. A sample that finds
no defect shows only that the lot is unlikely to have more than a given defect rate:

$$
P_\text{accept} = \sum_{k=0}^{c} \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}
\qquad
n_{c=0} = \left\lceil \frac{\ln(1 - C)}{\ln(1 - p)} \right\rceil
$$

Here $n$ is the sample size, $c$ the largest number of defectives that is accepted, $p$ the defect rate in the lot and
$C$ the confidence. Use zero-acceptance plans ($c = 0$) for critical characteristics: one defective rejects the lot or
starts 100% inspection. ISO 2859-1 and the Squeglia plans give standard sample sizes.

## Worked example: a D6AC closure forging

The supplier forged and heat-treated a lot of aft closures from D6AC bar, and sends a 3.1 certificate for the hardened
and double-tempered condition, with a copy of the producer's certificate. The drawing gives minimums of 1520 MPa UTS,
1450 MPa yield and 8% elongation for this example. The required type (3.1) and the confidence (95%) are for this
example only. Push **Load worked example** in the calculator to load these inputs.

1. **The documents.** The type, the specification (AMS 6431, written with and without a space), the condition and the
   heat number H41872 all agree. The results, 1586 MPa UTS, 1475 MPa yield and 9% elongation, meet the minimums. The
   chain to the producer is complete.
2. **Hardness against strength.** The certificate also gives 43 HRC. That is approximately 424 HV, or **1401 MPa**:
   12% below the UTS on the certificate, outside the ±10% of the conversion. One of the two values is wrong, or they
   are from different coupons or different furnace loads.
3. **Test direction.** The tests are in the longitudinal direction. The closure boss carries its load across the grain
   (short transverse), where the properties are usually lower. The result does not represent the load path.
4. **Verdict: query the supplier.** Nothing fails, but the certificate is not yet evidence. The supplier's records show
   that the hardness was on an untempered offcut; a test of the coupon gives 47 HRC (approximately 1560 MPa, 2% from
   the UTS). The drawing requires short-transverse tests for this part, and the supplier adds them. All checks are then
   correct.
5. **Receiving sample.** A hardness check on 13 closures, accepted only if all 13 pass, accepts a lot with 1% outside
   the limits 88% of the time. It accepts 95% of lots at 0.39% defective (AQL) and rejects 90% of lots at 16.2% (LTPD).
   To catch a lot with 5% defective with 95% confidence needs **59** parts. For a fracture-critical closure, this is
   why each part gets a hardness test, and not a sample.

## Where this stops

- **A review aid, not an acceptance.** Your inspection plan and your quality procedures decide what is accepted, and
  by whom.
- **The hardness conversion is approximate** (about ±10%) and for carbon and low-alloy steels only.
- **Sampling assumes a lot much larger than the sample,** random samples and a test that finds every defect.
- **Composites and non-metallics** are traced by batch of fibre, resin and prepreg, with shelf life and out-time, and
  by the cure record of each part. The same logic applies, with other documents.

## On the drawing

- **The material specification and condition,** with the heat-treatment specification ([Specifying materials](../../materials/specification/)).
- **The certificate type** that is required, usually on the purchase order. Make sure that the drawing and the order
  agree.
- **Traceability:** lot-traceable or serialised, and the identity to be kept through each process.
- **Marking:** the content, the method and its specification, the location, and where marking is not permitted, for
  example "no impression stamping".
- **Test direction and location** where they are important, for example short-transverse tests from a forging
  prolongation.
- **First article inspection** and the records to be delivered with the parts.
