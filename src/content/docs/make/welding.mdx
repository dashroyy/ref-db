## Why this matters

Welds join the cylinders, domes, skirts and bosses of metal motor cases, and they hold together much of the test
hardware: heavyweight cases, stands and pressure lines. A weld is not parent metal. It has a different
microstructure, residual stress near yield, a heat-affected zone that can be harder or softer than the plate, and
flaws that the process leaves.

Most weld failures start from one of three causes: a crack from hydrogen in a hard heat-affected zone, a flaw that
inspection did not find, or a weld put at a point of high cyclic stress. Each cause is controlled on the drawing and
in the welding procedure, not after the part is made.

<Analogy title="A casting inside the part">
  Making a weld is like pouring a small ingot into a mould cut in your part. The mould is cold metal, so it quenches
  the ingot and the metal next to it. A good foundry controls the temperature of the mould, keeps the melt dry and
  clean, and X-rays the casting. A welder does the same with preheat, dry consumables and NDT.
</Analogy>

## The weld and its heat-affected zone

The arc puts heat into the joint along a moving line. The **heat input** of one pass is:

$$
Q = k\,\frac{U\,I}{v}
$$

Here $U$ is the arc voltage, $I$ the current, $v$ the travel speed and $k$ the thermal efficiency of the process: 0.6
for TIG, 0.8 for manual metal arc and MIG/MAG, and 1.0 for submerged arc (EN 1011-1). The usual unit is kJ/mm.

The metal next to the weld pool does not melt, but it gets one fast thermal cycle. This band is the
**heat-affected zone** (HAZ). Each point gets a different peak temperature, so the HAZ has bands with different
microstructures.

<WeldZones />

In hardenable steels, the **cooling time from 800 to 500 °C**, $t_{8/5}$, sets the microstructure of the HAZ. A short
time gives hard martensite that cracks easily
([Metallurgy](../../fundamentals/metallurgy/#quenching-hardenability-and-martensite)). A long time gives soft, coarse
grains with low toughness. The heat input, the thickness and the preheat set $t_{8/5}$. In aluminium alloys and in quenched-and-tempered steels, the heat
over-ages or over-tempers the HAZ, which becomes **softer** than the plate.

## Hydrogen cracking and preheat

Hydrogen cracking (cold cracking) occurs when four conditions are present together:

1. **Hydrogen** in the weld, from moisture in the consumables, the flux, the shielding gas or the joint surface.
2. **A hard, susceptible microstructure,** usually martensite in the HAZ.
3. **Tensile stress,** from restraint and the residual stress of the weld.
4. **A low temperature,** below approximately 150 °C.

The crack can appear hours after welding, as the hydrogen diffuses to the hard zone. Remove one condition and it does
not occur. **Preheat** slows the cooling, which makes the HAZ softer, and keeps the joint warm while the hydrogen
diffuses out.

EN 1011-2 gives two methods to calculate the minimum preheat. Method B uses the carbon equivalent:

$$
\text{CET} = \text{C} + \frac{\text{Mn} + \text{Mo}}{10} + \frac{\text{Cr} + \text{Cu}}{20} + \frac{\text{Ni}}{40}
$$

$$
T_p = 697\,\text{CET} + 160\tanh\!\left(\frac{d}{35}\right) + 62\,\text{HD}^{0.35} + (53\,\text{CET} - 32)\,Q - 328
$$

Here $T_p$ is in °C, $d$ is the plate thickness in mm, HD the diffusible hydrogen in ml per 100 g of weld metal and
$Q$ the heat input in kJ/mm. The method applies for CET 0.2 to 0.5, $d$ 10 to 90 mm, HD 1 to 20 and $Q$ 0.5 to
4.0 kJ/mm. Method A uses the IIW carbon equivalent
$\text{CEV} = \text{C} + \text{Mn}/6 + (\text{Cr} + \text{Mo} + \text{V})/5 + (\text{Ni} + \text{Cu})/15$ and charts.

| Hydrogen scale | HD, ml/100 g | Typical source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | More than 15 | Cellulosic electrodes |
| B | 10 to 15 | Rutile electrodes; poorly stored consumables |
| C | 5 to 10 | Some flux-cored wires; basic electrodes not baked |
| D | 3 to 5 | Baked basic electrodes; MIG/MAG with solid wire |
| E | 3 or less | TIG; vacuum-packed electrodes |

The hydrogen level is the easiest part to control. Store and bake consumables as their maker specifies, and weld on a
clean, dry joint. Steels with a CET above 0.5, such as D6AC and 4340, are outside method B. They need a high
preheat, a controlled interpass temperature, and a stress relief or temper before the weld cools to room temperature.

## Cooling time

EN 1011-2 Annex D gives $t_{8/5}$ for two cases. In thick plate, heat flows in three directions (3D); in thin plate,
it flows in two (2D):

$$
t_{8/5}^{\,3D} = (6700 - 5T_0)\,Q\left(\frac{1}{500 - T_0} - \frac{1}{800 - T_0}\right)F_3
$$

$$
t_{8/5}^{\,2D} = (4300 - 4.3T_0)\times10^5\,\frac{Q^2}{d^2}\left[\left(\frac{1}{500 - T_0}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{1}{800 - T_0}\right)^2\right]F_2
$$

Here $T_0$ is the preheat or interpass temperature in °C, and $F_2$ and $F_3$ are factors for the shape of the joint
(1 for a bead on plate, 0.9 for the fill passes of a butt weld, less for fillet welds). Below the **transition
thickness**, the 2D equation applies and a thinner plate cools more slowly. Above it, the thickness does not change
$t_{8/5}$.

The welding procedure qualification gives the acceptable window of $t_{8/5}$ for a steel and a consumable, through
hardness and impact tests of the HAZ. The window converts directly to a range of heat input for each thickness and
preheat.

## The processes

| Process | Heat input | Hydrogen | Used for | Watch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TIG (GTAW, 141) | Low to medium, $k = 0.6$ | Very low | Thin walls, roots, titanium, aluminium, high-strength steels | Slow; shielding of the root and of titanium |
| MIG/MAG (GMAW, 131/135) | Medium, $k = 0.8$ | Low | Structural steel and aluminium, fast fill | Lack of fusion with short-circuit transfer |
| Manual metal arc (111) | Medium, $k = 0.8$ | Depends on the electrode | Site work and repairs | Electrode storage; slag inclusions |
| Submerged arc (121) | High, $k = 1.0$ | Depends on the flux | Thick plate, long seams | Coarse HAZ; flux storage |
| Electron beam (51) | Very low, deep and narrow | Very low (vacuum) | Deep welds with little distortion, in high-strength alloys | Fit-up; needs a vacuum chamber |
| Laser (52) | Very low | Low | Thin parts, small welds | Fit-up; fast cooling of the HAZ |
| Friction stir (43) | Solid state, no melting | None | Aluminium alloys, including 2219 and Al-Li | Large forces and fixtures; a keyhole at the end |

Each process has a different heat input and a different set of flaws. Choose the process with the joint, not after.

## Flaws and weld quality

<WeldDefects />

ISO 5817 gives three quality levels for steel welds: **B** (stringent), **C** (intermediate) and **D** (moderate).
Each sets limits for each type of flaw, for example the size of porosity, the depth of undercut and the misalignment.
For aerospace fusion welds, AWS D17.1 gives classes A, B and C, with Class A for welds whose failure would cause the
loss of the vehicle. The class sets the NDT, as in [NDT](../ndt/).

- **Planar flaws are the dangerous ones.** Lack of fusion and cracks are sharp. Porosity is round and usually less
  important for strength, but it shows a process that is not under control.
- **A repair weld is a new weld.** It has its own heat cycle, residual stress and HAZ, often in a part that was
  already heat-treated. Limit the number of repairs at one location, and repeat the NDT.

## Joint efficiency and strength

Pressure-vessel codes reduce the allowable stress of a welded joint with a **joint efficiency** $\eta$. It depends on
the joint type and on how much of the weld is inspected. ASME VIII Division 1, Table UW-12, gives:

| Butt joint | Full radiography | Spot | None |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Double-welded, or with the root made equal to it | 1.00 | 0.85 | 0.70 |
| Single-welded with a backing strip left in | 0.90 | 0.80 | 0.65 |

The efficiency is a factor for the uncertainty of the flaws, not a statement of the strength of the weld metal. It
enters the case wall directly ([Case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/)). Two further effects can make the joint weaker:

- **HAZ softening.** Aluminium alloys in the T6 and T8 tempers lose much of their strength in the HAZ. Eurocode 9 uses
  HAZ factors of approximately 0.5 to 0.65 for 6xxx alloys. The welded strength comes from the welding code or from
  tests of the procedure, not from the parent data.
- **Weld metal strength.** A filler that is weaker than the plate (undermatching) puts the strain into the weld. For
  high-strength steels, the usual answer is to weld before the final heat treatment, so the weld and the plate harden
  and temper together.

## Fatigue of welded joints

A welded joint fails in fatigue at a much lower stress range than the parent metal. The weld toe is a sharp notch,
there are small flaws at it from the start, and the residual stress is near yield. For these reasons, the fatigue
strength of a welded joint **does not increase with the strength of the steel**, and the mean stress has little
effect.

Codes give S–N curves by **detail class**. In the IIW recommendations, the FAT class is the stress range in MPa for
2 million cycles, on a slope of 3:

$$
N = 2\times10^6\left(\frac{\text{FAT}}{\Delta\sigma}\right)^3
$$

| Detail (steel) | FAT |
| --- | --- |
| Butt weld ground flush, NDT | 112 |
| Butt weld from the shop, flat, toe angle 30° or less, NDT | 90 |
| Other butt weld, NDT | 80 |
| Butt weld welded from one side, root checked by NDT | 71 |
| Butt weld welded from one side, root not checked | 36 |
| Load-carrying fillet weld, failure through the throat | 36 |

Aluminium classes are approximately 0.4 of the steel values. Three effects change the stress range at the weld:

- **Misalignment.** An axial offset $e$ between two plates of thickness $t$ adds bending. For equal thicknesses, the
  stress increases by $k_m = 1 + 3e/t$. The class already includes 1.15 to 1.3; more than that adds to the stress
  range.
- **Shell bending.** Near a flange, a closure or a change of thickness, the wall bends
  ([Hand calcs](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/#thin-shells)). Include this bending in the stress range at the weld.
- **Thickness.** Above 25 mm, the class decreases by $(25/t)^n$.

Weld toe grinding, hammer or needle peening, and HFMI treatment can increase the fatigue strength of the toe. They do
not help a crack that starts at the root.

## Shrinkage and distortion

The weld metal contracts as it cools, and the plate next to it holds it. The result is residual stress near yield
along the weld ([Residual stress](../../materials/residual-stress/)) and distortion of the part. For a butt weld in
steel, Blodgett gives the transverse shrinkage as:

$$
s = 0.2\,\frac{A_w}{t} + 0.05\,g
$$

Here $A_w$ is the area of the weld cross-section, $t$ the thickness and $g$ the root gap. A smaller groove makes less
shrinkage, so a narrow groove, a double-V groove or a beam process distorts less. A girth weld makes a cylinder shorter
and pulls its diameter in at the weld. Thus:

- **Add the shrinkage to the cut length,** and take it into the length stack ([Tolerance stack](../../drawing/tolerance-stack/)).
- **Machine the datums after welding** and after the heat treatment, where the tolerances are tight.
- **Weld in a balanced sequence,** in a fixture, and control the interpass temperature.

## Welding motor hardware

- **High-strength steel cases** (D6AC, 4340 types) are welded with TIG or electron beam before the final hardening and
  tempering. The weld gets a preheat and a stress relief before it cools, then the full case is hardened and tempered,
  then inspected. A repair weld after the final heat treatment needs its own qualified procedure.
- **Maraging steels** weld well in the solution-annealed condition, because the martensite is soft. The case is aged
  after welding.
- **Weldable low-alloy steels** such as 15CDV6 are welded in the hardened condition, with a modest preheat and a stress
  relief below the tempering temperature. They suit test hardware and some flight cases.
- **Titanium** needs a full inert shield on both sides of the weld until it is below approximately 400 °C. The colour
  of the weld shows contamination: silver or light straw is acceptable; blue, grey or white is not.
- **Aluminium alloys** such as 2219 weld with TIG, variable-polarity plasma arc or friction stir. Design for the
  welded strength of the HAZ.
- **Qualify everything.** The welding procedure is qualified by test (ISO 15614-1, or AWS D17.1 for aerospace) and the
  welders are qualified to it (ISO 9606-1). ISO 3834 sets the quality system of the welding shop.

## Worked example: the girth welds of a heavyweight test case

The ground tests of the 300 mm motor ([Hand calcs](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/#worked-example-hand-checks-for-the-300-mm-motors-test-campaign))
use a heavyweight case: a thick steel case that is used again for many firings. It is a 15CDV6 tube, 300 mm inside
diameter with a 12 mm wall, with a forged weld-neck flange at each end. A girth weld joins each flange to the tube:
single-V, welded from the outside with TIG, with no backing. The MEOP is 10 MPa. The criteria for this example only:
a $t_{8/5}$ window of 8 to 20 s, a partial factor $\gamma_{Mf} = 1.25$, and 1000 cycles (250 firings × 4). Push
**Load worked example** in the calculator to load these inputs.

1. **The steel.** The certificate of the heat gives 0.15 C, 1.0 Mn, 1.4 Cr, 0.9 Mo, 0.25 V, 0.1 Ni and 0.1 Cu
   (typical values for this example). CET is **0.42**, inside method B. CEV is 0.84, which shows a hardenable steel.
2. **Heat input.** The fill passes use 12 V, 200 A and 1.3 mm/s. The arc energy is 1.85 kJ/mm; with $k = 0.6$ for
   TIG, $Q$ is **1.11 kJ/mm**.
3. **Preheat.** With a 12 mm plate and scale D hydrogen (5 ml/100 g), the minimum preheat is **114 °C**. The
   procedure says 125 °C, to allow for the measurement. With damp filler at 10 ml/100 g, it would need 144 °C; with
   3 ml/100 g, 96 °C.
4. **Cooling time.** At 125 °C, the fill passes cool from 800 to 500 °C in **14.2 s**, inside the window. The plate is
   below the transition thickness of 16.9 mm, so the heat flows in 2D. The root pass (10 V, 100 A, 1.0 mm/s, so
   0.6 kJ/mm) cools in **4.2 s**: too fast. The window needs 0.83 to 1.32 kJ/mm at this preheat. Either increase the
   heat input of the root pass, or show with hardness tests in the procedure qualification that the next passes temper
   the root HAZ enough.
5. **Shrinkage.** A 60° groove with a 2 mm gap and a 1.5 mm root face has an area of 88 mm². Each girth weld shortens
   the case by approximately **1.6 mm**, so 3.1 mm for the two. Cut the tube long by that amount, and machine the
   flange faces and the seal grooves after welding and stress relief.
6. **Fatigue, as designed.** Each firing is one pressure cycle. The axial membrane stress across the girth weld is
   $pr/2t = 10 \times 156 / 24 = 65$ MPa. The flange stops the growth of the tube, so the wall bends near it. With a
   rigid flange, the edge bending is 201 MPa at the flange and falls to zero 26 mm away. The weld sits at the end of
   the hub, 60 mm from the flange, where the bending is −40 MPa: the range at the outer surface is **105 MPa**. The
   root is checked by RT, so the detail is FAT 71. The drawing allows 1 mm of offset: $k_m = 1.25$, which the class
   includes. The design life is **317 000 cycles**, against 1000 required (damage 0.003).
7. **Fatigue, the wrong way.** Put the same weld at the root of the flange, and do not check the root. The range is
   now $65 + 201 = 266$ MPa on FAT 36. The life is **2550 cycles**, 124 times less, and below the $10^4$ cycles where
   the S–N data starts. With 2 mm of offset, it is 1660 cycles. The cure costs nothing on the drawing: a weld-neck
   flange that moves the weld away from the flange, and an inspected root.

The heavyweight case passes by a large margin, as designed. The two decisions that made it pass were made on the
drawing: where the weld is, and how the root is inspected.

## Where this stops

- **EN 1011-2 method B is for non-alloy, fine-grained and low-alloy steels** inside its range. High-hardenability
  steels (CET above 0.5), stainless steels, nickel alloys, titanium and aluminium need their own procedures.
- **The cooling-time equations** assume a single pass on a uniform plate. Multi-pass welds temper earlier passes, and
  real joints have different thicknesses on each side.
- **Blodgett's shrinkage rule** is an empirical rule for steel butt welds. Distortion of a real part depends on the
  sequence, the fixtures and the restraint. Measure the first parts.
- **The IIW curves** are for the nominal stress method with constant-amplitude loading, in air, below 1.5 × yield.
  For corrosion, high temperature, a few cycles at high stress or a known flaw, use fracture mechanics
  ([Leak before break](../../verify/leak-before-break/)).
- **No weld-metal or HAZ toughness.** The procedure qualification shows these by test.

## On the drawing

- **The weld symbols** to ISO 2553 or AWS A2.4: the joint, the groove, the size and the process.
- **The welding standard and quality level,** for example AWS D17.1 Class A, or ISO 5817 level B, with ISO 3834.
- **The qualified procedure and welder qualification** that the shop must use, or the requirement for them.
- **Preheat, interpass and post-weld heat treatment,** where the design depends on them, or the procedure that sets
  them.
- **The NDT of each weld,** with its method, extent, acceptance standard and the delay after welding.
- **The geometry that the fatigue analysis assumes:** the maximum offset, a flush-ground surface or toe treatment where
  needed, and the root condition.
- **Repair limits:** how many repairs are allowed at one location, and that each repair needs approval and repeat NDT.
- **Machining after welding** for datums and seal surfaces, with the allowance for shrinkage.
