## Why this matters

A motor case is a pressure boundary with many joints: the closures, the nozzle-to-case joint, the igniter boss, the
transducer and test ports and their plugs. Each has seals, and each seal is checked by a leak test before the motor
leaves the factory. The leak test is often the only proof that a joint was assembled correctly: that the O-ring is
present, not cut, not twisted, and in the correct groove.

A leak test proves only what it can see. A test with a sensitivity of $10^{-3}$ mbar·L/s says nothing about a leak of
$10^{-5}$. A test that drifts with temperature can fail a good joint or pass a bad one. Thus the test must be designed,
in the same way as the joint.

<Analogy title="A slow puncture">
  To find a slow puncture, you pump up the tyre, wait a day and check the pressure. If the night was cold, the pressure
  is lower even with no puncture; if the sun was on the tyre, it is higher even with one. To find where the air comes
  out, you put the tube in water and look for bubbles. Pressure decay and tracer tests are these two methods, made
  quantitative.
</Analogy>

## "No leaks" is not a requirement

A leak requirement has five parts. Without all five, a pass means only that the test could not see anything:

1. **The allowable leak rate,** with its unit.
2. **The gas,** for example helium or nitrogen.
3. **The pressure,** upstream and downstream, and the direction.
4. **The method** and its demonstrated sensitivity, smaller than the allowable.
5. **The conditions:** temperature, soak and hold time, and the calibration with a reference leak.

## Leak-rate units

A leak rate is a flow of gas expressed as pressure × volume per time. It is a quantity of gas, like a mass flow:

| Unit | In Pa·m³/s | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pa·m³/s | 1 | SI unit |
| mbar·L/s | 0.1 | Usual in Europe and on helium leak detectors |
| atm·cm³/s (std cm³/s) | 0.1013 | Usual in the US and in pressure-decay work |
| sccm (std cm³/min) | 0.00169 | Flow meters |
| Torr·L/s | 0.1333 | Vacuum work |

**1 mbar·L/s is 1 cm³ of gas at 1 bar each second.** At $10^{-6}$ mbar·L/s, 1 cm³ is lost in 12 days.

## The methods

| Method | Sensitivity, mbar·L/s | Integral or local | Good for | Watch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bubble, immersion or spray | $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$ | Local | Finding gross leaks, cheaply | Operator; wetting; contamination of the part |
| Pressure decay | $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$ | Integral | A whole assembly, or the space between two seals | Temperature, test volume, hold time |
| Vacuum decay (pressure rise) | About $10^{-4}$ | Integral | Parts that can be evacuated | Outgassing and moisture |
| Mass flow | About $10^{-3}$ | Integral | Large volumes, faster than decay | Temperature; flow-meter range |
| Hydrogen tracer (5% H₂ in N₂) | $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-7}$ | Local | Low background, low-cost gas | Calibration; ventilation |
| Helium sniffer | $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-7}$ | Local | Finding the leak on a pressurised part | Background helium, probe distance and speed |
| Helium accumulation (hood) | $10^{-7}$ to $10^{-8}$ | Integral in the hood | The total rate of a pressurised part at atmosphere | Hood volume, mixing, permeation |
| Helium vacuum (chamber or inside-out) | $10^{-9}$ and below | Integral | The highest sensitivity | Needs vacuum; pressure direction; time |

Use a sensitive integral test to measure the rate, and a local test to find the leak if it fails. The sensitivity in
your test comes from the procedure and a calibrated reference leak (ISO 20486), not from the table.

## Pressure decay, done correctly

Fill the test volume $V$ to the test pressure, isolate it, and measure the pressure drop over the hold time $t$. With
the ideal gas law at constant volume, a change of gas temperature also changes the pressure. The leak rate is:

$$
Q = \frac{V}{t}\left(\Delta p - p_\text{abs}\,\frac{\Delta T}{T}\right)
$$

Here $\Delta p$ is the measured pressure drop, $\Delta T$ the measured fall of gas temperature and $T$ the absolute
temperature. At 8 bar absolute, a temperature change of only 0.05 K changes the pressure by 137 Pa, which is often more
than the leak. Thus:

- **Soak before the hold.** Filling heats the gas (adiabatic compression). Wait until the gas, the part and the gauge
  have a stable temperature, and record it.
- **Know the uncertainty.** Each reading of pressure and temperature has a resolution. The smallest leak that the test
  can show is approximately $\tfrac{V}{t}\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\delta p^2 + (p\,\delta T/T)^2}$. A longer hold and a smaller
  volume make it smaller.
- **Keep the volume small and known.** The gauge, the tubing and the valves are part of the test volume. They are often
  larger than the space between the seals. Measure the volume, for example by expansion from a known volume.
- **A pass clears everything; a fail locates nothing.** Decay measures the sum of all leaks from the test volume. Use a
  tracer or a bubble test to find the leak.

## Helium tracer methods

Helium is used because it is inert, light (it goes through small paths), and rare in air (approximately 5 ppm), so a
mass-spectrometer detector sees a small amount above the background.

- **Sniffer:** a probe moved along a pressurised part finds where helium comes out. Its sensitivity depends on the
  distance and the speed of the probe and on the background. It locates; it does not measure the total.
- **Accumulation (hood):** a sealed hood of free volume $V$ around the part collects the leak. The concentration
  increases by $\Delta C$ in the time $t$, so $Q_\text{He} = \Delta C\,V\,p_\text{atm}/t$. Divide by the helium fraction
  in the part to get the total leak. A longer time and a smaller hood find smaller leaks.
- **Vacuum:** the part (or a chamber around it) is evacuated and connected to the detector. It is the most sensitive
  method, but the pressure across the seal may be in the opposite direction to service.
- **Permeation.** Helium goes through elastomers slowly. After some minutes it comes through an O-ring by permeation,
  even with no leak path. Measure before that, or measure the permeation background and subtract it.

## From the test rate to the service rate

A test with helium at a low pressure does not give the leak of the service gas at MEOP. Through the same leak path, the
flow depends on the regime. For a capillary of diameter $d$ and length $L$:

$$
\text{viscous:}\quad Q = \frac{\pi d^4}{256\,\mu L}\left(p_1^2 - p_2^2\right)
\qquad
\text{molecular:}\quad Q = \frac{\pi}{12}\,\bar v\,\frac{d^3}{L}\,(p_1 - p_2),\quad \bar v = \sqrt{\frac{8RT}{\pi M}}
$$

Viscous flow applies when the path is much larger than the mean free path of the gas, $\lambda = \tfrac{\mu}{p}\sqrt{\pi RT/2M}$
(Knudsen number $\lambda/d$ below about 0.01). It is usual for leaks above approximately $10^{-5}$ mbar·L/s. Molecular
flow applies to very small leaks and low pressures. In viscous flow the rate increases with the square of the pressure,
so a leak at MEOP can be hundreds of times the leak in a low-pressure test.

Two results for the design:

- **Write the requirement as a test rate,** with its gas, pressures and method, and keep the argument that relates it
  to the service condition in the analysis.
- **Pressure-energised seals close as the pressure increases.** An O-ring at MEOP usually leaks less than the viscous
  estimate. A crack or a porous weld does not close.

## Leak testing motor hardware

<LeakPort />

- **Joints with two seals** get a port between the seals ([Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/#leak-check-ports)).
  It is the only way to test each seal of a redundant pair. The test pressure pushes the primary seal from its back,
  opposite to service: where possible, also test in the service direction, for example with low-pressure helium in the
  case and accumulation at the port.
- **Port plugs** are part of the pressure boundary after the test. Each needs its own seal, torque and check.
- **The case** gets a leak test after the proof test, usually with gas at a much lower pressure than proof, because of
  the stored energy ([Proof testing](../../verify/proof-testing/#test-media-and-safety)).
- **Before and after environments.** Repeat the joint leak tests after vibration, thermal cycling and transport, and
  after each reassembly. A seal that passes once can be disturbed.
- **A through crack is a leak.** Where the case can leak before it breaks, a leak test finds a crack that grew through
  the wall ([Leak before break](../../verify/leak-before-break/)).

## Worked example: the aft closure seals

The aft closure has two radial O-rings with a port between them ([Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/#worked-example-the-aft-closure-seal)).
The requirement for this example only is $1 \times 10^{-5}$ std cm³/s of helium at 700 kPa gauge between the seals.
Push **Load worked example** in the calculator to load these inputs.

1. **The test volume.** The space between the seals is 2 cm³, but the port, the line and the gauge add 10 cm³. The test
   volume is **12 cm³**. A shorter line would make every result below better.
2. **Pressure decay.** Nitrogen at 700 kPa gauge, a 600 s hold, a gauge resolution of 20 Pa and a temperature
   resolution of 0.02 K. The pressure falls by 150 Pa and the gas cools by 0.05 K. Without the temperature correction,
   the leak is $3.0 \times 10^{-5}$ std cm³/s: three times the allowable, and the joint would be rejected. The cooling
   explains 137 Pa of the drop. The corrected leak is $2.6 \times 10^{-6} \pm 1.6 \times 10^{-5}$ std cm³/s:
   **inconclusive**, because the uncertainty is larger than the allowable.
3. **What controls the uncertainty.** One reading of 0.02 K is 55 Pa at this pressure, against 20 Pa for the gauge.
   With these instruments, the uncertainty equals the allowable after a 16 minute hold, and half of it after 33 minutes,
   if the temperature stays stable for that long. A better temperature measurement or a smaller volume is the better
   answer.
4. **Helium hood.** The space between the seals is filled with helium at 700 kPa, and a 5 L hood collects for 10
   minutes on the outside of the joint. The rise is 0.05 ppm, below the 0.1 ppm resolution of the detector. The leak of
   the secondary seal is less than $8.3 \times 10^{-7}$ std cm³/s: **12 times below** the allowable. The hood does not
   see the primary seal, which leaks into the case. The decay test, or helium in the case with accumulation at the
   port, covers it.
5. **Test to service.** The allowable is the flow through a capillary of approximately 1.7 µm over the 3 mm seal
   contact. With air at the 10 MPa MEOP, the flow is viscous and the same path passes **175 times** more:
   $1.7 \times 10^{-3}$ std cm³/s, or 1 cm³ at 1 bar every 9 minutes. The O-ring is pressure-energised, so the real
   service leak is less; a crack would not be.

## Where this stops

- **Ideal gas at constant volume** for pressure decay. The volume changes a little with pressure, and the gas
  temperature is not uniform. Calibrate the test with a reference leak in the test volume.
- **One round capillary** for the conversion. Real leak paths are slots, porosity and cracks, often in the transitional
  regime. The conversion gives limits, not a value.
- **No permeation** through elastomers, and no outgassing or moisture.
- **No hot gas.** The leak of hot combustion gas through a seal is an erosion problem first: keep the gas away from the
  seal ([Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/)).

## On the drawing

- **The allowable leak rate** with its unit, the gas, the test pressure and its direction.
- **The method and its standard,** for example helium accumulation to ISO 20485, or pressure decay to EN 13184, and the
  sensitivity that it must demonstrate with a reference leak.
- **The soak and hold times** and the temperature record for pressure decay.
- **Where the test is done:** each joint and port, and the sequence: after proof, after environments, after each
  reassembly.
- **The port plugs:** seal, torque and their own check.
