## Why this matters

The process decides more than the cost. It decides the properties of the material (grain flow, porosity, residual
stress), the tolerance and finish that the shop holds without extra work, the inspection that the part needs, and
the lead time. A drawing that ignores the process can ask for a tolerance that only grinding holds on a feature that a
forging leaves rough, or for an internal passage that no tool can reach.

Most of a motor is machined: closures, bosses, nozzle housings, joint rings. The material for these parts comes as
plate, bar, forging or ring. Cases are welded from rolled sheet, flow formed, or wound in composite. Additive
manufacturing (AM) has a place where it removes joints or makes internal passages possible, not where it makes a
machinable part more expensively.

<Analogy title="Carving and moulding">
  A sculptor can carve a figure from a block of wood, or mould it in clay and fire it. Carving is quick to start but
  wastes most of the block, and the grain of the wood runs straight through the arms and the neck, where it is weak.
  Moulding needs a mould, which costs time and money, but each figure after the first is cheap and the shape can be
  anything. Machining from bar, forging and casting are the same choice, made with steel.
</Analogy>

## Product form and grain

<ProcessGrain />

- **Plate and bar** are quick to get and need no tooling. But much of the material becomes chips (a high
  **buy-to-fly ratio**, the mass bought divided by the finished mass), and the part cuts across the grain. In thick plate
  the **short-transverse** direction, through the thickness, has the lowest strength, elongation and toughness.
- **Forgings** put the grain along the shape, and give better short-transverse properties and fatigue resistance at the
  fillets. The cost is the die and a lead time of months. Specify the grain flow or a macro-etch acceptance on the
  drawing where it matters.
- **Rolled rings** give circumferential grain for flanges, joint rings and skirt rings.
- **The allowables must be for the form that you buy.** A forging allowable does not apply to a part machined from
  plate, and a thin-section allowable does not apply to a thick section, because the quench rate is lower. See
  [Allowables](../../materials/allowables/) and [Material specification](../../materials/specification/).
- **Peripheral coarse grain** in some aluminium extrusions and forgings must be machined off loaded surfaces. Size the
  starting material for it ([Rocket motor hardware](../../motor/hardware/)).

**Design rule.** Give precision only to the features that need it. One seal bore at ±0.03 mm does not make the rest
of the part a ±0.03 mm part. The general tolerance covers the rest ([Fits & limits](../../drawing/fits-and-limits/#general-tolerances-iso-2768)).

## Thin-walled cases

- **Rolled and welded.** Sheet is rolled into a cylinder and welded along its length, then welded to forged domes or
  rings. The welds need a qualified procedure, full NDT and a joint efficiency in the sizing
  ([Case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/)). Steels such as 15CDV6 and maraging steels are used because they tolerate
  welding.
- **Flow formed.** A thick tubular preform, often a forging, is pressed onto a mandrel by rollers that reduce its wall
  in several passes. The case has no longitudinal weld, and cold work increases its strength. Wall reductions of 60–80%
  are usual. The wall tolerance is good, but the properties are directional, so test coupons from the formed
  direction.
- **Filament wound.** Fibre in resin is wound onto a mandrel over the metal bosses. With composites, the process is
  the material: the fibre and resin, the winding pattern, the tension and the cure are all part of the definition
  ([Rocket motor hardware](../../motor/hardware/)).

## Additive manufacturing

**Use AM where it removes assemblies or makes internal geometry possible.** Good candidates have internal channels,
several parts and joints that one part can replace, lattices or topology-optimised shapes, and a low quantity. Poor
candidates are simple prismatic parts, large flat sealing faces, high quantities, and parts with no route to
qualification.

**Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) rules of thumb:**

- Surfaces steeper than about 45° from the build plate support themselves. Shallower down-facing surfaces need
  supports, which leave marks and must be removed.
- Horizontal holes up to approximately 6–8 mm build without support. Larger holes need a teardrop shape, or support,
  and machining.
- The thinnest wall is approximately 0.3–0.5 mm, depending on the machine.
- Internal channels need a path for the powder to come out, and a way to inspect them. Below approximately 1 mm the
  powder cannot be removed reliably.
- The build orientation sets the anisotropy: properties in the build direction are usually different.

**Post-processing is part of the process:** stress relief before the part comes off the plate; hot isostatic pressing
(HIP) to close porosity in fatigue-loaded parts; machining of every sealing face, thread and datum; and CT inspection of
internal features. The as-built surface (Ra 6–15 µm) has a large effect on fatigue: machine or polish the critical
surfaces.

<Aside type="caution" title="Allowables belong to the process, not to the alloy">
  AM properties depend on the machine, the powder and the qualified parameter set. A different supplier or a change to
  the parameters needs new data. For spaceflight hardware, NASA-STD-6030 and MSFC-STD-3716 define the classification,
  the qualification of the process and the controls on each build.
</Aside>

## Cost and quantity

The cost of one part by a route is:

$$
C = \frac{C_\text{tooling}}{N} + \text{BTF}\cdot m\cdot p + C_\text{process} + h\cdot r
$$

with $N$ the quantity, BTF the buy-to-fly ratio, $m$ the finished mass, $p$ the material price, $h$ the machining
hours and $r$ the hourly rate. A route with tooling (a forging die, a casting pattern) is expensive at low quantity
and cheaper at high quantity. The **break-even quantity** between a route $a$ without tooling and a route $b$ with
tooling is the tooling cost of $b$ divided by the saving of $b$ on each part. For expensive materials such as titanium
and nickel alloys, the buy-to-fly ratio is usually the largest term.

## Worked example: the aft closure and a port manifold

The starting values in each tab of the calculator are the values of this example. The route costs are illustrative.

1. **The tightest feature of the closure** is the nozzle seal bore: ⌀150 H8 (±0.0315 mm) and Ra 0.4 µm
   ([Surface finish](../../drawing/surface-finish/#worked-example-the-surfaces-of-the-300-mm-motor)). In the **Process
   screen**, grinding and honing hold this as usual; turning, milling and EDM hold it only with care. Forging, casting
   and AM hold it only after machining. Thus the route is a forging or bar, turned, with the seal bore finish-turned
   under tool control or honed.
2. **The cost.** The closure is D6AC, 6 kg finished, at £12/kg from the material library and £90/h. At 20 closures,
   **bar** costs approximately **£1,476** each, an investment casting £1,533, a forging £2,190 and LPBF £2,819. The
   casting is cheaper than bar above 23 parts, and the forging above 47 parts. At 200 parts, the forging costs £1,065.
3. **The decision.** A cast pressure closure needs a casting factor, more inspection and a qualification of the
   foundry, so the cost of the casting route is higher than the table shows. For a development batch of 20, buy bar
   and machine it. For production, forge it: the grain then follows the boss, and the short-transverse properties at
   the fillets are better.
4. **A port manifold in AM.** The pressure transducer, the leak-check port and the vent of the aft closure use three
   fittings and two drilled cross-holes. One LPBF part in 316L or Ti-6Al-4V can replace them, with internal channels of
   ⌀3 mm and 150 mm long. In the **AM design check**: the channels have L/D 50, which is at the limit for removing the
   powder, so add exits and plan CT. The lowest overhang is 40°, which is possible but rough: turn the part, or add a
   chamfer. The ⌀7 mm horizontal port sags at the top: ream it, and machine every sealing face of the ports.

## Where this stops

- **Typical capabilities.** Shops and machines differ. Use the capability data of your supplier, demonstrated on your
  material.
- **Simple cost model.** No scrap, inspection, lead time or overhead, and one batch. Use quotes for decisions.
- **AM rules of thumb.** They are for common metal powders on common machines. The qualified parameter set and the
  design guide of your supplier give the real limits.
- **No propellant processing.** The casting and curing of propellant are energetics processes, outside the scope of
  this site.

## On the drawing

- **The product form** and its specification: plate, bar, forging or casting, with the forging grain flow or a
  macro-etch acceptance where it matters.
- **Machining allowances** and the condition of the surfaces that stay as forged, cast or built.
- **For AM parts:** the classification, the qualified process specification, the build orientation where it matters,
  HIP and heat treatment, the surfaces to machine, and the inspection (CT, witness coupons).
- **For cast parts:** the casting quality class, the radiographic acceptance and the casting factor in the analysis.
- **Only the precision that the function needs,** with the general tolerance for the rest.
