## Why this matters

On a motor, bolts hold the closures and the nozzle against the chamber pressure. They attach the igniter, the skirts
and the installed units, and they keep the seal glands closed. They fail in a small number of ways:

- **Separation.** The clamped parts lift apart. On a pressure joint, the seal gland opens, which is a leak path.
- **Yield.** This occurs during tightening, from tension and thread torsion together, or later under load.
- **Fatigue.** This occurs at the first engaged thread, where the stress concentration is worst.
- **Loosening.** Vibration turns the nut back. See [Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/).
- **Thread stripping.** This is a separate check, on the [Threads](../threads/) page.

All of these depend on one number that is difficult to control: the **preload**.

## Torque and preload

The tightening torque has three parts. One part stretches the bolt up the thread helix, one overcomes the thread
friction, and one overcomes the friction under the head or nut:

$$
T = F_i\left[\frac{P}{2\pi} + \frac{\mu_t\,d_2}{2\cos 30^\circ} + \mu_b\,\frac{D_{km}}{2}\right] \;\approx\; K\,F_i\,d
$$

Here $P$ is the thread pitch, $d_2$ the pitch diameter, $\mu_t$ and $\mu_b$ the thread and head friction, and $D_{km}$
the mean bearing diameter. The short form uses one **nut factor** $K$.

<TorqueSplit />

Friction changes from bolt to bolt. Thus one torque gives a band of preloads, not one value. Torque control is the least
accurate method. Turn-of-nut, measurement of bolt stretch (for example with ultrasound) and direct tension indicators
are each more accurate. The scatter for your method, and how much preload you can use, come from your applicable
standard.

## Stiffness and the joint diagram

The bolt and the clamped parts are two springs in series with the same preload. The bolt stretches by $F_i/k_b$, and
the parts compress by $F_i/k_m$. The stiffness of the bolt comes from its sections in series:

$$
\frac{1}{k_b} = \frac{1}{E_b}\left(\frac{0.5d}{A_d} + \frac{l_s}{A_d} + \frac{l_t}{A_{d3}} + \frac{0.5d}{A_{d3}} + \frac{0.4d}{A_d}\right)
$$

The terms are the head, the plain shank, the threaded length in the grip, the engaged thread and the nut. The clamped
parts carry the load in two 30° cones from the bearing faces:

$$
k_m = \frac{\pi E_m d \tan 30^\circ}{2 \ln\!\left[\dfrac{(l\tan 30^\circ + d_w - d)(d_w + d)}{(l\tan 30^\circ + d_w + d)(d_w - d)}\right]}
$$

An external load $P$ that pulls the parts apart stretches the bolt a little more, and decreases the load on the parts a
little. Only the **load factor** share gets to the bolt:

$$
\Phi = \frac{k_b}{k_b + k_m}, \qquad P_{sep} = \frac{F_i}{1-\Phi}
$$

$$
F_b = F_i + \Phi P, \qquad F_c = F_i - (1-\Phi)P
$$

The clamped parts are usually much stiffer than the bolt, so $\Phi$ is small, often 0.1 to 0.3. When $P$ gets to
$P_{sep}$, the clamp force is zero, the joint opens, and the bolt carries all the load.

<Analogy title="A tug of war on a rope already pulled tight">
  Two teams hold a rope tight against a post between them. A child adds a small pull to one team. The tension in the
  rope almost does not change. The additional pull mostly decreases the force on the post. Only when the force on the
  post is zero does each additional pull go directly into the rope. The bolt is the rope, and the clamped parts are the

## Margins

Each check uses the preload that is worst for it, with the factors and the preload scatter of your programme
([Margin policy](../../verify/margin-policy/)):

- **Separation:** use the minimum preload (low scatter, losses, the worst temperature):
  $MS = F_{i,min} / \big(k_{sep}(1-\Phi)P\big) - 1$.
- **Yield and ultimate:** use the maximum preload with the factored load. The bolt load is $F_{i,max} + \Phi\,kP$
  while the joint stays closed, and $kP$ after it has opened.
- **Tightening:** at the maximum preload, the bolt has tension and thread torsion together:
  $\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2}$, with $\tau$ from the thread torque on the stress-area diameter. VDI 2230
  usually tightens to approximately 90% of yield in this combined stress.

Standards such as NASA-STD-5020 give more rules, for example how to use preload in the ultimate check, and which loads
to use for separation. Follow your applicable standard where it is different.

## Temperature

When the bolt and the clamped parts expand differently, the preload changes:

$$
\Delta F_i = (\alpha_m - \alpha_b)\,\Delta T\, l\;\frac{k_b k_m}{k_b + k_m}
$$

Steel bolts in an aluminium closure lose preload when cold and get more preload when hot. Titanium or composite parts
have the opposite sign. A motor is assembled at room temperature, and it is stored, transported and fired anywhere in
its qualified range. Thus check the two ends of the range. Plates of different materials also slide against each other
across the bolt pattern. See [Differential expansion](../differential-expansion/).

## Fatigue

Only $\Phi P$ gets to the bolt. Thus preload protects it. A load cycle from zero to $P$ changes the bolt stress by only
$\pm\Phi P / 2A_s$. But bolts have a low fatigue strength, because the first engaged thread is a sharp notch. Thus
rolled threads (rolled after heat treatment where possible) and a high, reliable preload are important. Assess the
stress change on the [Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/) page. A joint that separates under load loses this
protection fully.

## Pressure closures on a motor

The chamber pressure acts over the seal diameter, and the bolts share the result:

$$
P = \frac{p\,\pi D_s^2/4}{n}
$$

- **The seal gland must stay closed:** at proof pressure as well as at MEOP, at the cold condition, and with the
  minimum preload. If the parts lift, the O-ring must follow the gap. See [O-ring design](../../seals/o-ring-design/).
- **Flanges turn and pry.** A flange that bends adds a prying force, which the concentric model on this page does not
  include. Keep the bolts near the shell wall and the flange stiff. Analyse prying where the flange is thin.
- **Many smaller bolts** spread the clamp more evenly than a few large bolts, and they keep the gland closed between
  the bolts.

## Worked example: the aft closure

The 300 mm case has a bolted aft closure. MEOP is 10.0 MPa ([MEOP build-up](../../motor/propellant/)), and the seal
diameter is 260 mm. There are 30 M10 class 12.9 bolts through a 24 mm steel grip (16 mm of plain shank), tightened to
60 N·m, with a lubricant that gives μ = 0.12. The preload scatter of ±25% and the factors (separation 1.2, yield 1.1,
ultimate 1.25, fitting 1.15) are **for this example only**. Push **Load worked example**.

1. **Load:** $10.0 \times \pi \times 130^2 = 531$ kN on the closure, so $P = 17.7$ kN for each bolt.
2. **Preload:** 36.5 kN ($K = 0.164$). Only 15% of the torque stretches the bolt. With ±25%, the preload is between
   27.4 and 45.6 kN.
3. **Stiffness:** $k_b = 362$ kN/mm and $k_m = 2097$ kN/mm, so $\Phi = 0.147$. Each bolt gets 2.6 kN of its 17.7 kN.
4. **Separation:** the joint opens at $27.4/(1 - 0.147) = 32.1$ kN. Against $1.2 \times 17.7 = 21.2$ kN,
   **MS = +0.51**.
5. **Yield:** the factored load is $1.1 \times 1.15 \times 17.7 = 22.4$ kN. The bolt load is
   $45.6 + 0.147 \times 22.4 = 48.9$ kN, against $1100 \times 58.0 = 63.8$ kN: **MS = +0.30**.
6. **Ultimate:** $1.25 \times 1.15 \times 17.7 = 25.4$ kN. The bolt load is 49.4 kN, against 70.7 kN: **MS = +0.43**.
   The joint is still closed, so the preload carries most of the load.
7. **Tightening:** 70% of yield in combined stress at the nominal preload, and 87% at the top of the scatter.
8. **Fatigue:** each pressurisation changes the bolt stress by only ±22 MPa.

**The same closure in aluminium** (7075, the same bolts and the same torque) shows the trade-offs. The softer flange
increases $\Phi$ to 0.33. A cold soak 60 °C below the assembly temperature causes a loss of 3.9 kN of preload. The
separation margin increases to +0.65, because less of the load comes out of the clamp. But the bolt now gets a third of
each load. The yield margin decreases to +0.15, and the fatigue stress change more than doubles, to ±50 MPa.

**Dry threads** at the same 60 N·m give only 25.6 kN. This is 30% less preload, which decreases all the margins that
depend on it.

## Where this stops

- **One bolt, concentric axial load, one clamped material.** Eccentric loads, prying and flange rotation need the
  eccentric method of VDI 2230 or a finite-element model. Loads applied within the clamped parts, and not under the
  head and nut, decrease $\Phi$ (the load-introduction factor of VDI 2230).
- **No gaskets.** A soft gasket in the grip makes $k_m$ small and $\Phi$ large.
- **Shear joints** need a different check: friction grip from the clamp force, or bearing and bolt shear after they
  slip.
- [Thread stripping](../threads/) and [locking](../locking-and-retention/) have their own pages. Here, embedding and
  relaxation are entered as a percentage loss.
- **Basic stress area.** The thread list is the site's [thread library](../threads/#limits-of-size): ISO metric coarse
  and fine, MJ, UNC, UNF, UNJC and UNJF. The margins use the basic stress area $A_s$, as ISO 898-1 and the bolt
  specifications do. At the 6g minimum-material limits, the M10 stress area is 55.4 mm², 4.4% below 58.0 mm²: with it,
  the yield margin of the worked example falls from +0.30 to +0.25. Use the minimum-material area where your programme
  asks for it.

## On the drawing

- **Torque value and tolerance, and the lubricant.** The lubricant is as important as the torque.
- **Tightening sequence and passes:** a cross pattern in stages, so that the flange pulls down evenly.
- **Fastener specification:** property class or material specification, thread class, plating and its hydrogen
  embrittlement relief, and washer type and hardness.
- **Locking feature and reuse rules:** if a fastener or locking element can be used again, and any re-torque after a
  soak or a test.
