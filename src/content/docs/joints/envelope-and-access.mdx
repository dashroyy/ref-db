## Why this matters

A CAD model shows the parts where they will be at the end. It does not show the hand, the socket, the torque wrench,
the borescope or the crane hook that put them there. A bolt that cannot be reached is found on the shop floor, when
the parts are made and the schedule is fixed. Then the fix is a scallop cut in a finished part, a special tool, or a
fastener tightened to "as tight as it would go": an uncontrolled preload in a joint that the analysis assumed was
controlled.

<Analogy title="A ship in a bottle">
  A ship in a bottle fits perfectly once it is in. The skill is all in the order of assembly and the tools, which you
  never see in the finished model. A motor is the same: every part must go in through a space that the parts already
  in place leave open.
</Analogy>

## Check every fastener for

- **Socket clearance.** The outside diameter of the socket, not the bolt head, sets the minimum distance to a wall and
  the minimum pitch to the next fastener. Check against the actual tool that you will use: sockets of the same size
  differ by maker and drive.
- **The torque wrench path.** The wrench length and the swing it needs. A fastener that you can reach but not tighten to
  its torque has an uncontrolled preload ([Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/#torque-and-preload)).
- **The insertion path.** The bolt must get into the hole from somewhere: count its full length plus the head.
- **Visibility.** The assembler must see the fastener seat and the locking feature, and the inspector must see the
  witness mark or the lock wire ([Locking and retention](../locking-and-retention/)).

**Socket-head cap screws in counterbores** need much less space around them than hex heads: the tool is a hex key or a
bit socket that is about the size of the head. They suit closely spaced bolts and fasteners near walls.

## Beyond fasteners

- **Assembly order.** Can every part get to its position with the others already in place? Write the sequence down
  while you design, and check it in the model step by step.
- **Lifting points and the centre of gravity** for anything that you cannot lift by hand: where the hook goes, what it
  lifts against, and the proof load of the lifting feature.
- **Inspection access** for the CMM probe, the borescope and the NDT transducer. An ultrasonic probe needs a flat
  surface and a path for the couplant; radiography needs the film or detector on the other side
  ([NDT](../../make/ndt/)).
- **Thermal clearance at the extremes.** Parts that fit at 20 °C can touch at the operating limit
  ([Differential expansion](../differential-expansion/)).
- **Harness and pipe routes** with their bend radii, connectors and clamps, not as thin lines. Follow your wiring
  standard (AS50881 for aerospace) and the tube bend tables of your fitting supplier.
- **Removal.** What must come off to replace the part that will wear or fail first? Design for the second assembly,
  too.

**Design rule.** Model the critical tools as solids in the assembly: the socket with its extension and wrench head, the
key, the borescope. It takes ten minutes and finds the problems that otherwise turn up on the shop floor.

## Worked example: the aft closure bolts

The aft closure has 30 M10 hex bolts on a 285 mm bolt circle ([GD&T](../../drawing/gdt/#worked-example-the-aft-closure-its-bolts-and-the-nozzle-seal-groove)),
tightened to 60 N·m ([Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/#worked-example-the-aft-closure)). For this example only: the
nearest wall is 22.5 mm from the bolt axes, there is 45 mm of straight approach above each hole, and the service range
is −40 to +60 °C. Select **Load worked example**.

1. **Socket.** A standard 16 mm socket is about 24.3 mm across. It needs 13.2 mm from the bolt axis to the wall
   (22.5 mm is available) and a pitch of 22.4 mm to clear the next head. The bolts are 29.8 mm apart: **the socket
   fits**. On this circle, at most 39 bolts can be tightened one at a time with this socket.
2. **Cap screws instead.** Socket-head cap screws in ⌀18 counterbores with an 8 mm bit socket of about 18 mm need a
   pitch of only 18.0 mm. If the joint later needs more bolts, this is the way to fit them.
3. **Wrench.** 60 N·m on a 250 mm torque wrench is a hand force of **240 N**. A 72-tooth ratchet needs only 5° of
   swing (22 mm at the hand), and 40° is free. An open spanner needs 60°: **it does not fit**.
4. **Insertion.** An M10 × 40 bolt needs 40 + 6.4 + 2 = **48.4 mm** of straight approach, and only 45 mm is available:
   **the bolt cannot go in**. Use an M10 × 35 (43.4 mm), fit studs in the case, or install the bolts before the part
   that blocks them.
5. **Heat.** An aluminium 7075 electronics box, 200 mm long, sits in a 4340 steel bracket with 0.08 mm of clearance at
   20 °C. At −40 °C the gap opens to 0.213 mm. At +60 °C it is **−0.009 mm: the box touches** and loads its fasteners.
   Increase the gap to at least 0.1 mm, or let the box slide on slotted holes.

## Where this stops

- **The socket is an estimate.** Real sockets, extensions and wrench heads differ by maker and drive size. Measure the
  tools that the shop will use and model them.
- **Straight lines only.** The tool checks one axis and one plane. A 3D model with the tools as solids finds the
  interference along the whole path.
- **No human-factors data.** The hand-force limit, reach and posture come from your human-factors standard
  (DEF STAN 00-250, MIL-STD-1472).
- **Free expansion.** The thermal gap assumes that both parts expand freely along one line, at a uniform temperature.

## On the drawing

- **Tool access** where it is not obvious: a note that the bolts are installed before a part, or a scallop for a socket.
- **The torque and the tool** where the access forces a special tool (a crow-foot changes the torque setting).
- **Assembly sequence** in the assembly drawing or the build instruction, when the order matters.
- **Lifting points** with their proof load, and the centre of gravity of heavy assemblies.
- **Clearances at the extremes,** not only at 20 °C, for parts that must not touch in service.
