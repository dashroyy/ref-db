## Why this matters

A fastener that becomes loose causes two types of damage. First, the joint loses its clamp: a closure leaks, or a
bracket rattles and fatigues. Second, the loose part becomes debris that can jam a mechanism or puncture a different
part. Motors and their hardware are shaken during transport, on the vehicle and by their own burn. NASA-STD-5020B
records that fasteners that became loose and came free have had an effect on previous missions.

There are two ways to lose preload:

- **Non-rotational:** embedding, relaxation, creep and temperature. The [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/) page gives
  these.
- **Rotational self-loosening:** the nut turns back. This page gives this mechanism.

## Why a tight nut turns back

<ThreadRamp />

While there is no slip, a tightened thread locks itself. The friction on the thread and under the head resists
rotation several times more than the ramp of the thread pushes the nut off. In 1969, Gerhard Junker showed that
**sideways (transverse) slip** of the joint stops this lock. Each time the clamped parts slide relative to each other,
the friction resists the slip. At that moment, only the ramp acts on the nut:

$$
T_\text{ramp} = \frac{F_i\,P}{2\pi}
$$

A very small rotation in each cycle is sufficient. At vibration frequencies of tens to hundreds of hertz, the rotation
adds up quickly. Axial vibration causes much less loosening than transverse vibration. Local slip at the thread or under
the head can start the process before the full joint slides.

<Analogy title="A book on a tilted board">
  A book is on a board with a small tilt, and friction holds it. Now move the board quickly from side to side. The book
  slips sideways each time. While it slips, the friction cannot also stop it from sliding down the tilt. Thus it moves
  down a little with each shake. The ramp of the thread is the tilt. The sideways slip of the joint is the shake.
</Analogy>

## The first lock is preload

If you stop the slip, the nut cannot become loose. The joint grips sideways with the interface friction times the clamp
force:

$$
F_{Q,\text{slip}} = \mu_T \; q \; \big(F_{i,min} - (1-\Phi)P\big) \;\ge\; k \, F_Q
$$

Here $F_{i,min}$ is the minimum preload (with scatter, losses and temperature from the [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/)
page). The share of the axial load, $(1-\Phi)P$, is subtracted. $q$ is the number of slip planes, and $k$ is the factor
of your programme on the transverse load $F_Q$. This is why NASA-STD-5020B calls preload the **primary locking
feature**. If the slip check fails, a locking feature only limits the damage. To prevent the slip:

- **Increase the clamp force:** more torque, a larger or stronger bolt, or more bolts.
- **Add friction or slip planes.** The surface condition is important. Coatings, lubricant and contamination on the
  faying surfaces decrease the grip.
- **Carry the shear with other parts:** dowels, shoulder bolts or a spigot. Then the bolts only clamp.

## What locking features actually do

<JunkerCurves />

| Feature | What it does | Without preload? | Verify by (NASA-STD-5020B Table 4) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Safety wire, cable, cotter pin | Positive: the part turns only until the wire is tight, then it cannot come off | Yes, mechanical | Visual inspection after installation |
| Adhesive thread locker, cured | Holds preload better than the other features, also in severe vibration | Yes | Torque test on witness samples |
| Prevailing torque: deformed thread, insert, patch | Keeps the fastener captive; does not hold preload after the joint slips | Yes | Running torque measured during installation |
| Serrated bearing face | Works well, but only while preload stays; can damage the bearing surface | No | (none) |
| Jam nut | Uses its own preload | No | (none) |
| Split or tooth lock washer | Becomes flat at a small fraction of preload: little or no locking | No | Do not use as a secondary lock |

On small fasteners, the running torque of a prevailing-torque feature is a fraction of a newton-metre. The
self-loosening moment of a slipping joint is several times larger. The standard gives $F_i P/2$ as an estimate (after
Hess, 2017), which is larger than the ramp torque $F_i P/2\pi$. Thus the feature cannot stop rotation. It stops the
fastener from falling out.

## Safety wire, cotter pins and staking

- **Safety wire and cable.** Install the wire so that its tension pulls each fastener in the **tightening** direction.
  NASM 33540 and SAE AS567 give the practice. On a bolt that turns in operation, connect the nut and bolt only to each
  other, not to the structure.
- **Cotter pins.** These go through a castellated nut and a drilled bolt. They are the usual choice for clevis bolts
  and pivot bolts.
- **Adhesive thread lockers.** These are anaerobic adhesives that cure between the threads. They work only if the
  process is correct: clean parts, primer where necessary, the correct gap, and a full cure. NASA-STD-5020B requires
  that the process is developed and validated first. Pre-applied coatings that dry without bonding the threads are not
  adhesives. Do not use them for critical joints.
- **Epoxy staking.** A fillet of epoxy connects the fastener head and the part. It is mostly used on small fasteners, up
  to approximately M4 or #8, and on electronics, where NASA-STD-8739.1 applies.

## Retaining rings

A retaining ring (circlip, spiral ring or lock ring) sits in a groove and holds a part against a shoulder. Small
rings retain bearings, pins and actuator parts. Large internal rings and segmented key rings can retain a closure
against the full pressure end load of a motor. A ring has no preload, so the questions are different from those for a
bolt:

- **The groove usually decides the capacity, not the ring.** The edge of the groove yields in bearing, or the land
  behind the groove shears out, before the ring fails. In an aluminium housing, groove-edge yield governs well before
  ring shear. Two first checks, with $D$ the groove diameter, $h$ the depth of the groove that the ring bears on and $L$
  the land from the groove to the end of the part:
  $$
  \sigma_\text{bearing} = \frac{F}{\pi D h}
  \qquad
  \tau_\text{land} = \frac{F}{\pi D L}
  $$
- **The abutting part must be square and sharp.** A chamfer or a radius on the part that pushes on the ring makes the
  ring dish (cone) and come out of its groove at a much lower load. The catalogue capacity of a ring usually assumes a
  sharp, square abutment. If the part must have a chamfer, use the reduced capacity that the ring maker gives for it.
- **Keep the groove edge sharp,** with a small radius in the root only. A large edge break is a ramp that the ring
  climbs.
- **Speed and vibration.** External rings can open at high rotation speed, and rings with a gap can walk round in
  vibration. Use a ring with a locking feature, or a full ring, where this can occur.
- **Assembly and inspection.** Make sure that the ring is fully seated: a ring that sits on the groove edge looks
  correct and holds almost nothing. Give an inspection method, for example a gauge or a visual check of the ring gap.
- **High thrust.** For a high axial load, a shoulder, a threaded retainer or a bolted ring is usually better. A
  retaining ring in a pressure closure needs the same analysis, proof test and inspection as a bolted joint (see
  [Load paths & joints](../load-paths/)).

## Requirements for spaceflight hardware

NASA-STD-5020B §4.6 gives the rules for NASA spaceflight hardware. Other programmes have equivalent rules:

- **At least one locking feature that does not depend on preload,** on each threaded fastening system, for all preloads
  (§4.6.1). Jam nuts and serrated faces possibly do not qualify, because they depend on preload. A redundant feature
  can be advisable for some joints (FAA AC 20-71).
- **A mechanical locking feature** (safety wire or a cotter pin) on each bolt that is an axis of rotation in operation
  (§4.6.2).
- **A validated process** for adhesive lockers and staking (§4.6.3).
- **Verification of presence and performance** (§4.6.4): inspection for mechanical features, running-torque measurement
  at installation for prevailing torque, and witness samples for adhesives.
- **Running torque and preload.** If the torque is not specified as "above running torque", subtract the maximum
  running torque from the minimum specified torque, and the minimum breakaway torque from the maximum specified torque,
  before you calculate the preload (Appendix A.2, Eqs. 27–30).
- **Thread engagement.** The fastener must be sufficiently long for fully formed threads to engage a prevailing-torque
  feature (§4.7.4).

## Worked example: the electronics unit on the aft skirt

The 4 kg unit from the [Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/#worked-example-a-4-kg-unit-on-an-aft-skirt-bracket)
page has 29.0 g at 3σ (1137 N) in its lateral test axis. Four M6 class 10.9 screws in prevailing-torque inserts hold
it. The maximum running torque is 0.4 N·m (an example, from the part standard of the insert). The screws are tightened
to 10 ± 0.5 N·m, not specified as above running torque, with a friction of 0.12. The preload scatter of ±25% and the
slip factor of 1.25 are **for this example only**. Push **Load worked example**.

1. **Load for each screw:** $1137/4 = 284$ N sideways, shared equally. This ignores the overturning moment of the unit.
2. **Effective torque:** subtract the running torque from the minimum. This gives 9.1–10.5 N·m.
3. **Preload:** 9.5 kN at mid-range torque, and 6.6 kN at the minimum torque with −25% scatter.
4. **Slip:** with an interface friction of 0.15, the joint grips $0.15 \times 6.6 = 1.0$ kN for each screw, against
   $1.25 \times 284 = 355$ N. **MS = +1.81**. The joint does not slip, so it does not self-loosen. The preload does the
   locking.
5. **The insert:** it does not depend on preload, so it meets §4.6.1. Measure the running torque at installation to
   verify it. But its 0.4 N·m is much less than the 1.5 N·m ramp torque (4.8 N·m with the estimate of the standard). It
   keeps the screws captive. It would not hold their preload if the joint slipped.

**Snug-tight at 2 N·m** is a frequent shortcut on a "light" bracket. It gives only 1.5 kN of minimum preload and a grip
of 219 N, which is less than the factored 355 N: **MS = −0.38**. The joint slips on each large cycle of the
qualification test. The screws turn back to the running torque of the inserts, and the unit rattles. This is a test
failure, but no screw falls out.

## Where this stops

- **Equally shared shear.** Overturning moments, prying and unequal bolt spacing put more load on some bolts than on
  others. Check the worst bolt.
- **3σ is not the largest peak.** Random vibration has occasional peaks above 3σ (see Random vibration). A slip margin
  at 3σ does not prevent rare micro-slip. This is one reason why the locking feature is still necessary.
- **Local slip** under the head or in the threads can start the loosening before the full joint slides. Bending and
  thermal cycling can also cause it.
- **Interface friction** changes with finish, coating, contamination and wear. Use measured or specified values.

## On the drawing

- **The locking feature, by specification:** insert or nut part number, adhesive and its cure, or safety wire to
  NASM 33540 with wire material and diameter.
- **The torque, and how to use the running torque:** "above running torque", or a measured running-torque range to
  subtract.
- **Verification:** running torque recorded at installation, witness samples for adhesive, and inspection of wire and
  pins.
- **Reuse limits.** Prevailing-torque features lose torque with each use. Tell if fasteners can be used again.
