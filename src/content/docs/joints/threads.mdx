## Why this matters

The thread is where the load of a bolt goes into the part. A thread failure is more dangerous than a bolt failure. A
broken bolt is easy to see, it is ductile, and you can replace it in minutes. Stripped threads in a tapped closure or
skirt can show nothing until the joint fails. To repair them, you must drill them out and install an insert, and
sometimes you must scrap the part. On a motor, this is important for tapped holes in aluminium skirts, brackets and
nozzle hardware, and in high-strength steel closures.

## Thread forms

| Form | Profile | Where it is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ISO metric, M | 60°, flat or rounded root | General engineering; coarse pitch by default, fine pitch specified as M10 × 1.25 |
| Unified, UNC / UNF | 60°, the same profile in inch sizes | US hardware and older aerospace parts |
| MJ and UNJ | 60°, with a large controlled root radius on the bolt | Aerospace fasteners: lower stress concentration at the root, thus better fatigue life |
| Trapezoidal, Acme | 30° or 29° | Power screws and actuators: loads that move, not clamping |
| Buttress | Asymmetric | Heavy loads in one direction |
| Pipe, tapered: NPT, BSPT (ISO 7, R) | Tapered | Seals on the flanks with a sealant; not a structural fastener thread |
| Pipe, parallel: BSPP (ISO 228, G) | 55°, parallel | Fittings that seal on a face, a bonded washer or an O-ring, not on the thread |
| British Standard Whitworth and Fine, BSW / BSF | 55°, rounded crests and roots | Older UK hardware and test equipment |
| British Association, BA | 47.5° | Small screws in older instruments and electrical parts |

**Old and foreign threads.** A 55° Whitworth or BSP thread can go a few turns into a 60° thread of a similar pitch and
then seize, or hold with a much lower strength. On older test equipment and imported fittings, identify each thread with
a thread gauge and a pitch gauge before you assemble it.

**Fine or coarse?** A fine pitch has a slightly larger stress area and finer adjustment. But its threads are shallower,
so in soft materials it strips and galls more easily, and it cross-threads more easily. Coarse threads are the usual
choice for tapped holes in aluminium.

**Do not mix forms.** NASA-STD-5020B §4.7.2 gives the incompatible pairs:
- fine with coarse, or inch with metric;
- the same pitch at different diameters;
- an external **MJ** thread in an **M** internal thread, or **UNJ** in **UN**.

These mismatches cause interference, seizure, low strength, or an incorrect preload for a given torque.

## Tolerance classes

ISO 965 gives each thread a **tolerance grade** (a number: smaller is tighter) and a **tolerance position** (a letter:
the distance of the zone from the basic size). Internal threads use capital letters, and external threads use lower
case letters:

- **6H / 6g:** the general-purpose fit. The "g" position leaves a small allowance below the basic size on the bolt.
- **4h6h / 4H5H:** the tighter classes used for MJ aerospace threads (ISO 5855).
- **Unified: 2A / 2B** for general use, and **3A / 3B** for tighter, aerospace work.

The plating must fit in that allowance. A coating of thickness $c$ on the 60° flanks increases the pitch diameter by
$4c$. Thus a plated thread made to "h", with no allowance, does not go into its nut.

## Rolled or cut

**Rolled** threads are formed, not machined. The grain flows around the thread form, and the root stays in
compression. Rolled threads are much better in fatigue than cut threads, especially when they are rolled **after** heat
treatment, so that the heat treatment does not relieve the compressive layer
([Residual stress](../../materials/residual-stress/)). Aerospace bolts are rolled. A thread cut on a lathe is a weaker
and rougher feature, and the root of a cut thread is a sharp notch for [fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/).

## Thread stripping

<ThreadProfile />

When threads strip, each set shears on a cylinder. In the form of FED-STD-H28 and VDI 2230, with the pitch $P$ and the
engagement length $L_e$:

$$
A_\text{int} = \frac{\pi L_e\, d_{min}}{P}\left[\frac{P}{2} + \tan 30^\circ (d_{min} - D_{2,max})\right]
$$

$$
A_\text{ext} = \frac{\pi L_e\, D_{1,max}}{P}\left[\frac{P}{2} + \tan 30^\circ (d_{2,min} - D_{1,max})\right]
$$

Here $d$ is the major diameter and $d_2$ the pitch diameter of the bolt, and $D_1$ is the minor diameter and $D_2$ the
pitch diameter of the nut, at their minimum-material limits. At the basic size, they become
$A_\text{int} = \tfrac{7}{8}\pi d L_e$ and $A_\text{ext} = \tfrac{3}{4}\pi D_1 L_e$. The internal threads fail when the
load gets to their shear ultimate × $A_\text{int}$, and the bolt threads fail at their shear ultimate × $A_\text{ext}$.
The bolt itself breaks at $R_m A_s$.

**Make the bolt the weak link.** NASA-STD-5020B §4.7.4 requires that a tapped part has sufficient complete threads
engaged, so that the bolt fails in tension before the threads strip. A broken bolt is easy to see, it is ductile, its
load moves to the adjacent bolts, and it is easy to repair.

- **In steel,** the internal and external threads have almost the same strength, and a standard nut height
  (approximately 0.8 d) is sufficient.
- **In aluminium,** the shear strength of the part is a fraction of that of the bolt. Thus the engagement must be
  longer, approximately 1.5–2 d in practice. The basic-size areas give a shorter length. The tolerances, dilation and
  thread bending use the difference.
- **The thread strength is not uniform.** The first engaged threads carry most of the load. Thus a very long engagement
  adds little strength. The additional length mostly gives protection against tolerance and wear.

<Analogy title="Tearing a strip of perforated stamps">
  Perforations connect a row of stamps. If you pull a short strip, it tears along the perforations. If you pull a long
  strip, the stamps tear first. The engaged threads are the perforations. Each additional thread adds a little strength,
  until the bolt, not the thread, is the weakest part.
</Analogy>

## Inserts and procured internal threads

- **Helical-coil inserts** (NASM 8846) are wire coils in an oversize tapped hole, approximately $d + 1.3P$. The threads
  of the part then shear at that larger diameter, so a given length is stronger. The coil also gives a hard thread that
  resists wear for repeated assembly, and you can use it to repair a stripped hole.
- **Key-locked inserts** (MIL-I-45914) are solid bushes held by keys. Heavy-duty versions resist pull-out better than
  coils of the same length.
- **Rate a procured nut or insert by its specification,** not by a stripping analysis, because they dilate under load.
  For an insert, use the lower of its internal-thread strength and its pull-out strength from the part
  (NASA-STD-5020B §4.4.1). Pull-out from non-metallic or sandwich parts needs a test.
- **A stainless insert in aluminium** needs a primer at the interface to prevent [galvanic corrosion](../../materials/galvanic/).

## Plating, galling and lubrication

- **Plating** increases the pitch diameter by four times its thickness (see above). On high-strength steels,
  electroplating needs a bake to relieve hydrogen embrittlement.
- **Galling** is the cold welding of the flanks. It occurs on stainless steel and titanium threads, especially when the
  two parts are of the same material. A lubricant, a different mating material, or silver plating on one part prevents
  it.
- **The lubricant sets the preload.** The thread friction $\mu_t$ uses a third of the torque. See the torque split on
  [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/).

## How threads feed the bolted-joint calculation

- **Stress area** $A_s$, from the pitch and minor diameters. The tensile strength of the bolt is $R_m A_s$.
- **Thread friction** and the pitch term in the [torque–preload relation](../bolted-joints/#torque-and-preload).
- **Engaged thread length,** which is part of the compliance of the bolt.
- **Prevailing torque,** if the internal thread has a locking feature. See
  [Locking and retention](../locking-and-retention/).

## Worked example: the bracket screws

The four M6 class 10.9 screws that hold the electronics unit on the aft skirt (from
[Locking and retention](../locking-and-retention/)) go into a 6061-T6 bracket, with a shear ultimate of approximately
186 MPa (use your allowable).

1. **The bolt** breaks at $1040 \times 20.1 = 20.9$ kN.
2. **Tapped 6 mm deep (1 d):** the threads of the bracket shear on $\tfrac78 \pi \times 6 \times 6 = 99$ mm², at
   $186 \times 99 = 18.4$ kN. **The threads strip first**: the strength ratio is 0.88.
3. **Tapped 9 mm deep (1.5 d):** 27.6 kN, with a ratio of 1.32. **The bolt breaks first**, at basic size. Equal strength
   occurs at 6.8 mm. The 1.5 d rule leaves space for tolerance, dilation and thread bending.
4. **A 6 mm helical-coil insert:** the bracket shears at ⌀7.3 mm, which gives 22.4 kN and a ratio of 1.07. The bolt
   breaks first in a shorter hole. You must also check the rating of the insert.
5. **In service:** the 9.5 kN preload loads the 9 mm tapped threads to approximately a third of their strength.

In a steel nut, equal strength occurs at only 2.9 mm. This is why a standard nut (4.8 mm for M6) never strips before
the bolt breaks.

## Where this stops

- **Basic-size areas are an upper limit.** For a design value, use the minimum-material diameters from the tolerance
  class. The full method of VDI 2230 adds nut dilation and thread bending.
- **Static strength only.** The root of the first engaged thread controls thread fatigue. See rolled threads, MJ/UNJ
  roots and the [fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/) method.
- **Temperature and creep.** Aluminium threads lose strength when hot. The long-term clamping of soft parts can relax.

## On the drawing

- **Thread designation and class,** for example M6 × 1 – 6H, MJ6 × 1 – 4H5H, or ¼-28 UNJF-3B.
- **Full-thread depth,** separate from the drill depth, for tapped holes, and the minimum engagement that the analysis
  assumed.
- **Inserts by specification and length** (NASM 8846, MIL-I-45914), with a primer for stainless steel in aluminium.
- **Plating with its thickness and the embrittlement relief,** and the thread lubricant.
- **Fastener length:** at least two pitches past a nut or an insert, and clear of the incomplete run-out threads
  (NASA-STD-5020B §4.7.4–4.7.5).
