## Why this matters

A motor is a pressure vessel and a structure at the same time. The pressure loads are very large: more than 700 kN on
each end of the 300 mm case on this site. But they balance inside the case. The loads that go out of the motor (thrust,
inertia and bending) are ten times smaller. They go through the stiffest path, which is not always the path that was
designed to carry them.

When you follow the load path (which part carries which load, from where to where), you find:

- **the joints that carry the pressure,** and the joints that never carry it;
- **the attachment that takes the thrust,** and the attachment that must be free;
- **the redundant path that resists the case** as it grows under pressure and temperature. This load is often larger
  than the thrust.

## Follow the pressure

Pressure pushes on all the inside surfaces of the case. On a closure, the end load is the pressure times the area
inside its seal:

$$
F_{end} = p\,\frac{\pi D^2}{4}
$$

The pressure pushes the forward dome forward with $p\,\pi D^2/4$ over the case bore. It pushes the aft closure and the
nozzle back with almost the same force. The case wall holds the two ends together in tension. This is the axial stress
that [case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/) uses. Almost all of the pressure load closes inside the case and never gets
to the vehicle.

The throat is different. There is no surface opposite to it, so the pressure pushes the forward dome more than the aft
end. The difference is the thrust:

$$
F = C_F\, p\, A_t
$$

Here $C_F$ is the thrust coefficient of the nozzle. It includes the pressure on the divergent cone. The ratio of end
load to thrust is $D^2 / (C_F\,d_t^2)$, which is 24 for the motor in the worked example.

<Analogy title="You cannot push a car from inside it">
  Sit in a car and push on the dashboard with all your force. The car does not move. Your hands push the car forward,
  your back pushes the seat back, and the two forces cancel inside the car. Pressure in a motor does the same to each
  wall. Only at a hole (the throat) is there nothing to push back. That unbalanced area gives the thrust.
</Analogy>

The same method gives the loads on the joints of the aft end:

- **The nozzle,** attached over a seal diameter $d_n$, has a push-off force of $p\,\pi d_n^2/4$, minus the thrust that
  it makes: $F_{noz} = p\,\pi d_n^2/4 - F$. This blow-off load goes into its attachment.
- **The aft closure,** sealed at $D_s$, carries $p\,\pi D_s^2/4 - F$ during firing. In a static pressure test it
  carries the full $p\,\pi D_s^2/4$, because there is no flow and thus no thrust to decrease it. Size its joint for the
  test as well as for firing.

## Through the skirts to the vehicle

The thrust accelerates the motor and all that it pushes. With thrust only, the acceleration is
$a = F / (m_{motor} + m_{ahead})$. The forward skirt passes on only the force that the mass ahead of it needs,
$m_{ahead}\,a$:

$$
F_{skirt} = F\,\frac{m_{ahead}}{m_{motor} + m_{ahead}}
$$

The remaining force accelerates the motor. The case and closures take their share through their walls, and the grain
takes its share through its bond, with a mean shear of $m_{grain}\,a / (\pi D L)$. Thus:

- **The skirt load increases during the burn.** As the propellant burns, the same thrust pushes less motor and more
  payload. The peak is near burnout, not at ignition.
- **The thrust compresses the case wall** between the aft end and the forward skirt. In a firing motor, this only
  decreases the pressure tension a little. In a motor without pressure, the case and skirts are thin shells in
  compression. Examples are an upper stage that carries the thrust of the stage below, and any motor during handling
  and transport. See [Shell buckling](../../verify/shell-buckling/).
- **The aft skirt** carries the motor on the ground, or connects it to an interstage, fins or steering actuators. One
  skirt is the thrust takeout. Design the other skirt with the knowledge that it is not.

Bending from flight loads and steering adds to the axial load. Thin shells are checked with the **running load**, which
is the force for each unit length of circumference:

$$
N = \frac{P}{\pi D} \pm \frac{4M}{\pi D^2}
$$

At the attach ring, the bolts share the same loads. For $n$ equally spaced bolts on a bolt circle $D_b$, with the joint
closed and its neutral axis at the centre, the bolt with the most load carries:

$$
F_{bolt} = \frac{4M}{n\,D_b} - \frac{P}{n}
$$

Here $P$ is the axial compression. Compression from thrust decreases the bolt loads. Thus the worst bolt tension often
occurs with bending and **no** thrust: during coast, handling, or before ignition. That bolt load is the external load
for [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/).

## Stiffness and load sharing

When two paths connect the same two points, they have the same displacement. Thus they share the load **by
stiffness**. For a bar or tube, $k = EA/L$:

$$
P_1 = P\,\frac{k_1}{k_1 + k_2}
$$

For paths in series, add the flexibilities. For paths in parallel, add the stiffnesses. Three results are frequent
causes of design errors:

- **Gaps decide which path takes the load first.** A path with clearance carries no load until the joint has moved
  through the clearance. In a shear joint with clearance holes, the first bolt that touches takes the load. In a long
  bolted or riveted lap, the end fasteners take the most load.
- **Capacities do not add.** In a bonded and bolted joint, the bond is much stiffer. It takes almost all the load, and
  it fails at a displacement that is too small for the bolts to carry load. The joint fails when its first path fails.
  The bolts then carry all the load alone. Thus they are a fail-safe, not additional strength.
- **Redundant paths resist each other.** If one path grows more than the other (from pressure growth, thermal expansion
  or a tolerance), a connection between them causes a load in the two paths, with no applied load:

$$
F = \delta\,\frac{k_1 k_2}{k_1 + k_2}
$$

A motor case grows axially under pressure by $\delta = p\,r\,L\,(1 - 2\nu)/(2E\,t)$, and it grows more radially. If you
attach it rigidly at the two skirts to a structure that does not grow, the attachments carry that growth. The usual
solution is to **fix one end and let the other end slide**. Use an axial slip joint with more travel than the growth,
or a flexure, which still carries the radial loads and shear loads.

Radial growth also loads the skirt. At MEOP, the radius of the 300 mm case grows by 0.68 mm. An integral skirt must
follow this growth at its root. If a steel skirt followed it fully, it would have the 0.45% hoop strain of the case,
approximately 930 MPa, with no pressure inside it. This stress decreases within a few times $\sqrt{rt} \approx 14$ mm of
the joint. In practice, the skirt also holds the case back locally, and the junction needs a shell analysis or a
finite-element analysis. Composite cases have several times more strain. Thus their skirts are bonded through a rubber
shear ply, which lets the case grow under the skirt.

<Analogy title="A four-legged table">
  A three-legged stool stands evenly on any floor. A four-legged table on an uneven floor moves until you put a shim
  under one leg. Then the stiffest and longest legs carry most of the weight. Redundant load paths are the same as the
  table. They share load by stiffness and fit, not by design intent. A small misfit can put more load on a leg than the
  weight does.
</Analogy>

## Joint types

| Joint | Where on a motor | How it carries load | Possible problems |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Integral or welded skirt (Y-joint) | Skirts on metal cases | Skirt machined, forged or welded on at the dome–cylinder junction | Discontinuity stress as the case grows and the skirt does not; weld quality at the junction |
| Bonded skirt with shear ply | Composite cases | Shear through a rubber ply that lets the case grow under the skirt | Peel at the ends; bond verification; the temperature range ([Differential expansion](../differential-expansion/)) |
| Bolted flange, axial bolts | Closures, nozzles, attach rings | Preloaded bolts in tension; flange contact takes compression | Separation, prying, flange rotation ([Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/)) |
| Radial bolts or pins in shear | Skirt to vehicle; closures of small motors | Fasteners through overlapping rings, in bearing and shear | Bearing, shear-out and net-section failure; hole clearance (the first fastener that touches) |
| Tang-and-clevis pinned joint | Segmented case joints | Pins in double shear through a tang and clevis | Joint rotation that opens the seal gap as the case expands (Challenger, 1986) |
| Lock ring or segmented key | Closures of metal cases | A ring in matching grooves, in shear and bearing | Stress concentration at the grooves; the ring rolls out; assembly clearance |
| Threaded closure | Small motors | The thread carries the full end load | Stripping ([Threads](../threads/)); galling; seal compression |
| Clamp band | Stage and payload interfaces | A wedge-section band squeezes two flanges together | Band preload; gapping under bending. Release devices are outside the scope of this site |

For shear joints, check the share $V$ of each fastener (the load divided among the fasteners, plus any bending) in
several ways against your allowables and factors:

- **Fastener shear:** $\tau = V / (m\,A)$, with $m$ shear planes (one in a lap, two in a clevis).
- **Bearing:** $\sigma_{br} = V / (d\,t)$ on the hole in each part. Usually the thinnest or softest part governs.
- **Shear-out and net section:** the part tears out to the edge, or fails across the row of holes. The edge distance
  and the pitch set these. Pinned lugs and clevises have their own first checks on
  [Hand calcs](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/#lugs-and-pins).

Also think about the sealing and the stiffness of each joint together. A joint that is sufficiently strong but turns
or opens under pressure is a leak path. See [Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/).

## Worked example: the 300 mm motor's load path

This is the 300 mm D6AC case at its MEOP of 10 MPa ([case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example)), with an
illustrative 50 mm throat and $C_F = 1.5$ from the grain page. The aft closure seals at 260 mm with 30 bolts
([Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/#worked-example-the-aft-closure)). The nozzle seals into it at an illustrative 110 mm.
The masses are from the [hardware page](../../motor/hardware/): 137.81 kg at ignition, 26.61 kg at burnout, and a 50 kg
payload on the forward skirt. The thrust stays at its MEOP value until burnout, which gives the maximum loads.

1. **Pressure.** The pressure pushes the forward dome forward with $10 \times \pi \times 150^2 = 707$ kN, and the aft end
   back with 677 kN. The 29.5 kN difference is the thrust. It is **24 times smaller** than the end loads, which close
   inside the case.
2. **Nozzle and closure.** The push-off force on the nozzle is $10 \times \pi \times 55^2 = 95.0$ kN, minus its thrust.
   This gives a **65.6 kN** blow-off load into its attachment. The aft closure carries 501 kN during firing (16.7 kN for
   each bolt), but 531 kN in a static pressure test (17.7 kN). The bolted-joints example correctly used the test value.
3. **Acceleration.** $29.5 / (137.81 + 50) = 157$ m/s², which is **16.0 g** at ignition. It increases to **39.2 g** at
   burnout.
4. **Into the payload.** The forward skirt passes on $29.5 \times 50 / 187.81 = 7.84$ kN at ignition and **19.2 kN** at
   burnout. The inertia of the motor takes the remaining force: 21.6 kN, then 10.2 kN. At ignition, the 17.4 kN of the
   grain is spread over its 0.94 m² bond, which gives a shear of 18.5 kPa. This is very small.
5. **Bending.** With 3 kN·m at the forward skirt (the flight load that the
   [shell-buckling](../../verify/shell-buckling/#worked-example-the-300-mm-case-unpressurised) example used), the
   running load of the skirt is $20.3 + 42.0 = 62.3$ N/mm in compression at burnout. This is 31 MPa in a 2 mm wall.
6. **Attach ring.** Sixteen bolts on a 280 mm circle each take $4M/(nD_b) = 2.68$ kN from the bending. The thrust
   decreases this: 2.19 kN at ignition and 1.48 kN at burnout. The worst case is bending **without** thrust, 2.68 kN.
   This is the external load for each bolt in [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/).
7. **A redundant attachment.** Now assume that the motor is also fixed at its aft skirt to an aluminium airframe tube
   (⌀330 × 2 mm, 1000 mm between the skirts). The axial stiffness of the case is
   $205\,000 \times \pi \times 301.4 \times 1.377 / 1000 = 267$ kN/mm, and the airframe stiffness is 149 kN/mm. In
   series against a mismatch, the stiffness is 95.5 kN/mm.
   - **At MEOP,** the case grows $10 \times 150 \times 1000 \times (1 - 0.58) / (2 \times 205\,000 \times 1.377) =
     1.12$ mm. This causes **107 kN** in the two attachments.
   - **At −40 °C,** 60 °C below the assembly temperature, the aluminium has also shrunk 0.68 mm more than the steel. The
     total is 1.80 mm, which gives **172 kN**. This is almost six times the thrust, and nine times the force that the
     forward skirt passes to the payload.
   - The aft attachment has a capacity of 120 kN, and the ultimate factor is 1.25. These two values are **for this
     example only**. Push **Load worked example** in the second calculator. The result is **MS = −0.44**.
   - **The solution:** let the aft skirt slide axially, with 2 mm of travel. The mismatch never closes the gap, and the
     force is zero. The forward skirt alone takes the thrust.

## Where this stops

- **Quasi-static, thrust only.** Drag, weight, steering side loads, the ignition transient, thrust oscillation and
  vibration ([Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/)) come from the loads analysis of the vehicle. Here the
  thrust stays at its MEOP value, which is an upper limit of a real thrust trace.
- **Beam theory and an elastic bolt ring.** Local load introduction, prying, flange contact and a joint that opens on
  the tension side need a detailed analysis.
- **Two paths.** Real structures have many paths, and you need a finite-element model to find their stiffnesses.
- **The nozzle attach plane is at chamber pressure.** The flow at the nozzle entrance decreases the pressure a little.
- **Elastic, room temperature.** Stiffness and capacity change across the temperature range. Joints with gaps also
  slip and fret.

## On the drawing

- **The interface:** hole pattern, bolt circle, datums and mating surfaces, from the interface control document of the
  vehicle. Also tell which interface is the thrust takeout.
- **The fixed end and the sliding end:** the axial travel of the slip joint, and the loads that it still carries.
- **Interface limit loads:** axial, shear and bending at each attachment, usually in the interface control document.
- **Fastener torques and preload** at the attach rings and closures. See [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/).
- **Bond and shear-ply details** for bonded skirts: adhesive, ply thickness, surface preparation, and the inspection
  that verifies the bond.
