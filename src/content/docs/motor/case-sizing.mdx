## Why this matters

The case is usually the heaviest inert part of a solid motor. Each kilogram of case is a kilogram of propellant that
the motor cannot carry. The **propellant mass fraction** (propellant mass divided by total motor mass) is the hardware
parameter with the largest effect on performance. Thus case sizing is a balance. The case must be light enough to give
performance, and strong enough to never fail, because there is no leak warning and there is an energetic payload
inside.

## The intuition: why cylinders split lengthwise

<Analogy title="A sausage in a hot pan">
  Overcooked sausages and frozen water pipes always split **along their length**, never around their circumference.
  This is not a weakness in the skin. It is geometry. The force that tries to open a lengthwise seam increases with the
  *diameter*. The force that tries to pull the end off increases with the end *area*, and the full circumference
  resists it. The calculation shows that the lengthwise seam has exactly two times the stress.
</Analogy>

<HoopVsAxial />

For a thin-walled cylinder with an inner radius $r$ and a wall $t$, at an internal pressure $p$:

$$
\sigma_h = \frac{p\,r}{t} \qquad\qquad \sigma_a = \frac{p\,r}{2t} = \frac{\sigma_h}{2}
$$

A **sphere** has no "long direction". All cuts are the same as cut B. Thus its wall stress is $p\,r/2t$ in all
directions. This is why a hemispherical dome needs only **half the wall** of the cylinder that it closes, and why the
sphere is the lightest possible pressure vessel. Domes are bubbles. Flat closures are plates in bending, and they are
heavy.

## The three pressures

A case is not designed to the operating pressure. It is checked against a set of pressures, which all refer to the
**maximum expected operating pressure (MEOP)**:

<PressureLadder />

- **MEOP** already includes the hot-day grain temperature, the manufacturing variation, the ignition spike and erosive
  burning. See [why pressure is amplified](../../fundamentals/how-a-solid-motor-works/#the-consequence-for-hardware-small-changes-are-amplified).
- **Proof** is applied to each case (or to a defined sample) to show no yield and no leak. It is also your best
  **flaw screen**. Any crack that is large enough to fail at proof fails on the test stand, not in service.
- **Design burst** is the minimum ultimate capability that the design must have. Analysis shows it, and tests to
  failure on qualification articles also show it.

This gives two sizing requirements, and the thicker wall applies:

$$
t_{\text{proof}}:\;\; \sigma_h(p_{\text{proof}}) \le \eta\,\sigma_y
\qquad\qquad
t_{\text{burst}}:\;\; \sigma_h(p_{\text{burst}}) \le \eta\,\sigma_{\text{UTS}}
$$

Here $\eta$ is the weld (joint) efficiency ([Welding](../../make/welding/#joint-efficiency-and-strength)).
High-strength case steels have a yield strength near the UTS, so burst
usually governs. For lower yield-to-tensile ratios, proof can govern.

## When "thin" stops being thin

The thin-wall formula assumes that the stress is uniform through the wall. It is not uniform. It is highest at the
bore. The Lamé thick-cylinder solution gives the exact hoop stress at the inner surface:

$$
\sigma_{h,\text{bore}} = p\,\frac{r_o^2 + r_i^2}{r_o^2 - r_i^2}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
t = r_i\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma + p}{\sigma - p}} - 1\right)
$$

Below approximately $t/r = 0.1$, the two formulas agree within a few per cent. Motor cases are usually much thinner
than that, but closures, bosses and high-pressure gas generators are not. The calculator below always uses Lamé, and
it also shows the thin-wall value, so that you can see the difference.

## Leak before break

A case can fail in two ways. In **leak before break**, a crack grows through the wall, releases gas, and somebody
notices it. In **break before leak**, a crack gets to its critical size while it is still below the surface, and the
case ruptures without warning. The simplest screen (Irwin) asks if a crack that has just gone through the wall would be
stable. Such a crack is approximately as long as the wall is thick on each side, with a half-length $c \approx t$:

$$
c_{\text{crit}} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_{IC}}{\sigma_h}\right)^2
\qquad\text{leak before break if}\qquad c_{\text{crit}} \ge t
$$

The full method is on [Leak before break](../../verify/leak-before-break/). It grows a surface flaw through the wall and
checks the through crack that it becomes, with bulging and plastic collapse.

<Analogy title="A balloon versus a football">
  If you make a small hole in a balloon, it bursts. The material has a high stress and little toughness, so the hole
  runs. If you make a small hole in a football, it only becomes flat. The hole stays a hole. You want your case to be
  like the football. Higher-strength steels let you make the wall thinner, but their toughness usually decreases as
  their strength increases. This moves you towards the balloon.
</Analogy>

This is only a screening check. Real fracture-control plans use solutions for surface cracks and embedded cracks,
measured toughness at the correct orientation, temperature and thickness, and a crack-growth analysis over the pressure
history of the motor.

## Worked example

This is a welded D6AC case: internal diameter 300 mm, cylinder length 1000 mm, MEOP 10 MPa (100 bar), proof factor
1.10, burst factor 1.25, and full-penetration welds with full radiography ($\eta = 0.90$). The material values from the
database are: UTS 1520 MPa, yield 1450 MPa, $K_{IC} \approx 70$ MPa√m and density 7.85 g/cm³. The factors are for this
example only. Push **Load worked example** in the calculator to load these inputs.

1. **Design pressures.** $p_{\text{proof}} = 1.10 \times 10 = 11.0$ MPa; $p_{\text{burst}} = 1.25 \times 10 = 12.5$ MPa.
2. **Allowables with weld efficiency.** $0.9 \times 1520 = 1368$ MPa ultimate; $0.9 \times 1450 = 1305$ MPa yield.
3. **Thin-wall thicknesses.** Burst: $t = 12.5 \times 150 / 1368 = 1.371$ mm. Proof:
   $t = 11.0 \times 150 / 1305 = 1.264$ mm. **Burst governs.**
4. **Lamé check.** $t = 150\left(\sqrt{1380.5/1355.5} - 1\right) = 1.377$ mm. This is within 0.5% of the thin-wall
   value, as expected for $t/r \approx 0.009$.
5. **Stress at MEOP.** $\sigma_h \approx 1094$ MPa hoop, and approximately 547 MPa axial.
6. **Leak before break.** $c_{\text{crit}} = \frac{1}{\pi}(70/1094)^2 = 1.30$ mm, which is **less than** the 1.38 mm
   wall. A through crack would not be stable. Thus this case would probably **burst without a leak first**.
7. **Mass.** The cylinder is approximately 10.2 kg of steel. The case holds approximately 85 litres (with hemispherical
   domes) at 10 MPa.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the example shows">
  The membrane sizing is easy, and the result is very thin. Relative to its size, this wall is thinner than an
  eggshell. The difficult part is step 6. At this strength level, the case does not leak before it breaks. Thus its
  safety depends on **NDT that reliably finds small flaws** and on the **proof test**, which screens all larger flaws.
  Load the calculator with these inputs, then try 15CDV6 or maraging 18Ni-250 to see how the trade changes.
</Aside>

## Where this tool stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Membrane stress is the easy part">
  The calculator sizes the **cylinder away from its ends**. Real cases fail at the discontinuities that it does not
  model.
</Aside>

- **Junctions between dome and cylinder.** A cylinder and a dome at the same pressure try to grow by different
  amounts. When they are forced to match, local bending occurs, which can increase the stress to much more than the
  membrane values. A 2:1 ellipsoidal head also has a **compressive hoop stress** in its knuckle, which can buckle thin
  heads.
- **Closures, bosses and joints.** Load paths concentrate at polar bosses, bolted or pinned closure joints, clevis
  joints and skirt attachments. These need their own analysis (usually FEA). See
  [Load paths & joints](../../joints/load-paths/).
- **External and flight loads.** Axial thrust, bending from flight loads, and external pressure or handling loads add
  to the pressure stresses. Buckling, not burst, can govern thin cases without pressure.
- **Temperature.** The allowables must be at the temperature of the case during and after the burn. This includes the
  heat that soaks back from the insulation after burnout.
- **Composite cases.** Filament-wound cases are sized by netting analysis on fibre strength and wind angle, not by
  isotropic membrane theory. A separate page (planned) will cover them.

## Defining it on the drawing

- **Give more than the alloy.** Give the alloy, the condition or temper, the product form, the applicable specification
  and the melt practice. *"D6AC bar per AMS 6431, VAR, heat treated to 1520 MPa min UTS, double temper"* is a
  specification. *"D6AC"* is not.
- **Special processes get their own notes.** Heat treatment (with pyrometry to AMS 2750), welding procedure, NDT method
  and acceptance class, coatings and cleanliness are each separate callouts with their own specification.
- **Fracture-critical parts have different rules.** When a part is classified as fracture-critical, the drawing gives
  the flaw-size and NDE requirements, and the material comes from a controlled quality class.
- **The proof test is a drawing or acceptance requirement,** with the pressure, hold time, medium, temperature and
  acceptance criteria (no leak, permanent set limits).
