## Why this matters

A case needs at least one opening: the grain has to go in, and the nozzle has to be attached. The joint that closes
that opening carries the pressure on the whole closure. For the 300 mm case at its MEOP of 10 MPa, this is 707 kN,
24 times the thrust ([Load paths](../../joints/load-paths/#follow-the-pressure)). It is the most heavily loaded joint of
the motor.

The closure joint also has three other jobs. It must **seal**, and keep sealing while the case grows. It is where the
case is **grooved, drilled or threaded**, which puts the highest stress concentration of the case in its most highly
loaded part. And it is often **assembled after the grain is cast**, next to the loaded propellant. A strong joint can
still fail at any of these.

## The intuition: a lid on a jar

<Analogy title="A lid on a jar of something that ferments">
  The thread of the lid holds it on against the pressure of the gas inside, and it rarely strips. What fails is the
  seal: the lid bulges, its rim lifts a little, and the gas escapes past the rubber ring. A motor closure is the same
  problem with a thousand times the pressure. The joint must be strong enough not to let go, and stiff enough that the
  seal does not lift.
</Analogy>

## The end load

The pressure acts on everything inside the seal:

$$
F = p\,\frac{\pi d_s^2}{4}
$$

where $d_s$ is the diameter of the seal that closes the joint. A seal on a smaller diameter carries less load: this
is one reason for flanged closures with face seals inboard, and for small polar openings. Size the joint at the
**proof pressure** (no yield) and at the **design burst pressure** (no rupture), as for the case, with the fitting
factor that your programme applies to joints. A pressure test has no thrust, so the closure carries the full load; in
firing, an aft closure that holds the nozzle carries a little less.

## Choosing the joint

<ClosureTypes />

| Joint | Carries the load by | Gives | Costs | Usual in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Integral dome | Nothing: there is no joint | The lightest and most reliable end | The grain must go in through the other end | The forward end of most case-bonded motors |
| Bolted flange | Preloaded bolts in tension | A seal on a smaller diameter, inboard of the bolts; easy to inspect and to take apart | Two thick flanges and many bolts; preload control | Aft closures and nozzle joints ([Bolted joints](../../joints/bolted-joints/)) |
| Lock ring (shear ring, segmented key, inserted wire) | A ring in shear in grooves of the case and the closure | No flange: the closure fits inside the bore; quick to assemble | A groove in the case wall; no preload, so the joint has axial play | Closures of metal cases |
| Radial pins or bolts | Pins in shear through the joint ring and a skirt of the closure | Simple machining, easy to inspect | Holes in the case wall; load sharing between pins; many parts | Small and medium motors, skirt attachments |
| Threaded closure | The thread in shear | The fewest parts | Galling, cross-threading and thread damage on a large diameter; the flanks push the case out | Small motors and test hardware |
| Tang and clevis with pins | Pins in double shear | Joins case segments in the field | Joint rotation that opens the seal gap | Large segmented motors |

**The best joint is no joint.** Most case-bonded motors have a dome at the forward end that is part of the case, and
one opening at the aft end. In a composite case, the openings are the polar bosses, and the joint is between the boss
and the closure or the nozzle ([Composite cases](../composite-cases/#the-parts-that-the-sizing-leaves-out)).

## Sizing the shear joints

Each check is a stress, from the load $F$ at proof or burst, against an allowable of the part.

**Lock ring,** in a case of bore $D$, with a ring of axial height $h$ in grooves $g_1$ deep in the case and $g_2$ in the
closure, and a land $L$ from the groove to the end of the case:

$$
\tau_\text{ring} = \frac{F}{\pi D h}
\qquad
\sigma_\text{bearing} = \frac{F}{\pi g_1 (D + g_1)}
\qquad
\tau_\text{land} = \frac{F}{\pi (D + 2g_1) L}
$$

and the same bearing and land checks on the closure side. The groove in the softer part usually decides the capacity,
not the ring. The ring has no preload. Thus the joint moves by its axial clearance when the pressure comes on, and the
seal must accept the movement.

**Radial pins,** $n$ pins of diameter $d$ in single shear, through a joint ring of wall $t$ with an edge distance $e$:

$$
\tau_\text{pin} = \frac{F}{n\,\pi d^2/4}
\qquad
\sigma_\text{bearing} = \frac{F}{n\,d\,t}
\qquad
\tau_\text{shear-out} = \frac{F}{2n\,(e - d/2)\,t}
$$

with the bearing allowable for the edge distance (MMPDS gives bearing values at $e/d$ = 1.5 and 2), and the same checks
on the closure skirt. These are the lug checks of [Hand calcs](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/#lugs-and-pins). All the
pins share the load only if they all touch together: drill the case and the skirt together.

**Threaded closure,** with the stripping areas of [Threads](../../joints/threads/#thread-stripping): the internal thread
of the case shears on approximately $\tfrac{7}{8}\pi d L_e$ and the external thread of the closure on
$\tfrac{3}{4}\pi D_1 L_e$. The 30° flanks also push the case outward with $F\tan 30°$. Over the engagement, this adds a
hoop stress $F\tan 30° / (2\pi L_e t)$ in the case at the thread. For a large closure, this can be larger than the hoop
stress from the gas.

**In each case, check the net section of the case** at the groove, the holes or the thread, with the axial stress of
the end load and the hoop stress together. A seal on the pressure side of the joint keeps the gas away from this
section; the calculator still includes the gas pressure there, which is conservative.

## The joint ring

The case membrane of the 300 mm example is 1.4 mm thick. No groove, hole or thread fits in that. Thus the case gets
thick at its end: a **joint ring**, forged and welded to the cylinder, or machined in one piece with it.

- **The seal often sets its thickness, not the strength.** A bore seal in a thin wall opens as the case grows. The
  [sealing-strategy example](../../seals/sealing-strategy/#worked-example-the-aft-closure-seal) needs a 16.5 mm joint
  ring to keep a 5.33 mm O-ring squeezed at MEOP. The same ring then carries the lock-ring groove with a very large
  margin.
- **The taper from the membrane** to the joint ring is a discontinuity. It adds bending as the thin wall grows more than
  the thick ring. Make the taper gradual, and analyse it.
- **The groove root, the hole and the thread root** are the highest stress concentrations of the case. Give them
  generous radii and a good surface. Check fatigue and fracture there: for a high-strength steel case, the critical
  crack is small ([Leak before break](../../verify/leak-before-break/)), so these features need surface NDT (magnetic
  particle or eddy current) after machining and after the proof test.
- **Stiffness for the seal:** keep the closure stiff, so that it does not dish and pull the seal away from the case.

## Assembly after casting

For a case-bonded grain, the aft closure is usually installed after the grain is cast and cured. This changes the
design:

- **O-rings must go in without damage.** Use lead-in chamfers of 15° to 20° with no sharp edges, and keep the rings
  away from threads, holes and grooves as they pass. Use a lubricant that is compatible with the O-ring and the
  propellant ([Propellant](../propellant/#what-may-touch-it-materials-compatibility)).
- **A lock ring must be fully seated,** and the inspector must be able to see it. A segmented ring goes in through a
  window, and an inserted wire goes in through a tangential slot. Give a gauge or a visual check for the seating.
- **Pins need aligned holes.** Match-drill the case and the closure skirt, and mark their position.
- **Threads need protection** during the cast, and an anti-galling coating or a dry-film lubricant. A large thread
  that is started at an angle can gall and seize part of the way in.
- **Leak-check the joint** between its two seals before the motor leaves the factory
  ([Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/#leak-check-ports)).

## Segmented motors: the field joint

Large motors are cast in segments and joined at the launch site. The segments join with a **tang and clevis**: the tang
of one segment fits into the clevis of the next, and a ring of pins in double shear carries the end load. The joint is
strong. Its problem is stiffness: under pressure the case wall bulges between the joints, the joint rotates, and the
gap at the seals opens.

On the Space Shuttle in January 1986, a field joint of a solid rocket booster opened in this way at ignition. The
O-rings were cold, so they did not follow the opening gap, and hot gas went past them. The Rogers Commission found
that the cause was the design of the joint, which was too sensitive to temperature and to other factors. The
redesigned joint added a **capture feature** that limits the
rotation, a **third O-ring**, and **heaters** that keep the joint warm. The general lesson applies to every closure:
**a joint must be checked for how much it opens, not only for how much it carries.**

## Worked example: a lock-ring aft closure for the 300 mm case

The 300 mm D6AC case has a 16.5 mm joint ring at its aft end, from the
[sealing-strategy example](../../seals/sealing-strategy/#worked-example-the-aft-closure-seal). The closure is a
7075-T73 aluminium plug that seals on the 300 mm bore. It is held by a 17-4PH (H1025) lock ring, 5 mm high, in
grooves 2.5 mm deep in the case and in the closure. The case land is 12 mm and the closure land 20 mm. MEOP is 10 MPa.
The factors (proof 1.10, burst 1.25, fitting 1.15) are **for this example only**. Push **Load worked example**.

1. **End load.** $10 \times \pi \times 150^2 = 707$ kN at MEOP. With the fitting factor, this is 894 kN at proof and
   1016 kN at design burst.
2. **Ring shear.** $707\,000 / (\pi \times 300 \times 5) = 150$ MPa at MEOP. At burst, 216 MPa against
   $0.6 \times 1070 = 642$ MPa: **MS = +1.98**.
3. **Groove bearing in the aluminium closure.** The bearing area is $\pi \times 2.5 \times 297.5 = 2337$ mm², so the
   stress is 302 MPa at MEOP. At proof, 383 MPa against the 435 MPa yield: **MS = +0.14**. At burst, 435 MPa against
   505 MPa: MS = +0.16. **This check governs.**
4. **Groove bearing in the steel case:** 298 MPa at MEOP, MS = +2.55 at burst.
5. **Lands:** 62 MPa in the case land and 38 MPa in the closure land at MEOP. Both have large margins.
6. **Net section of the case at the groove:** 94 MPa (von Mises) at MEOP, MS = +10.2 at burst. The joint ring is
   thick because of the seal, so its strength is far from critical.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the example shows">
  The groove in the aluminium closure governs, not the ring and not the case: as on the
  [Retaining rings](../../joints/locking-and-retention/#retaining-rings) page, the softer part decides. Change the closure
  to steel and the ring shear governs, at MS = +1.98. Change to the **Radial pins** tab: 24 pins of ⌀10 mm in 4340
  through the joint ring and a 10 mm aluminium skirt give MS = +0.42 in pin shear, with the skirt bearing at +0.98. The
  bolted flange of the [bolted-joints example](../../joints/bolted-joints/#worked-example-the-aft-closure) seals on a
  260 mm diameter, so it carries 25% less load, but it needs 30 preloaded bolts and two flanges.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Hand checks of the load path">
  These checks size the parts that carry the end load. They do not show that the joint stays closed, or how long it
  lives.
</Aside>

- **Stress concentration, fatigue and fracture** at the groove root, the holes and the thread root.
- **Load sharing.** The first pin to touch and the first threads carry more than their share. The calculator divides
  the load evenly.
- **Ring rolling and pin bending.** A ring with chamfered or rounded faces rolls out of its groove at a lower load; a
  pin with a gap between the parts bends.
- **The closure itself.** The plate or plug of the closure bends under the pressure. Its stiffness sets how far the
  seal opens. Analyse it as a plate, or with FEA.
- **Joint opening.** Use [Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/) for the gap at the seal, and FEA for the
  rotation of the joint.
- **Temperature.** An aluminium closure in a steel case shrinks more when cold, which changes the fit and the seal.
  See [Differential expansion](../../joints/differential-expansion/).

## On the drawing

- **The joint ring:** the material, the forging and the weld to the cylinder, the taper, and the radii at the groove
  roots, holes and thread roots, with their surface finish.
- **The groove, hole or thread** as critical features, with their tolerances and the inspection of each.
- **The lock ring:** material, condition and the gap of a split ring; the seating check.
- **Pins:** material, fit class and the match-drilling instruction.
- **Threads:** the thread form and class, the coating or lubricant, and the assembly torque (if any).
- **NDT of the joint ring** after machining and after proof, and the proof test that the joint sees.
- **The seals and the leak check** of the joint ([O-ring design](../../seals/o-ring-design/)).
