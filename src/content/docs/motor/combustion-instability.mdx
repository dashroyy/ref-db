## Why this matters

In an unstable motor, the chamber pressure oscillates at one of the acoustic frequencies of the chamber. The
oscillation can grow to a large fraction of the mean pressure in a fraction of a second. For the hardware:

- **Vibration.** Kilohertz oscillations shake the motor and everything on it: guidance, electronics, the nozzle and its
  steering. The levels can be far above the vibration specification ([Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/)).
- **A rise in the mean pressure.** A large oscillation usually raises the mean pressure too (a "DC shift"). The case
  then sees more than the stable prediction, so the margin to MEOP shrinks or disappears
  ([Propellant](../propellant/#building-meop)).
- **Heat and erosion.** The oscillating flow thins the boundary layer, so the insulation and the nozzle get more heat
  and erode faster.
- **Thrust oscillation,** which goes into the vehicle structure.

Instability is also hard to find late. It can appear in some firings and not others, at one temperature and not
another, or only after the motor is disturbed. Thus it is part of the grain design, from the start.

## The intuition: feedback howl

<Analogy title="A microphone too close to its loudspeaker">
  Put a microphone near the loudspeaker that it feeds, and a loud howl starts. The room picks the note: it is one of
  the frequencies at which the room resonates. The amplifier supplies the energy. The howl grows until the amplifier
  can give no more. To stop it, you can move the microphone, turn the gain down at that frequency, or put soft material
  in the room to absorb the sound. In a motor, the chamber is the room, the burning surface is the microphone and the
  amplifier, and the particles in the gas are the soft material.
</Analogy>

## The modes of a motor

The chamber is a cavity full of hot gas with a speed of sound $a$ of about 1000 to 1200 m/s. It has two families of
modes:

$$
f_{nL} = \frac{n\,a}{2L}
\qquad\qquad
f_T = \frac{\alpha_{mn}\,a}{\pi D}
$$

- **Longitudinal modes** run along the chamber, of acoustic length $L$. For long motors they are tens to hundreds of
  hertz, and they hardly change during the burn.
- **Transverse modes** run across the port, of diameter $D$: tangential modes around it and radial modes in and out.
  $\alpha_{mn}$ is a root of the derivative of a Bessel function: 1.84 for the first tangential mode (1T), 3.05 for 2T
  and 3.83 for the first radial mode (1R). For a small port they are kilohertz, and **they fall as the port burns out.**

<AcousticModes />

Thus a mode can sweep into the range where the propellant responds most, part of the way through the burn. Many
instabilities start at a definite time in the burn, for this reason.

## What drives the oscillation, and what damps it

**Rayleigh's criterion:** a heat release that rises and falls in phase with the pressure adds energy to the wave. In a
motor, two couplings do this:

- **Pressure coupling.** The burn rate responds to the pressure oscillation. The response function
  $R_p = (r'/\bar r)/(p'/\bar p)$ depends on the frequency, with a peak that is usually in the hundreds of hertz to
  kilohertz range. It is measured in a T-burner.
- **Velocity coupling.** The oscillating gas flow along the burning surface changes the burn rate, as erosive burning
  does.

The losses work against them:

- **The nozzle** carries acoustic energy out with the flow. This is the main loss for longitudinal modes.
- **Particles** in the gas lag the gas motion, and the drag and the heat exchange take energy from the wave.
- **Flow turning:** the gas from the burning surface leaves it at right angles and must be accelerated by the wave.
- **The structure and the viscous boundary layers** take a little more.

The linear growth rate of a mode is the sum: $\alpha = \alpha_{\text{driving}} - \alpha_{\text{losses}}$. If it is
positive, the oscillation grows exponentially until non-linear effects limit it, at a **limit cycle** that can be a
large fraction of the mean pressure. A motor that is stable to small waves can still be **triggered** by a large
disturbance, for example a piece of insulation that goes out through the nozzle. This is why stable designs are also
tested with deliberate pulses.

## Why double-base motors are prone to it

Most composite propellants have aluminium. It burns to droplets of aluminium oxide, a mass of particles in the gas
comparable to a large fraction of the gas itself. Particles damp a wave best when their size suits its frequency:

$$
d_\text{best} = \sqrt{\frac{18\,\mu}{\rho_p\,\omega}}
$$

where $\mu$ is the gas viscosity, $\rho_p$ the particle density and $\omega = 2\pi f$. Small particles damp high
frequencies, and large ones low frequencies. The oxide in a metallised propellant has a wide range of sizes, so it damps
most of the kilohertz transverse modes.

**Double-base, CMDB without aluminium and other smokeless propellants have almost no particles.** The same transverse
modes then have little damping, and small tactical motors with these propellants have a long history of
high-frequency instability. The low smoke that these propellants are chosen for is the reason
([Propellant](../propellant/#double-base-propellants-forms-and-properties)).

## Vortex-driven oscillations in large motors

Large motors have a different problem. Gas flowing past a step, an inhibitor ring or a slot sheds vortices. When the
shedding frequency matches a longitudinal mode, the vortices hitting the nozzle drive it. The Space Shuttle boosters
and the Ariane 5 boosters both had low-frequency pressure and thrust oscillations of this kind. Aluminium does not cure
it, because the frequencies are low. The cures are in the geometry: the size, shape and stiffness of the inhibitors,
and the shape of the steps in the port.

## Mitigation

- **Break the symmetry of the port.** A tangential mode needs a round, symmetrical port. Radial slots, star points or
  slots at **uneven angles and of uneven length**, and slots at different positions along the grain give it no clean
  shape to settle in. They also add losses where the gas turns, and they move the frequencies. Do it in the part of the
  burn where the port is the size that puts a mode in the response band.
- **Spend less time in the band.** Shape the burn so that the port passes quickly through the dangerous sizes.
- **Rods or baffles in the port** (often called resonance rods) break up the transverse waves mechanically.
- **Particles.** The propellant designer can change the response of the propellant or add a small amount of particles
  for damping, at a cost in smoke and performance. Formulation is outside the scope of this site.
- **The nozzle and the cavities.** The ratio of the port to the throat area, and a submerged nozzle and its cavity,
  change the damping of the longitudinal modes.
- **The operating point.** The response of a propellant changes with the pressure and the temperature. A change of
  MEOP or of the firing temperature range can move a motor into or out of trouble.

Each geometric fix also changes the ballistics and the strain in the grain. Slot tips and star points concentrate
strain ([Propellant grain](../propellant-grain/#ignition-the-case-stretches-the-bore-stretches-more)): repeat the
burn-back and the structural screen after every change.

## How a design is shown to be stable

- **Propellant response:** T-burner tests give the response function at the operating pressures.
- **Analysis:** for each mode and each time in the burn, an acoustic energy balance adds the driving and the losses.
  Standard stability prediction programs do this. A positive growth rate is a finding to fix, not to argue with.
- **Firings:** sub-scale and full-scale firings with flush-mounted, high-frequency pressure transducers sampled at tens
  of kilohertz. The grease-filled ports used for performance cannot follow kilohertz oscillations
  ([Static test firing](../../verify/static-firing/#what-a-firing-measures)).
- **Pulsed firings:** deliberate disturbances during the burn show whether an oscillation dies out. Fire at the hot and
  the cold temperature limits too.

## Worked example: a smokeless version of the 300 mm motor

Take the 300 mm motor with a smokeless propellant in a tube grain: a 100 mm port that burns out to 300 mm, a chamber
of about 1.2 m acoustic length, and a speed of sound of 1100 m/s. Assume that T-burner tests show the strongest
response between 1.5 and 3.5 kHz (an illustrative band). Push **Load worked example**.

1. **Longitudinal modes.** $1100 / (2 \times 1.2) = 458$ Hz for 1L, then 917 Hz and 1375 Hz. All are below the band.
2. **First tangential.** $1.84 \times 1100 / (\pi \times 0.100) = 6.45$ kHz at ignition, falling to 2.15 kHz at
   burnout. It enters the band at 3.5 kHz when the port is 184 mm, which is **42% of the way through the web**, and it
   stays in the band until burnout.
3. **Higher transverse modes.** 2T falls from 10.7 to 3.56 kHz and just misses the band. 1R (13.4 to 4.47 kHz) and 3T
   stay above it.
4. **Particle damping.** The 1T mode in the band would be damped best by particles of 5.0 µm (at 3.5 kHz) to 6.3 µm
   (at 2.15 kHz), and the 1L mode by 14 µm particles. A smokeless propellant has almost none. The composite propellant
   of the other pages, with its oxide particles, would damp these modes strongly.
5. **What to do.** In a tube grain the port is round for the whole burn, so the 1T mode has a clean shape when it
   enters the band. The options: keep the port non-round in the second half of the burn (slots or fins at uneven angles
   in part of the grain), add rods or baffles in the port, or ask the propellant designer to move the response band or
   add damping. Then show with a stability analysis and pulsed firings that the damping wins.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the example shows">
  The danger is not at ignition, where the transverse modes are far above the band, but in the second half of the
  burn, when the port has grown. A screen of the modes against the response band, across the whole burn, finds this in
  minutes. The full stability analysis and the firings then decide it.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="A screen, not a stability prediction">
  The calculator finds the modes that can be driven. It does not calculate the driving or the losses, so it cannot say
  whether a mode will grow.
</Aside>

- **Real geometry.** Ports that are not round, a chamber with domes, slots, a submerged nozzle and the mean flow all
  shift the frequencies. Use an acoustic finite-element model of the cavity at several times in the burn.
- **The energy balance.** Pressure and velocity coupling, nozzle damping, particle damping and flow turning need the
  response data of the propellant and a stability analysis.
- **Non-linear behaviour.** The limit cycle, the DC shift and triggering need non-linear analysis and pulsed firings.
- **Particle damping** is for one particle size in this tool. Real oxide has a distribution of sizes, which changes
  along the motor.
- **Vortex shedding** needs a flow analysis of the port and of the inhibitors.

## In the specifications

- **A stability requirement:** the largest pressure oscillation allowed, usually as a fraction of the mean pressure,
  with the frequency range and the instrumentation that measures it.
- **The pulse test:** when, how large and how many, and the decay that passes.
- **The propellant response data** to be supplied, at the pressures and temperatures of the motor.
- **The grain features that control stability** (slot angles and sizes, star points, rods) as critical
  characteristics on the drawing.
