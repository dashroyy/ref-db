## Why this matters

The [internal insulation](../insulation/) protects the case from the combustion gas. The outside of the case also
heats. In flight, the air that the boundary layer brings to rest heats up by friction and compression. At Mach 3 and
10 km the air is at −50 °C, but a wall that loses no heat would reach about 300 °C. The heat flux into a cooler wall is
of the order of 100 kW/m².

- **A composite case** has a resin that softens at approximately 120 to 200 °C ([Composite cases](../composite-cases/#temperature-moisture-and-the-resin)).
  A bare case of 1.2 mm gets to that temperature in seconds.
- **A metal case** carries the pressure while it heats, and its strength falls with its temperature.
- **The bond lines** (of the external insulation to the case, and of the case to the internal insulation) and the
  grain next to the wall have their own limits.

Thus the outside of the case is designed with a flight: how fast, how high, for how long.

## The intuition: a bicycle pump

<Analogy title="The end of a bicycle pump gets hot">
  Pump a tyre hard and the end of the pump gets warm. Squeezing air heats it. A body flying at Mach 3 squeezes and
  shears the air next to its surface in the same way: the boundary layer brings the air to rest against the skin, and
  its kinetic energy becomes heat. The skin cannot be colder than that air for long, unless something stops the heat
  getting in.
</Analogy>

## The recovery temperature

A wall that loses no heat reaches the **recovery temperature**:

$$
T_r = T_\infty\left(1 + r\,\frac{\gamma - 1}{2}\,M^2\right)
$$

$T_\infty$ is the temperature of the air, $M$ the Mach number, and $r$ the recovery factor: approximately
$Pr^{1/3} = 0.89$ for a turbulent boundary layer and $Pr^{1/2} = 0.84$ for a laminar one. It is a little below the
stagnation temperature ($r = 1$). At 10 km, where the air is at −50 °C, the recovery temperature is 109 °C at Mach 2,
308 °C at Mach 3, 587 °C at Mach 4 and 945 °C at Mach 5. It rises with the square of the speed.

## How much heat flows in

The heat flux into a wall at $T_w$ is $q = h\,(T_r - T_w)$. On the body of a vehicle, the flat-plate correlations give
$h$ at a distance $x$ from the nose. **Eckert's reference temperature** makes them work at high speed: evaluate the
density and viscosity of the air at

$$
T^* = T_\infty + 0.5\,(T_w - T_\infty) + 0.22\,(T_r - T_\infty)
$$

and use $St = 0.0296\,Re_x^{*\,-0.2}\,Pr^{-2/3}$ for a turbulent boundary layer (or $0.332\,Re_x^{*\,-0.5}\,Pr^{-2/3}$
for a laminar one), with $h = St\,\rho^* u\,c_p$.

- **Turbulent heating is several times laminar heating.** Boundary layers on vehicle bodies are usually turbulent
  away from the nose. Assume turbulent unless you have evidence.
- **$h$ falls slowly along the body,** as $x^{-0.2}$. The front of the motor heats a little more than the back.
- **Pressure matters.** At the same Mach number, $h$ is lower at altitude, where the air is thin.
- **Some places heat far more:** the nose and the leading edges of fins (stagnation-point heating), and places where a
  shock hits the body. These need their own analysis.

## How fast the case heats

For a thin wall, the time constant is $\tau = \rho c_p t / h$ ([Transient thermal](../transient-thermal/#lumped-capacitance-one-temperature)).
With $h$ = 469 W/m²K (Mach 2.5 at 6 km, 1.5 m from the nose):

- **The 1.38 mm D6AC case:** $\tau = 7850 \times 480 \times 0.00138 / 469 = 11$ s. Its conductivity is high, so it
  heats almost uniformly.
- **The 1.20 mm carbon/epoxy case:** $\tau = 1570 \times 1000 \times 0.0012 / 469 = 4$ s. The conductivity through its
  thickness is low (a Biot number of 0.7), so its outer surface heats faster still.

A flight lasts tens of seconds, so both cases get close to the recovery temperature. The steel can take it, with the
right allowables. The resin cannot.

## Limits of the case and its bonds

- **Composite resin:** the case allowables fall well before the glass transition, and absorbed moisture lowers it.
  Use the hot and wet allowables of your laminate, below its glass transition with a margin.
- **The bond of the external insulation** is at the hot face of the case, so its adhesive needs the same limit, or a
  higher one.
- **Metal cases:** use the allowables at the temperature of the case while it carries pressure
  ([Allowables](../../materials/allowables/)). The heat also makes the case grow, which matters for the joints.
- **The grain** next to the case wall has its own temperature limits; most of the heat is taken by the case and the
  internal insulation first.

## External thermal protection

A few millimetres of a material with a low conductivity and a low density take most of the temperature drop.

| Material class | Examples | Gives | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cork composites | Cork phenolic sheet, cork in silicone | Low conductivity and density; easy to bond and form; chars and swells rather than spalling | Moisture; bond to the case; a char layer at high heat flux |
| Filled silicones | Silica-filled silicone sheet or coating | Flexible over a wide temperature range; makes a silica char | Higher density; needs special primers to bond |
| Low-density ablators | Sprayed or gunned epoxy-novolac or silicone systems | Fills complex shapes; good at high heat flux | Process control; thickness inspection |
| Intumescent coatings | Paints that swell when heated | Thin; mostly for fire | Little help at the heat fluxes of fast flight |

- **Size it for the whole flight**, from the hottest starting temperature, and to the case limit at its peak, which
  can come after the motor burns out.
- **Include the bond:** surface preparation, primer, adhesive and cure. A disbond lets the hot surface reach the case.
- **Rain and particles** erode the surface in flight, and handling damages it on the ground. A finish coat protects
  it.
- **The mass is small:** a few millimetres of cork is under 1 kg/m², much less than what a composite case saves.

## Other external heat

- **The base and the plume.** At the aft end, the hot particles in the plume radiate onto the nozzle, the skirt and
  any steering actuators, and hot gas can flow back into the base region, more so at altitude where the plume
  expands. Aft skirts and actuators often carry their own insulation or shields.
- **On the ground.** A motor in the sun runs much hotter than the air: MIL-HDBK-310 gives a hot-dry design day with
  air at about 49 °C and sunshine of about 1120 W/m². A light-coloured finish with a low solar absorptance helps. The
  grain temperature follows the daily cycle ([Transient thermal](../transient-thermal/#1-a-thin-case-follows-the-weather)),
  and it sets the hot-day MEOP.
- **In a fire.** External insulation slows the heating of the case in a fuel fire, which changes the response of the
  motor to fast cook-off ([Designing the case to fail gently](../hardware/#designing-the-case-to-fail-gently)).

## Worked example: the 300 mm motor as a booster

The 300 mm motor is the booster of a tactical vehicle. It burns for 9 s, at an average of Mach 2.5 at 6 km, and the
vehicle then coasts for 20 s at Mach 3 and 12 km. The motor case starts 1.5 m behind the nose, and the boundary layer
is turbulent. The case starts at 20 °C, and its inner face is taken as adiabatic. Push **Load worked example**.

1. **Heating.** In the burn, $h$ = 469 W/m²K and $T_r$ = 254 °C: 110 kW/m² into a 20 °C wall. In the coast, the air is
   thinner: 252 W/m²K, but $T_r$ = 291 °C.
2. **The bare D6AC case** (1.377 mm) is at 150 °C at burnout and 238 °C at the end of the coast. That is within its
   service limit, but the case carries pressure at up to 150 °C: use its allowables at that temperature.
3. **The bare carbon/epoxy case** (1.196 mm) passes its 120 °C limit after **1.7 s** and reaches 285 °C. It cannot fly
   without protection.
4. **Cork phenolic:** 1.62 mm keeps the case at 120 °C. It is 0.76 kg/m², or 0.72 kg on the 300 × 1000 mm cylinder.
   From a hot-day start at 50 °C it needs 1.96 mm. A cork/silicone ablator does it with 1.65 mm and 0.43 kg/m².
5. **With 2 mm of cork phenolic,** the case is at 35 °C at burnout, and reaches 98 °C at the end of the coast. The cork
   surface gets to 258 °C.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the example shows">
  The wound case saves 9.8 kg on this motor ([Composite cases](../composite-cases/#worked-example)). A bare wound
  case would not survive its first two seconds of flight. Less than 1 kg of cork keeps it below its limit for the whole
  flight. The protection is part of the composite case, not an extra: size them together. The case peaks at the end
  of the coast, after the motor has burned out, so the flight after burnout belongs in the analysis too.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Flat-plate heating, conduction only">
  The calculator gives a first size. The design needs the heating along the real trajectory and shape, and the
  thermal response of the insulation as it chars.
</Aside>

- **The real trajectory and shape:** heating changes continuously with the Mach number and the altitude. The nose,
  the fin leading edges, protuberances and shock interactions heat far more than the flat-plate value.
- **Transition** from laminar to turbulent flow is not predicted. The tool uses what you choose.
- **Charring and ablation.** At high heat flux the insulation chars, gives off gas and recedes. The conduction model
  includes none of this.
- **Real gas and radiation.** Air is taken as a perfect gas. Radiation from the hot surface, which cools it, and
  radiation from the plume, which heats the aft end, are not included.
- **The inner face** is adiabatic. Real cases lose a little heat inward, and gain heat from the combustion gas late in
  the burn, through the internal insulation.

## On the drawing

- **The external insulation:** material specification, thickness and tolerance, and the zones where it goes (the
  cylinder, the domes, the skirts, around the joints).
- **The bond:** surface preparation of the case, primer, adhesive and cure, and the inspection of the bond.
- **The finish:** a protective coat and its colour or solar absorptance.
- **Repair:** the damage that is acceptable, and the repair method.
- **The temperature limits** of the case and the adhesive, in the thermal requirements of the motor.
