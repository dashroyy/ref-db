## Why this matters

A solid motor has few parts: a case with two closures, insulation, the propellant grain, a nozzle, an igniter, and the
joints and seals between them. When a motor fails, the cause is usually one of these hardware parts, or the interface
between two of them. It is usually not the propellant chemistry. The Space Shuttle *Challenger* was lost in January
1986 because the seals of a booster field joint could not follow the joint as it opened under pressure (see
[O-ring design](../../seals/o-ring-design/)). Later that year, a Titan 34D was lost after insulation separated from the
case of one of its solid boosters and let the flame get to the steel.

After the propellant has burned, all of these parts are dead weight. Hardware design must balance these two
requirements: sufficient strength, temperature capability and margin to survive, and no more mass than necessary.

## The three pressures: MEOP, proof and burst

Each pressure-carrying part is judged against three pressures:

| Pressure | What it is | How it is shown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **MEOP** | Maximum expected operating pressure. It includes the hot-day grain temperature, the manufacturing variation of the propellant and the ignition and erosive-burning transients, not only the nominal pressure | Ballistic prediction and test firings at the temperature extremes |
| **Proof** | A pressure above MEOP, applied to each unit (or a defined sample) | No yield, no leak, no permanent set; the test also screens for flaws |
| **Burst** | The demonstrated ultimate capability | Analysis, and test to destruction of qualification articles |

**The factors are not yours to select.** The proof and burst factors come from the governing requirement: for example
DEF STAN 07-085, AIAA S-080 and S-081 for pressure vessels, or the design rules of your programme. Do not take them from
a textbook or from a different programme. See [Case sizing](../case-sizing/), [Proof testing](../../verify/proof-testing/)
and [Margin policy](../../verify/margin-policy/).

## The number every part answers to: mass fraction

The **propellant mass fraction** of the motor is the fraction of its mass that is propellant:

$$
\zeta = \frac{m_p}{m_p + m_\text{inert}}
$$

The rocket equation tells what this gives. $m_0$ is the mass at ignition and $m_f$ is the mass at burnout:

$$
\Delta v = I_{sp}\,g_0 \ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}
\qquad
m_0 = m_p + m_\text{inert} + m_\text{payload},
\quad
m_f = m_\text{inert} + m_\text{payload}
$$

Here $I_{sp}\,g_0$ is the effective exhaust velocity. For a solid motor, it is $C_F\,c^*$ (see the
[primer](../../fundamentals/how-a-solid-motor-works/#from-pressure-to-thrust)). The inert mass is in the denominator, so
it has two bad effects: the motor pushes it for the full burn, and it is still there at the end. Two results follow
directly:

- **The cost of one inert kilogram.** By differentiation:
  $$
  \frac{\partial\,\Delta v}{\partial m_\text{inert}} = -\,I_{sp}\,g_0\,\frac{m_p}{m_0\,m_f}
  $$
- **The exchange ratio.** To keep the same $\Delta v$ when the inert mass increases by $\delta$, the mass ratio
  $m_0/m_f$ must stay the same. Thus the propellant must increase by $(m_0/m_f - 1)\,\delta$. With a mass ratio of
  2.5, each additional kilogram of hardware needs 1.5 kg more propellant. This needs a longer case, more insulation and
  a larger nozzle. This chain is why it is very difficult to correct an overweight motor late in a design.

<Analogy title="Packing for a long hike">
  Each gram of tent, stove and pack frame is a gram of food that you cannot carry. You also carry the tent all the way
  to the end, long after you have eaten the food. Ultralight hikers do not eat less. They weigh their equipment. A motor
  designer does the same with the case, the insulation and the nozzle.
</Analogy>

Large motors with composite cases get mass fractions of more than 0.9. Small motors have much lower values, because
the igniter, the nozzle throat, the bosses and the seals do not become smaller in proportion to the propellant.

<Aside type="tip" title="A design sequence that works">
  First set the MEOP and the proof and burst factors. Then select the class of case material, size the wall and add the
  insulation. Only then check the remaining mass fraction. If you select the material last, after the geometry is
  frozen, the motor can be overweight with no remaining options.
</Aside>

### Worked example: the 300 mm motor from the other pages

This is the same motor as in the [case-sizing](../case-sizing/#worked-example),
[propellant-grain](../propellant-grain/) and [insulation](../insulation/) examples. These are the starting values of
the calculator.

- **Case:** 11.77 kg. This is the 1.377 mm D6AC cylinder with hemispherical domes, from the case-sizing example.
- **Propellant:** a 300 mm grain with a 100 mm bore, 1000 mm long, in the cylinder only:
  $\frac{\pi}{4}(0.300^2 - 0.100^2) \times 1.000 \times 1770 = 111.2$ kg.
- **Insulation:** 2 mm of EPDM along the cylinder, where the grain covers it until tail-off
  ($0.942\ \text{m}^2 \times 2\ \text{mm} \times 1100 = 2.07$ kg), and the 8.9 mm that the insulation example needed in
  the domes ($0.283\ \text{m}^2 \times 8.9\ \text{mm} \times 1100 = 2.77$ kg). The total is 4.84 kg.
- **Nozzle 6 kg and other hardware 4 kg:** round illustrative values. **Payload** 50 kg.
- **Exhaust velocity** from the illustrative values of the grain page: $C_F\,c^* = 1.5 \times 1540 = 2310$ m/s, which
  is $I_{sp} = 235.55$ s.

1. **Inert mass** $= 11.77 + 4.84 + 6 + 4 = 26.61$ kg, so $\zeta = 111.2 / 137.81 = 0.807$.
2. **Mass ratio:** $m_0 = 187.81$ kg and $m_f = 76.61$ kg, so $m_0/m_f = 2.452$.
3. **Ideal $\Delta v$** $= 2310 \times \ln 2.452 = 2310 \times 0.897 = 2071$ m/s.
4. **Cost of one inert kilogram** $= 2310 \times 111.2 / (187.81 \times 76.61) = 17.9$ m/s, or
   $2.452 - 1 = 1.45$ kg of additional propellant to keep the same $\Delta v$.
5. **The cost of the dome insulation.** The 2.77 kg that the bondline limit needed costs approximately **51 m/s**. The
   insulation example found that the bondline governs, not the char. This is the cost of that result, and the reason to
   examine a case with more heat capacity or a better insulator.

Assume that a composite case could do the work of the case with 6 kg less mass (an assumption, not a design). Then
$\Delta v$ would increase by approximately 113 m/s. Try it in the calculator. Then try the same 6 kg on the nozzle. A
kilogram has the same effect in all locations.

## Materials and motor performance

Each material choice changes the motor, not only the part. This table shows what each class of material gives at motor
level, and what it costs.

| Material class | Examples | What it gives | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Case, high-strength steel | D6AC, 15CDV6, 300M, maraging | Good fracture-toughness data and a long service history, which helps certification. High stiffness, so the case grows less and strains the grain and bondline less | The heaviest case for a given MEOP. It fragments under an unplanned stimulus, which is poor for IM |
| Case, filament-wound composite | Carbon/epoxy, glass/epoxy, aramid | The lightest case for a given burst pressure: the largest single gain in mass fraction. It softens when heated, so it vents early in a fast fire | Lower stiffness, so the grain and bondline must accept more strain. Impact damage that cannot be seen; more complex liner, bond and inspection |
| Case, titanium alloy | Ti-6Al-4V, Ti-6242 | Approximately 40% lighter than steel for a similar strength, with metallic NDT methods | Expensive material and slow machining. Galling and hydrogen pick-up at joints and threads |
| Case, aluminium alloy | 2219, 7075, 6082 | Light, for small motors and low pressures. 2219 welds and works at cryogenic temperatures | Low strength: the wall gets thick as the pressure increases. Loses strength quickly with temperature, so it needs more insulation |
| Insulation and liner | EPDM, silica-filled EPDM, carbon and silica phenolic | Protects the case. A good bond is part of the structure | Pure inert mass. A debond increases the burning surface and can overpressure the motor |
| Throat insert | Bulk graphite, carbon–carbon, tungsten, rhenium | Holds the throat area, so the pressure and thrust stay flat over the burn | Erosion against mass and cost: graphite erodes fastest; refractory metals erode least but are dense, at the aft end where mass is worst |
| Nozzle structure and exit cone | Carbon phenolic, silica phenolic, C-103, composite shells | Expansion ratio, which gives performance | More expansion ratio is more mass and length, at the aft end, far from the centre of gravity |

<Aside type="caution" title="Peripheral coarse grain">
  Extrusions and forgings in some aluminium alloys have a layer of coarse grain at the surface, which has lower
  fatigue and stress-corrosion resistance. Where the drawing does not permit it on loaded surfaces, the minimum stock
  removal sets the minimum size of the bar or forging, adds machining time, and can need a heavier starting billet. Agree
  the requirement before you size the part, or the finished wall may not be possible after the skin is removed.
</Aside>

## Design factors and their trade-offs

None of these is a target. Each one is a trade-off between what it gives and what it costs. The hardware designer
inherits most of them from the ballistic design, so know what they do to the hardware. See
[Propellant grain](../propellant-grain/) and [Propellant](../propellant/) for the details.

| Factor | What it is | What it gives | What it costs the hardware |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Volumetric loading | Propellant volume ÷ the volume in the case | Total impulse for a given envelope: the largest performance lever per unit size | A thinner web and less port area: higher risk of erosive burning at ignition, and less room for insulation and stress relief |
| Web fraction | Burn distance ÷ grain radius | Burn duration | Thick webs limit the grain geometry, and have larger thermal strains in environmental cycling |
| Port-to-throat area ratio | Flow area along the grain ÷ throat area | A small port gives more propellant in the case | If too small, fast gas along the bore increases the local burn rate (erosive burning): a pressure peak at ignition that a simple prediction does not show |
| Sliver | Propellant that is not burned when the flame gets to the liner | A simple port geometry | Dead mass and a slow tail-off. Less sliver needs a more complex port, which is harder to cast and qualify |
| Case-bonded or cartridge-loaded | Grain cast into the case, or made separately and inserted | Case bonding gives higher loading and support | The bondline becomes structural and safety-critical for the full service life. A cartridge is easier to inspect but wastes volume |
| Thermal cycling and ageing | Repeated temperature cycles in storage | Not a choice: it comes from the storage environment | The propellant expands much more than the case, so each cycle strains the grain and bondline. This is the main cause of cracks and debonds, which increase the burning surface |
| L/D ratio | Motor length ÷ diameter | Usually set by the vehicle | Long motors: more pressure drop along the port and erosive burning. Short motors: harder dome and nozzle integration |
| Temperature sensitivity of the burn rate | Burn rate against grain temperature | A property of the propellant | A hot motor has a higher pressure: MEOP comes from the hot case plus the variation plus any transient |
| Throat erosion | The throat grows during the burn | A property of the insert material | Pressure and thrust decrease over the burn. The insert material trades erosion against cost, mass and thermal-shock resistance |
| Ignition transient | The pressure rise from ignition to steady burning | A fast and repeatable start | The case, closures and grain see this peak first. It is part of the pressure design case |

## Then and now: why the materials changed

| Part | Then | Now | Cause of the change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Case | Welded high-strength steel (D6AC, maraging); titanium on some upper stages | Filament-wound carbon/epoxy (aramid/epoxy before it) | Fibre put exactly along the load paths: much higher strength for each unit of mass |
| Insulation | Nitrile rubber filled with asbestos or silica | EPDM filled with aramid or silica | Health (asbestos), lower density, better ageing |
| Throat | Bulk and pyrolytic graphite; tungsten on small motors | Carbon–carbon, often 3-D woven | Toughness under thermal shock, at lower mass |
| Exit cone | Tape-wrapped carbon phenolic and silica phenolic over a metal shell | Thin radiation-cooled carbon–carbon; extendible cones on upper stages | Mass; more expansion ratio in the same stowed length |
| Steering | Jet vanes, jetavators, liquid injection | Movable nozzles on flexible bearings, more and more with electromechanical actuators | No thrust lost in the exhaust; no injectant tank or hydraulics |
| Nozzle housing | Steel | Aluminium, titanium, composite | Mass |

Look at the last column again. Most of these changes came from a **manufacturing process** (filament winding, 3-D
weaving, tape wrapping and laminated bearings) as much as from a new material. When you select a material, ask which
process it makes possible. That is usually where the real improvement is. See [Process & AM](../../make/process-and-am/).

**Beyond solid motors.** Liquid engines and in-space hardware show the same pattern:

| Area | Then | Now | Cause of the change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Combustion chamber liner | NARloy-Z (Cu-3Ag-0.5Zr), forged and machined with much waste | GRCop-42 and GRCop-84 (Cu-Cr-Nb), printed by laser powder bed fusion with internal cooling channels; bimetallic chambers with a superalloy jacket added by directed energy deposition | Silver cost and machining waste. GRCop makes a stable oxide where NARloy-Z blanches. Printing puts the cooling channels in the part instead of brazing them on |
| Hot structure and manifolds | René 41 and Waspaloy (strain-age cracking when welded); Hastelloy X sheet | Haynes 282 (weldable); GRX-810 oxide-dispersion-strengthened alloy, made for printing; printed Inconel 939 | A little peak strength traded for weldability and printability, which usually wins once repairs and scrap are counted |
| Hydrogen-side hardware | Inconel 718 with a hydrogen-embrittlement penalty; A286 | NASA HR-1 (resists hydrogen embrittlement); JBK-75 | An alloy designed for the environment is better than a reduced allowable on one that is not |
| Cryogenic tanks | 2219-T87; 2014 and early Al-Li alloys | Al-Li 2195, friction-stir welded (about 5% lighter and stiffer than 2219); 2050 and 2099; 301 and 304L stainless for large reusable structures | Al-Li gives mass fraction directly. Stainless becomes stronger when cold, costs less, welds well and survives entry heating |
| Nozzle extensions | C-103 niobium with a silicide coating; carbon phenolic | Printed C-103; SiC/SiC ceramic-matrix composites; carbon–carbon | The material almost did not change; the process did. Printing removes most of the forming and joining |
| Small thruster chambers | Molybdenum with a disilicide coating; C-103 | Rhenium with an iridium coating; printed refractory and CMC chambers | Life and duty cycle: Ir/Re operates hotter for longer, at a very high price |

## Designing the case to fail gently

If a motor is in a fuel fire, or if a fragment hits it, it can ignite whatever the designer does. The hardware decides
**what occurs next**. If the case continues to hold pressure, the burning propellant pressurises it until it bursts.
This is an explosion. If the case opens early and stays open, the propellant burns at low pressure. This is a fire.
From the hardware side, insensitive-munitions (IM) design makes sure of the second result.

<ImResponse />

A design is assessed against these threats: **fast heating** (a fuel fire around the item), **slow heating** (a slow
increase, for example from a fire in an adjacent store), **bullet impact**, **fragment impact**, **sympathetic
reaction** (a reaction of an adjacent munition) and **shaped-charge jet**. The NATO policy is STANAG 4439. AOP-39 gives
the assessment method and the necessary response for each threat. As with all factors on this site, get these
requirements from the current editions, not from a page such as this one.

What the hardware designer controls:

- **Composite cases usually vent themselves in a fast fire.** The resin becomes soft and the case loses strength
  early.
- **Metal cases need a special feature.** The feature makes the case or a closure weaker, or releases it, at a
  temperature below the temperature where the propellant reacts violently.
- **Slow heating is the difficult case.** Fast heating heats the case before the propellant. Slow heating heats the
  full item together. Thus the propellant can get to its reaction temperature while the case is still cool, strong and
  intact. Most venting features exist because of slow heating.
- **A venting feature is structure with two functions.** It must hold MEOP with margin at all storage temperatures and
  for the full service life, and it must still open when necessary. It needs the same proof, ageing and qualification
  evidence as the remainder of the case.

**The threats.** The test standards and the usual required responses are below. Check them against the current editions
and the requirements of your programme.

| Threat | Test standard | What it simulates | Usual required response | What it means for the hardware |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fast heating (fast cook-off) | STANAG 4240 | A liquid-fuel fire around the item | Type V (burning) or better | The case must vent before the propellant reacts violently. A composite case usually does this itself |
| Slow heating (slow cook-off) | STANAG 4382 | A slow temperature increase, for example from a fire in an adjacent compartment | Type V or better | The difficult case: the case is still strong when the propellant reacts. A designed venting feature is usually necessary |
| Bullet impact | STANAG 4241 | Small-arms rounds at service velocity | Type V or better | Ignition in a closed case pressurises it quickly. The vent area and how the case breaks decide the result |
| Fragment impact | STANAG 4496 | A steel fragment from an adjacent detonation | Type V or better | A much more energetic stimulus than a bullet |
| Sympathetic reaction | STANAG 4396 | Detonation of an adjacent munition in storage or transport | Type III (explosion) or better | Also a packaging problem: spacing, dunnage and the container count |
| Shaped-charge jet | STANAG 4526 | An anti-armour jet through the item | Type III or better | Very difficult to control by case design alone; shielding and propellant choice usually decide it |

**Features that make the case open early.** Each one has a cost in normal service:

| Feature | How it works | Advantages | Costs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grooves in the case wall | A designed weak line opens under internal pressure before the full case bursts | No moving parts, no energy source; works for slow and fast heating | Reduces the strength of a pressure vessel. The groove becomes a fatigue and stress-corrosion feature in service. Difficult on a thick case |
| Meltable or eutectic retention | A plug or a closure retainer is held by a material that melts below the reaction temperature of the propellant; it releases and the case vents | Simple, and acts by itself in a thermal threat. Weakens the wall less than a groove | Thermal threats only: no help in bullet or fragment impact. The retainer and its seal must survive MEOP, proof and the full service environment. Adds a leak path and an ageing seal |
| Composite case | The resin softens and loses strength at a much lower temperature than a metal case | The best balance of performance and IM; fails progressively, without steel fragments | Effective in fast heating, less so in slow heating. Impact damage and its inspection become design drivers |
| Slotted metal shell | A metal shell with distributed slots cannot hold pressure by itself; a liner or overwrap carries the operating pressure | Limits pressure containment in an impact or cook-off; keeps axial stiffness for handling | Needs a separate pressure-carrying layer. The slots must not cut the load paths that the shell carries |
| Grain stress relief | Boots, flaps and articulated ends limit the stress at the bondline in thermal cycling and ageing | Better structural margin in service *and* better IM: fewer cracks that increase the burning surface | Complex shapes to make and inspect; the bondline NDT is difficult there |

Active venting systems that cut the case are energetic devices. They are outside the scope of this site.

## Defining motor hardware on the drawing

- **Give more than the alloy.** Give the alloy, condition, product form, applicable specification and melt practice.
  See the [specification builder](../../materials/specification/). *"D6AC bar per AMS 6431, VAR, heat treated to
  1520 MPa min UTS, double temper"* is a specification. *"D6AC"* is not.
- **Special processes get their own callouts.** Heat treatment (with pyrometry to AMS 2750), welding procedure, NDT
  method and acceptance class, coatings and cleanliness each need a note and a specification. If you cannot verify a
  property by a measurement of the finished part, it needs an approved process and a certificate.
- **Fracture-critical parts have different rules.** When a part is classified as fracture-critical, its drawing gives
  the flaw-size and NDE requirements, and its material comes from a controlled quality class. Agree the classification
  before you finalise the tolerances, because it controls surface finish, radii and inspection access.
- **Traceability is a drawing requirement.** Give the batch or heat marking, its location and its method. Stamping is
  a stress raiser and is not permitted on many fracture-critical surfaces. Etching usually is permitted.
- **Dissimilar metals.** A steel case with an aluminium closure, or carbon fibre against any metal, makes a galvanic
  cell. Specify isolation (sealant, primer, isolating film) *and* an electrical bonding path on purpose. These two
  requirements are in opposite directions. See [Galvanic corrosion](../../materials/galvanic/).
- **Differential expansion.** A carbon throat insert in a metal housing has large temperature changes across materials
  with very different expansion. If a fit depends on temperature, give the assembly temperature on the drawing.
- **Seals in hot environments.** Confirm the seal compound against the real fluids and temperatures. Check if your
  programme requires approved seal materials. Many programmes do.
- **Bondlines are structure.** Bonds between insulation and case, and liner bonds, carry load and are the usual failure
  path. Define the surface preparation, adhesive, bondline thickness, cure and the NDT that verifies the bond. A bond
  without an inspection requirement is a joint that nobody has verified.
- **Peripheral coarse grain.** Where coarse grain is not permitted on loaded surfaces of aluminium extrusions and
  forgings, give the minimum stock removal, and size the starting material for it.
- **Example callouts** for the main parts are in the component cards above. They are illustrative: the specifications
  and classes come from your programme.
