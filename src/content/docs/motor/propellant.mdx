<Aside type="note" title="What this page covers, and what it does not cover">
  On this page, propellant is a material with properties: how it burns, how stiff and strong it is, how it ages, and
  which materials can touch it. There are no formulations, proportions, processing or burn-rate tailoring. These are
  the responsibility of your energetics and propulsion specialists and their safety authority.
</Aside>

## Why this matters

Two of the numbers that size motor hardware come from the propellant. The first is the **MEOP**, which is the design
pressure of the case, the joints and the proof test. The second is the **strain capability**, which decides if the
bore cracks on a cold night. Both change with temperature much more than the properties of a metal. Between −40 °C and
+60 °C, the illustrative propellant on this page changes its chamber pressure by approximately 22%. Its stiffness
changes by more than ten times, as a function of the loading rate.

The hardware designer does not select these numbers. But you must know where they come from, what they depend on, and
exactly which numbers to ask for.

## Part 1: Ballistic properties

### The numbers you inherit

| Property | Symbol | What it means | Where you use it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Burn-rate coefficient | $a$ | Burn rate at unit pressure, at a reference temperature | The pressure balance ([primer](../../fundamentals/how-a-solid-motor-works/#why-chamber-pressure-settles-at-one-value)) |
| Pressure exponent | $n$ | How much the burn rate increases with pressure: $r = a\,p^n$ | Increases each variation by $1/(1-n)$ |
| Temperature sensitivity of burn rate | $\sigma_p$ | % change in burn rate for each kelvin of grain temperature, at constant pressure | Hot-day MEOP (below) |
| Temperature sensitivity of pressure | $\pi_K$ | % change in chamber pressure for each kelvin, at constant $K_n$ | MEOP and burn time (below) |
| Density, characteristic velocity | $\rho_p$, $c^*$ | Mass for each volume; gas made for each unit of throat flow | Pressure, thrust, mass fraction |

### Temperature: why the hot day sets MEOP

A propellant grain that starts warm burns faster. Over a firing-temperature range, an exponential describes this effect
well:

$$
a(T) = a_0\,e^{\,\sigma_p (T - T_0)}
$$

The [pressure balance](../../fundamentals/how-a-solid-motor-works/#why-chamber-pressure-settles-at-one-value) gives
$p_c \propto a^{1/(1-n)}$. Thus at constant $K_n$, the chamber pressure follows the same law, with a larger
coefficient:

$$
p_c(T) = p_c(T_0)\,e^{\,\pi_K (T - T_0)},
\qquad
\pi_K = \frac{\sigma_p}{1 - n}
$$

At constant $K_n$, the burn rate increases by the same factor. Thus the **burn time decreases** as
$e^{-\pi_K (T-T_0)}$. A hot motor pushes harder for a shorter time. The total impulse almost does not change, because
the same propellant burns with almost the same $c^*$. Thus the hot and cold pressure traces have almost the same area.

<Analogy title="Kindling from a sunny windowsill">
  Kindling that was in the sun catches fire and burns faster than kindling from a cold shed. The flame uses less of its
  heat to bring the fuel to its burning temperature, so it moves on sooner. Warm propellant is the same. In a motor
  there is a second step. Faster burning increases the pressure, which increases the burn rate again (the $1/(1-n)$
  amplification), until the throat flow balances it.
</Analogy>

### Building MEOP

MEOP is the nominal pressure taken to the worst firing condition of the motor, plus the manufacturing variation:

1. **Hot day.** Take the nominal peak pressure at the reference temperature to the hottest firing temperature with
   $\pi_K$.
2. **Manufacturing variation.** Each variation is amplified by $1/(1-n)$:
   - **burn rate:** the scatter in $a$ from batch to batch;
   - **burning area:** grain dimensions and bore tolerances;
   - **throat area:** a smaller throat gives a higher pressure.
3. **Combining them.** Independent variations are usually combined by root-sum-square (RSS). A worst-case stack puts
   each variation at its limit at the same time, and it is more conservative. Your programme sets the method and the
   probability level. This page does not.
4. **Transients from firings.** These are the ignition spike, erosive burning early in the burn in long, narrow bores,
   and grain deformation under pressure. They come from test data, and you add them to the result.

<Aside type="caution" title="Ask for the build-up, not only the number">
  A MEOP without its temperature range has little meaning. Ask your ballistics team for the build-up: the hottest
  firing temperature, the variations included, the combination method, and if the transients are included. If the
  operating temperature range increases later in the programme, MEOP also increases, and so does the case wall.
</Aside>

### Worked example: building the 10 MPa MEOP

The values are illustrative and similar to AP/HTPB: a nominal peak pressure of 8.8 MPa at 20 °C, $n = 0.35$ and
$\sigma_p = 0.13$ %/K. The firing range is −40 °C to +60 °C. The variations are burn rate ±3%, burning area ±1% and
throat area ±1%. These are the starting values of the calculator.

1. **Sensitivity.** $\pi_K = 0.0013 / 0.65 = 0.0020$ /K (0.20 %/K, within the 0.1–0.3 %/K that the primer gives). The
   amplification is $1/(1-n) = 1.54$.
2. **Hot day.** $8.8 \times e^{\,0.0020 \times 40} = 8.8 \times 1.083 = 9.53$ MPa.
3. **Variations.** Burn rate $1.03^{1.54} - 1 = 4.65\%$; burning area $1.01^{1.54} - 1 = 1.54\%$; throat area
   $(1/0.99)^{1.54} - 1 = 1.56\%$.
4. **Combined (RSS).** $\sqrt{4.65^2 + 1.54^2 + 1.56^2} = 5.14\%$, so MEOP $= 9.53 \times 1.0514 = 10.02$ MPa. This is
   the 10 MPa design pressure of the [case-sizing example](../case-sizing/#worked-example).
5. **Worst-case stack.** $1.0465 \times 1.0154 \times 1.0156 - 1 = 7.92\%$, which gives 10.29 MPa (2.6% higher). Your
   programme decides which method to use.
6. **The cold end.** The nominal pressure is $8.8 \times e^{-0.0020 \times 60} = 7.80$ MPa, and 7.41 MPa on the low
   side. The cold motor burns 12.7% longer and the hot motor 7.7% shorter. The minimum pressure is also important. It
   sets the lowest thrust, and it must stay above the pressure that the propellant needs to burn steadily. Get this
   number from your ballistics team.

The difference between nominal and MEOP is 1.22 MPa. Of this, the hot day gives 0.73 MPa (60%). Usually, temperature
is the largest single term, not manufacturing.

## Part 2: Mechanical properties

### Stiffness depends on the clock and the thermometer

Composite propellant is a highly filled rubber. As with [Silly Putty](../propellant-grain/#the-material-a-filled-rubber-with-a-memory),
its stiffness depends on how fast you load it and how cold it is. **Cold has the same effect as fast.** This
equivalence is time–temperature superposition. It lets one set of laboratory tests describe years of storage and also
milliseconds of ignition.

The **shift factor** $a_T$ connects the two. It tells how many times slower all relaxation processes are at the
temperature $T$ than at a reference temperature $T_s$. For many polymers, which include propellant binders, it follows
the Williams–Landel–Ferry (WLF) equation:

$$
\log_{10} a_T = \frac{-C_1\,(T - T_s)}{C_2 + T - T_s}
\qquad\qquad
\xi = \frac{t}{a_T}
$$

A load applied for a time $t$ at a temperature $T$ has the same effect as a load applied for the **reduced time** $\xi$
at $T_s$. Read the stiffness for that $\xi$ from the **master curve** of the propellant. This is its relaxation modulus,
measured over many decades of time and shifted to one temperature.

With the illustrative constants ($T_s = -20$ °C, the general WLF constants, and a power-law master curve):

- **Cool-down to −40 °C over 8 hours.** $a_T \approx 148$, so 8 hours at −40 °C has the same effect as 194 s at

- **Ignition at −40 °C,** with pressurisation in approximately 50 ms. The same $a_T$ changes 50 ms into 0.34 ms.
  $E \approx 58$ MPa, which is **14 times stiffer**, in the same grain at the same temperature.
- **Storage for 10 years at 20 °C** is far out on the relaxed end of the curve: approximately 3 MPa, the equilibrium
  value.

There are three results for hardware:

1. **Thermal strain analyses use the relaxed modulus,** and ignition analyses use the stiff, short-time modulus. The
   [grain page](../propellant-grain/) does this.
2. **The bond stress at a cold ignition is high,** because the grain is stiff at the time when the case stretches.
3. **Measure the capability at the rate and temperature of each load case.** There is no single strength.

### Strength and strain capability

Strength is measured on dog-bone tensile specimens across a matrix of temperatures and strain rates. Each test gives
the **maximum stress** $\sigma_m$, the **strain at maximum stress** $\varepsilon_m$ and the **strain at break**
$\varepsilon_b$. Design margins usually use $\varepsilon_m$ or $\varepsilon_b$, as the programme specifies.

- **Dewetting.** At higher strains, the binder pulls away from the filler particles. Small voids open, the volume of the
  material increases, and the stress–strain curve bends over. This sets $\varepsilon_m$.
- **Pressure helps.** A superimposed pressure (as at ignition) prevents dewetting and increases the strain capability.
  Thus capability measured at atmospheric pressure is conservative for ignition. Programmes test under pressure, or
  they accept the conservatism.
- **Biaxial is worse than uniaxial.** The bore surface has strain in two directions at the same time, and the biaxial
  capability is usually lower than the uniaxial capability. It is measured on biaxial specimens, or the analysis rules
  of the programme include it.
- **Rate and temperature collapse onto one curve.** If you plot stress at break against strain at break, the results
  from all rates and temperatures are on a single **failure envelope** (Smith, 1963). This is the strength equivalent
  of the master curve.
- **Damage accumulates.** Each thermal cycle uses part of the capability. Long-life motors are assessed with
  cumulative-damage methods, not one load case at a time.

### Ageing

Propellant properties continue to change in storage. The hardware margins must be sufficient at the **end of life**,
not only on the delivery day.

- **Composite propellants** usually become harder. The binder continues to cross-link, so the modulus increases and
  the strain capability decreases. Plasticiser can migrate into the liner and insulation. Where the oxidiser is
  hygroscopic, the propellant absorbs moisture.
- **Nitrate-ester propellants** also have a chemical limit. The stabiliser is used up over time, and this sets a safe
  life that is separate from the mechanical life.
- **What this means for hardware.** Grain and bondline margins are calculated with aged properties. The ageing evidence
  comes from accelerated and real-time ageing, and from surveillance motors taken from the stockpile. See
  [Storage & ageing](../../verify/storage-ageing/).

## Propellant families as engineering materials

The family is important for hardware, because stiffness, temperature range, ageing and, most of all, compatibility are
different between families.

| Family | Mechanically | Temperature range | Exhaust and performance | Ages mainly by | Hardware points to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Double-base (DB) | Stiff, similar to a glassy polymer; one phase | Narrow; brittle when cold | Low smoke; lower $I_{sp}$ than composites | Stabiliser depletion; plasticiser migration | The copper rule; cold cracking |
| Elastomer-modified cast double-base (EMCDB) | Tougher than DB | Wider than DB | Low smoke | As DB, plus elastomer migration | Copper rule in full |
| Composite-modified double-base (CMDB) | Filled; partly similar to a composite | Moderate | Higher $I_{sp}$ than DB; smoky when metallised | The mechanisms of the two families; filler debonding | The compatibility rules of the two families |
| AP composite | Highly filled rubber, viscoelastic | Wide | High $I_{sp}$; visible exhaust when metallised | Binder hardening; migration; bondline ageing; moisture | Bondline integrity; humidity control |
| AN composite | Filled rubber | Limited by oxidiser phase changes during cycling | Lower $I_{sp}$; low signature, no chlorine | Moisture; phase changes | Moisture ingress and corrosion |

## Double-base propellants: forms and properties

Double-base propellant is nitrocellulose that is gelatinised by a liquid nitrate-ester plasticiser. Each of the two
components carries fuel and oxidiser in its own molecules, so the propellant is one homogeneous phase, with no crystals
and no separate binder. This gives it a low-smoke exhaust, a smooth burning surface, and the mechanical behaviour of a
glassy polymer. It also gives it the two problems of the family: it is brittle when cold, and its nitrate esters
migrate and decompose.

| Cast double-base (CDB) | A grain cast in a mould or in the case | Larger, and in more complex shapes | Free-standing, or bonded to the case through a liner | Larger tactical motors and sustainers |
| Composite-modified double-base (CMDB) | Usually a cast grain: a double-base matrix filled with oxidiser crystals and often aluminium | As CDB | As CDB | More energy, where smoke is acceptable |
| Elastomer-modified cast double-base (EMCDB) | A cast grain with a crosslinked polymer in the double-base matrix | As CDB | Bonded to the case | Low-smoke motors that are case-bonded and fire over a wide temperature range |
| Crosslinked nitrate-ester propellants (XLDB, NEPE) | A cast grain: a crosslinked binder plasticised with nitrate esters and filled with oxidiser | Large | Bonded to the case | Large motors that need the most energy |

### A plateau in the burn rate

Some double-base propellants have a **plateau**. Over a band of pressure, the burn rate hardly changes, so the pressure
exponent $n$ is close to zero. In a **mesa**, the burn rate falls as the pressure increases ($n$ below zero). In that
band, the chamber pressure changes much less with the grain temperature and with small changes of burning area or
throat area, because the amplification $1/(1-n)$ is close to 1. Try it in the [MEOP calculator](#calculator): with
$n = 0.05$, a 1% change of burning area gives about 1.05% of pressure, and with $n = 0.35$ it gives 1.54%.

Ask for the burn-rate law over the full range of pressure. Outside the plateau, the exponent can be high, and a motor
that runs at the edge of its plateau can move off it on a hot day.

### Free-standing or bonded to the case

- **A free-standing grain** (extruded, and often cast) is not bonded to the case. Supports hold it against
  acceleration and vibration, and a trap at the aft end keeps it in place. It needs clearance for its expansion,
  because double-base propellant expands several times as much as steel. With the illustrative 80 µm/m·K of the grain
  screen against 12.3 µm/m·K for steel, the length of a 1 m grain changes 6.8 mm more than its case over 100 K.
- **An inhibitor covers the surfaces that must not burn.** It must stay bonded to the grain, and it must stop the
  nitrate ester that migrates into it. If the inhibitor debonds, the flame gets to a surface that was not designed to
  burn, and the pressure increases.
- **A grain bonded to the case** takes the strain of the cool-down and of ignition, and plain double-base has little
  strain capability when cold. In the [Propellant grain](../propellant-grain/#calculator) calculator, select the
  double-base class at −40 °C: the 300 mm example grain has about 8% of cool-down strain against an illustrative
  capability of about 2.5%. For this reason many double-base grains are free-standing, and EMCDB was developed.

### Ageing and the safe life

- **Chemical life.** The nitrate esters decompose slowly in storage, and a stabiliser in the propellant takes up the
  products. When the stabiliser is used up, the decomposition accelerates. Surveillance measures the stabiliser that
  remains, and this sets a chemical safe life that is separate from the mechanical life.
- **Migration.** The nitrate-ester plasticiser moves into the inhibitors, liners, insulation and adhesives that touch
  the grain. The surface of the grain becomes harder, and the material that takes up the plasticiser can swell, soften
  or debond. Specify barrier layers and compatible materials, and test them together (below).
- **Mechanical ageing.** The grain becomes stiffer and less extensible with age. Check the grain and the bond with aged
  properties ([Storage & ageing](../../verify/storage-ageing/)).

The compositions, the manufacture, the burn-rate tailoring and the hazard classification of these propellants are the
responsibility of your energetics specialists and their safety authority.

## What may touch it: materials compatibility

A material is not "compatible with propellant" in general. Nitrate-ester propellants and composite propellants degrade
by different mechanisms. Thus the same metal can be excluded near one and only controlled near the other. **Read across
the row.** A test shows compatibility. A datasheet does not. STANAG 4147 is the applicable agreement. It uses methods
such as vacuum stability, heat-flow calorimetry and, for nitrocellulose-based propellants only, stabiliser depletion.

<Aside type="caution" title="Two important rules">
  **The copper rule.** Copper and copper alloys are excluded near nitrate-ester (double-base) propellant. Do not use
  brass fittings, bronze bushes, beryllium-copper tools, solder that contains copper, or copper plating. This applies
  to the hardware, the tooling, the handling equipment and the store. A bought-in part can easily break this rule.

  **The silicone rule.** Silicone is not permitted in most propellant areas and bond areas. It does not react, but it
  migrates, transfers on contact and destroys adhesive bonds. Bondlines are structure, so a silicone fingerprint can
  cause a motor to fail. This includes release agents, sealants, lubricants and hand creams.
</Aside>

| Material | Nitrate-ester | AP composite | AN composite | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Copper and copper alloys | **Excluded** | Approval | Approval | Copper accelerates nitrate-ester decomposition; with other propellants, corrosion and contamination |
| Lead and lead alloys | **Excluded** | Approval | Approval | Often present without notice in free-machining brass and steel, and in solders |
| Silver (plating, brazing) | **Excluded** | Approval | Approval | The same type of problem as copper near nitrate esters |

| Carbon and low-alloy steel | Approval | Approval | Approval | Corrodes; incompatibilities are often in the finish |

the approved list of your programme applies.

## Where this stops

- $r = a\,p^n$ is a fit over a pressure range. Some propellants change their exponent within their operating range.
  Use the law only over the range of the fit.
- The exponential temperature law is also a fit. It is valid over the conditioning range that it came from.
- The MEOP build-up shows the mechanism. The real calculation follows the statistical method of your programme and
  includes transients measured on firings.
- The master curve and its constants are illustrative. Real analyses use measured relaxation data and fitted shift
  factors, and treat dewetting, pressure and ageing separately.
- The contents of the propellant and how it is made are outside the scope of this page.

## On the drawing

- **Specify the propellant by its specification or designation,** never by a description of its contents. Batch
  acceptance (burn rate at reference conditions within a band, density and mechanical properties) is in the propellant
  specification. The motor drawing refers to this specification.
- **Give the operating and storage temperature limits** on the motor drawing or interface document. MEOP and all grain
  margins are valid only within these limits.
- **Name each material that can touch propellant** (metal, finish, seal, adhesive, release agent, and also temporary
  covers) from the approved list. Never write "or equivalent", and never let the shop select.
- **Define the bondlines as structure:** surface preparation, liner, cure and inspection. See
  [Insulation](../insulation/#on-the-drawing).
