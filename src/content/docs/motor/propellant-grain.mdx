## Why this matters

The grain is the fuel tank, the combustion chamber wall and the throttle of the motor, all at the same time. Its
**geometry** sets how much burning surface is exposed at each instant. Thus it sets the chamber pressure and the thrust
for the full burn. It also sets how much propellant fits in the case, and how high the peak pressure is. The case must
be sized for this peak.

Its **structural integrity** is a safety subject, not a performance subject. A crack is new burning surface. More
surface gives more gas and a higher pressure. Because pressure amplifies changes by $1/(1-n)$, this can cause a case
failure. This is why cold-soak cracking and debonding are very important.

This page has two parts: [geometry and burn-back](#part-1-geometry-and-burn-back), then
[structural integrity](#part-2-structural-integrity).

## Part 1: Geometry and burn-back

### The burning surface moves like a grass fire

During most of a burn, each exposed point of propellant moves back **perpendicular to the local surface** at the same
burn rate $r$. After the flame burns a distance $y$ (the **web burned**), the surface is the set of all points that are
at a distance $y$ from the original surface.

<Analogy title="A grass fire spreading from a burning outline">
  Light the edge of a field along an irregular line. With no wind, the fire front moves into the grass at the same speed
  everywhere. Bumps that point into the grass become smooth arcs. Narrow areas of grass between two fronts burn from
  the two sides and disappear first. If you draw the front each minute, you get contour lines. The burn-back map in the
  simulator below uses these contours for its colours.
</Analogy>

This has two direct results:

- **Port corners that point into the propellant** (star tips, slot ends) become round arcs as they burn.
- **Propellant ridges that point into the port** (between star points) stay sharp and move back quickly, because they
  burn from the two sides. When these ridges burn out, the burning area decreases quickly.

### Words you need

| Term | Meaning | Why it is important |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Web, $w$ | Distance that the flame burns before it first gets to the case | Sets the burn time: $t_b \approx w / \bar r$ |
| Web fraction | $w$ divided by the outer radius of the grain | High web fraction = long burn and more propellant, but more structural strain |
| Volumetric loading | Propellant volume ÷ volume available inside the case | How much of the case you filled with propellant |
| Sliver | Propellant that stays when the flame first touches the case | Burns in a low-pressure tail-off, and gives little useful thrust |
| $K_n$ | Burning area ÷ throat area | Sets the chamber pressure |
| Port ÷ throat area | Flow area along the bore relative to the throat | If too small, the bore flow is fast and causes erosive burning |

### Shape sets the thrust curve

<GrainProfiles />

- **Tube (internal-burning cylinder).** The port only becomes larger, so the burning area and the thrust increase
  until the end: **progressive**. It is simple and robust, but the peak pressure at the end of the burn sizes the case.
- **Star.** The area of the points decreases while the circular region increases. You can balance these to get an
  almost **neutral** thrust. The disadvantage is a **sliver** behind the star valleys, unless the design adds fillets or
  sliver-filling inserts.
- **Core plus radial slots (wagon wheel, and the slotted section of a finocyl).** A very large initial burning area for
  a high-thrust boost. It is usually **regressive** as the slots open.
- **End burner.** It burns face-on, like a cigarette. It is perfectly neutral, with the longest burn time and the most
  propellant for the case, but low thrust. Also, the case wall behind the face has hot gas on it for the full burn.
- **BATES (stacked short tubes, ends burning).** The bore increases while the ends become smaller. The two effects
  almost cancel.

The BATES balance is a good example of geometry analysis. For a segment of outer diameter $D$, port $d$ and length $L$,
with the two ends burning, the burning area after a web $y$ is:

$$
A_b(y) = \underbrace{\pi (d + 2y)(L - 2y)}_{\text{bore}} + \underbrace{2 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4}\left(D^2 - (d+2y)^2\right)}_{\text{two ends}}
$$

If you make the area at ignition equal to the area at burnout ($d + 2y = D$), you get the usual sizing rule:

$$
L = \frac{3D + d}{2}
$$

With this length, the trace starts and ends at the same level, with a small hump between.

### From burning area to thrust

Combine the burning area with the pressure balance from [the fundamentals page](../../fundamentals/how-a-solid-motor-works/#why-chamber-pressure-settles-at-one-value).
At each web position $y$:

$$
p_c(y) = \left(\rho_p\,a\,c^*\,\frac{A_b(y)}{A_t}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-n}}
\qquad
\frac{dt}{dy} = \frac{1}{a\,p_c^{\,n}}
\qquad
F = C_F\,p_c\,A_t
$$

Go through the web in steps and add the time. This gives the pressure and thrust traces. It also gives a useful check.
The mass flow through the throat is $p_c A_t / c^*$, thus the total impulse is:

$$
I_t = \int F\,dt = C_F\, c^*\, m_p
$$

This is true for all grain shapes. The geometry changes *when* the motor gives the impulse, not how much impulse it
gives.

### Simulator

The simulator finds the burn-back. For each point in the cross-section, it calculates the distance to the original
port outline. This is the grass-fire rule, done numerically. Try the presets, then change one dimension at a time.

<BurnbackSim />

Things to try:

- **Tube → star.** With the same propellant and the same case, the star makes the thrust curve flatter, but it leaves a
  sliver. Look at the burn map. The sliver is the propellant between the star valleys and the case when the flame first
  touches the wall.
- **Make the star points deeper** (larger tip diameter). The profile changes from neutral to regressive.
- **BATES:** change the segment length away from $(3D+d)/2$ and look at the tilt of the trace.
- **Make the tube port smaller,** to 50 mm. The port-to-throat warning comes on.

### Worked example: tube grain by hand

The grain has an outer diameter of 150 mm, a port of 80 mm and a length of 600 mm, with inhibited ends. The propellant
has the default values: $a = 4.05$ mm/s at 1 MPa, $n = 0.35$, $\rho_p = 1770$ kg/m³, $c^* = 1540$ m/s and
$C_F = 1.5$. The throat is sized so that the peak pressure is 7 MPa.

1. **Burning area.** At the start: $\pi \times 80 \times 600 = 150{,}800$ mm². At the end (the port gets to 150 mm):
   $\pi \times 150 \times 600 = 282{,}700$ mm². The ratio is 1.875: **progressive**.
2. **Throat.** The peak pressure is at the end. $K_n = 7^{0.65} \times 10^9 / (1770 \times 4.05 \times 1540) = 320.9$,
   so $A_t = 282{,}700 / 320.9 = 881$ mm² and $D_t = 33.5$ mm.
3. **Starting pressure.** $p \propto A_b^{1/(1-n)}$, so $p_0 = 7 \times (80/150)^{1.538} = 2.66$ MPa. A change of 1.9
   times in area becomes a change of 2.6 times in pressure.
4. **Burn time.** The burn rate at the end is $4.05 \times 7^{0.35} = 8.00$ mm/s, and
   $r \propto (\text{port radius})^{n/(1-n)}$. Integrate $dt = dy/r$ from a port radius of 40 mm to 75 mm. This gives
   $t_b = 5.11$ s.
5. **Mass and impulse.** $m_p = \frac{\pi}{4}(150^2 - 80^2) \times 600 \times 1770 \times 10^{-9} = 13.4$ kg, and
   $I_t = 1.5 \times 1540 \times 13.4 = 31.0$ kN·s. Thus the average thrust is approximately 6.1 kN.

The tube preset of the simulator gives these numbers within approximately 1%. The automated tests check the two.

<Aside type="caution" title="Where the burn-back model stops">
  Real motors have effects that this model does not include. **Erosive burning:** fast gas along the bore increases
  the local burn rate. This is why a low port-to-throat ratio gives an ignition spike. Also: the **ignition
  transient**; **throat erosion** (the pressure decreases during the burn); the **burn-rate scale factor and "hump"**
  between small test motors and full-scale grains; **3-D geometry** (finocyl fins, conical ports, domed ends); and grain
  **deformation** under pressure, which changes the port. Production ballistics use full internal-ballistics codes,
  calibrated with motor firings.
</Aside>

## Part 2: Structural integrity

### The material: a filled rubber with a memory

Composite propellant is approximately 85–90% solid particles (oxidiser and aluminium) in a rubber binder. Mechanically
it is a **viscoelastic** solid. Its stiffness and strength depend on **how fast** you load it and **how cold** it is.
The models for this are on the [viscoelasticity page](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/).

<Analogy title="Silly Putty">
  If you pull Silly Putty slowly, it stretches a long way. If you pull it quickly, it breaks. If you put it in the
  freezer, even a slow pull breaks it. Propellant follows the same pattern, and cold has the same effect as fast. This
  equivalence (time–temperature superposition) lets a few laboratory tests describe years of storage and milliseconds
  of ignition.
</Analogy>

Three facts control all the results below:

1. **Propellant is almost incompressible.** It is easy to change its shape, but its volume almost does not change (the
   bulk modulus is thousands of times the shear modulus). Where its volume *tries* to change, the geometry decides
   where the movement goes.
2. **Relaxation decreases the stress, not the strain.** In a case-bonded grain, the geometry and the expansion
   coefficients set the bore strain, not the stiffness. Slow cool-downs relax the *stress*, but the *strain* stays.
3. **Capability depends on rate, temperature, pressure and age.** There is no single "strain to failure". Compare each
   load case with the capability measured at its own rate and temperature. The capability decreases as the propellant
   ages.

### The load cases

| Load case | What occurs | Usually governs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cure cool-down and cold storage | Propellant tries to shrink much more than the case that it is bonded to | Bore cracking, bond tension |
| Temperature cycling | Repeated cool-down strains | Cumulative damage over the service life |
| Ignition pressurisation | The case expands; the incompressible grain amplifies this at the bore | Bore cracking at cold firing |
| Flight acceleration, vibration | Inertial loads on a soft grain | Bond shear at the ends, slender grains |
| Long-term storage (slump) | The grain sags under its own weight for years | Large motors, bore deformation |

### Cool-down: why the bore cracks

Propellant cures warm and bonds to the case at that temperature, where it has no stress. Its thermal expansion
coefficient is approximately $10^{-4}$/K, which is approximately eight times that of steel. Thus when it cools, it tries
to shrink much more than the case. The outside is bonded to the case and cannot follow. The propellant also cannot
change its volume. Thus the shrinkage must come from the bore, which opens.

<GrainCooldown />

<Analogy title="A ring doughnut glued inside a steel bangle, put in the freezer">
  The doughnut tries to shrink, but its outer edge is bonded to the bangle, which almost does not shrink. The doughnut
  cannot become smaller from the outside, so its hole becomes larger. This stretches the inside surface around its
  circumference. A doughnut that is thicker relative to its hole has more volume to accommodate, and its small hole
  must stretch more.
</Analogy>

For a long, case-bonded cylindrical grain with a bore radius $a$ and an outer radius $b$, let $m = b/a$. Because the
propellant is incompressible, the area of each ring of propellant can change only by thermal expansion. This sets the
displacement field. With the boundary condition at the case, the bore hoop strain on cooling by $\Delta T$ (negative for
cooling) is:

$$
\varepsilon_{\theta}(a) = \Delta T \left[\frac{3\alpha_p - \alpha_c}{2}\,(1 - m^2) + m^2\,\alpha_c\right]
\;\approx\; \tfrac{3}{2}\,(\alpha_p - \alpha_c)\,|\Delta T|\,(m^2 - 1)
$$

The approximate form is within $\alpha_c |\Delta T|$ (approximately 0.1%) of the full form. The important term is
$m^2$: **the bore strain increases with the square of the outer-to-bore ratio**. A grain with a small bore and a thick
web gives the most propellant, but it is the most difficult grain to keep free of cracks. This is the main structural
trade in grain design.

The same analysis gives the radial tension that pulls the grain off the case at the bond. It uses the relaxed modulus
$E$ of the propellant for this slow load:

$$
\sigma_r(b) = E\,(m^2 - 1)\,(\alpha_p - \alpha_c)\,|\Delta T| \;\approx\; \tfrac{2}{3}\,E\,\varepsilon_\theta(a)
$$

A soft propellant keeps the bond stress low. It does not decrease the bore strain.

### Ignition: the case stretches, the bore stretches more

At ignition, the pressure pushes the grain against the case, and the case expands by a fraction of a per cent. The
propellant cannot change its volume much. Thus the small outward movement at radius $b$ causes a much larger movement
of the bore:

$$
\varepsilon_{\theta}(a) = m^2\,\varepsilon_{\theta,\text{case}} + \frac{m^2 - 1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{z,\text{case}} + \frac{p}{K}\right)
$$

Here $K$ is the bulk modulus of the propellant. For a thin closed-end case with a modulus $E_c$, a Poisson's ratio
$\nu_c$ and a wall $h$:

$$
\varepsilon_{\theta,\text{case}} = \frac{p\,b}{E_c\,h}\left(1 - \frac{\nu_c}{2}\right)
\qquad
\varepsilon_{z,\text{case}} = \frac{p\,b}{E_c\,h}\left(\frac{1}{2} - \nu_c\right)
$$

This connects the grain directly to [case sizing](../case-sizing/). A lighter case with more stress stretches more, and
the bore of the grain gets more strain. The worst case is usually a **cold firing**, where the cool-down strain is
already present when ignition adds its share.

### Worked example: HTPB grain in the D6AC case

This is the same 300 mm D6AC case as the [case-sizing worked example](../case-sizing/#worked-example) (wall 1.377 mm,
MEOP 10 MPa), with a 100 mm circular bore ($m = 3$). The composite propellant has $\alpha_p = 100 \times 10^{-6}$/K, a
relaxed modulus of 3.6 MPa and a bulk modulus of 3.5 GPa. The steel has $\alpha_c = 12.3 \times 10^{-6}$/K and $\nu_c = 0.29$ (from the material library). The
stress-free temperature is 60 °C and the coldest firing is at −40 °C, so $\Delta T = -100$ K. The safety factor of 1.5
below is for this example only. Push **Load worked example** in the structural calculator to load these inputs.

1. **Cool-down strain.** $100 \times \left[\frac{300 - 12.3}{2} \times 8 - 9 \times 12.3\right] \times 10^{-6} = 10.4\%$.
2. **Case strain at MEOP.** $\frac{p b}{E_c h} = \frac{10 \times 150}{205{,}000 \times 1.377} = 0.531\%$. Thus the hoop
   strain is $0.454\%$ and the axial strain is $0.112\%$.
3. **Ignition strain.** $9 \times 0.454\% + 4 \times (0.112\% + 10/3500) = 4.09\% + 1.59\% = 5.7\%$.
4. **Cold ignition.** $10.4\% + 5.7\% = 16.1\%$. Against the illustrative capability of 39.6%, with a safety factor of
   1.5, the margin is $39.6 / (1.5 \times 16.1) - 1 = +0.64$.
5. **Bond tension.** $3.6 \times 8 \times 88 \times 10^{-6} \times 100 = 0.25$ MPa.

Now set the **concentration factor to 2.5**. This is approximately the effect of a star point. The cold-ignition strain
becomes 40%, and the margin becomes negative. The same grain passes with a circular bore and fails with a star. This is
why star points get large fillet radii and their own detailed analysis.

### Design levers

- **Larger bore / smaller web.** This has the largest effect, because the strain changes as $m^2$. It decreases the
  propellant loading.
- **Stiffer or thicker case.** This decreases the ignition strain directly. Composite cases are light, but they stretch
  more: this example grain fails in a carbon case of the same duty (see
  [Composite cases](../composite-cases/#worked-example)).
- **Increase the minimum firing temperature.** Each degree removed from $\Delta T$ removes strain. Usually this needs
  an agreement at system level.
- **Decrease the stress-free temperature.** A cooler cure, or a cure under pressure, decreases the cool-down
  $\Delta T$.
- **Stress-relief flaps and boots** at the grain ends. Where the bond stops, the stresses are much higher than the
  long-cylinder values above.
- **Cartridge-loaded grains** (made separately and put into the case) do not have the bonded cool-down problem. But
  their support and inhibition are more complex. They are usual for double-base grains, which become glassy at low
  temperature.

### Where these formulas stop

<Aside type="caution" title="Screening, not qualification">
  These are long-cylinder, elastic, closed-form results. A real structural analysis of a grain uses viscoelastic finite
  elements with measured relaxation data. It includes the grain ends and all geometric features, adds the damage over
  the full thermal and handling history, includes ageing, and is validated against instrumented motors and analogue
  tests.
</Aside>

- **Ends and terminations.** Bond stresses at the grain ends can be several times the values above.
- **Non-circular ports.** Star points, slot tips and fin roots need FEA. The concentration factor here is only an
  estimate.
- **Dewetting.** At high strains, the binder separates from the solid particles and voids open. The material is then
  not incompressible, and its response changes.
- **Cumulative damage.** Each thermal cycle uses some capability. Programmes monitor this with cumulative damage models
  and with surveillance tests of aged motors.

### Differences from REF·DB v1

<Aside type="note" title="Two corrections in this version">
  **Pressurisation strain.** v1 used $\tfrac{p}{2G}\,\tfrac{b^2}{b^2 - a^2}$. This is the bore strain of a grain with
  *no case around it*. For a soft composite propellant, it predicts strains of hundreds of per cent. In a case-bonded
  grain, the case controls the bore strain, as in the formula above.

  **Relaxation credit.** v1 decreased the cool-down bore strain by a viscoelastic relaxation factor. In a case-bonded
  grain, the bore strain is kinematically imposed. Thus relaxation decreases the stress but not the strain. The page now
  applies relaxation only where it is correct: to the bond stress, through the relaxed modulus.
</Aside>

## On the drawing

- **Grain geometry:** port profile, fillet radii and web, with the tolerance on each. Fillet radii at star points and
  slot tips are structural features, not cosmetic features.
- **Liner, insulation and inhibitor:** specifications, thicknesses, and which surfaces are inhibited.
- **Bond and void acceptance:** the NDT method (usually radiography or CT for voids, cracks and debonds) and its
  acceptance criteria.
- **Stress-relief features:** the flap geometry, and the release treatment that lets the flaps separate.
- **Temperature limits:** give the storage and firing temperatures in the motor specification, because the structural
  margin depends on them.
