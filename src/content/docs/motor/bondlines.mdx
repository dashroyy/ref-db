## Why this matters

Most solid motors bond the grain to the case. The bond makes the case and the grain one structure: the grain cannot
move in the case, and gas cannot get between them. If the bond lets go, a new surface burns. The burning area and the
pressure go up, and hot gas reaches the insulation before its time. A separation is not a cosmetic defect: it can burst
the case.

In the long cylinder, the bond has an easy job. The [grain page](../propellant-grain/#cool-down-why-the-bore-cracks)
puts its radial tension at a fraction of an MPa. The bond is weakest where it stops, and bond failures usually start
at a termination: the end of the grain, the edge of a flap, or a defect in the bondline.

## The intuition: a strip of tape

<Analogy title="Tape on a box">
  A strip of tape on a box does not fail in its middle. It fails at its edge, where you start to peel it. Pull the tape
  along its length, and the load goes into the box over a short length near the end; the rest of the strip carries
  almost nothing. A bonded grain is the same. The cylinder holds, and failures start where the bond stops.
</Analogy>

## The layers from the case to the propellant

| Layer | Its job | Bonded to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Case | The pressure vessel | The insulation, with an adhesive, or by curing the insulation in place |
| Insulation | Keeps the heat of the burn off the case; often also makes the flaps | The case and the liner |
| Liner | A thin rubber layer that the propellant cures against. It makes the bond, and it stops liquids from moving between the propellant and the insulation | The insulation and the propellant |
| Propellant | The grain | The liner |
| Inhibitor | Stops burning on surfaces that must not burn, such as the ends of the grain | The propellant |

Each interface must be stronger than the propellant itself. A good bond, pulled to failure, tears the propellant and
leaves propellant on both faces: a **cohesive** failure. A bond that fails at an interface, an **adhesive** failure,
shows a process problem. Examples are a contaminated or badly prepared surface, a liner that was cured too far or not
far enough before the cast, or a liquid that moved across the bond.

## How a bond is tested

- **Tensile bond tests:** a block of propellant cast on a liner and insulation, pulled at right angles to the
  bondline.
- **Peel tests:** the insulation peeled from the propellant at 90° or 180°. They show how a crack runs along the bond.
- **Shear tests:** a double lap joint, for the bond in shear.
- **Several rates and temperatures.** The bond is viscoelastic, like the propellant
  ([Viscoelasticity](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/)). Its strength at the cold end and at the rate of ignition
  is not its strength at room temperature.
- **Samples cast with each motor** (witness panels), from the same batches of liner and propellant, and some of them
  tested after ageing ([Storage & ageing](../../verify/storage-ageing/)).
- **Record the failure mode** of each sample, not only the stress. An adhesive failure is a warning, even at a high
  stress.

## The bond in the long cylinder

The [grain page](../propellant-grain/#cool-down-why-the-bore-cracks) derives the cool-down loads. For a grain with a bore
radius $a$, an outer radius $b$ and $m = b/a$:

- **Cool-down** pulls the grain off the case with a radial tension $\sigma_r(b) = E\,(m^2 - 1)\,(\alpha_p - \alpha_c)\,|\Delta T|$,
  with the relaxed modulus $E$.
- **Pressure** at ignition pushes the grain onto the case, so in the cylinder the bond is in compression.
- **Acceleration** along the axis: the bond carries the weight of the web,

$$
\tau = \frac{\rho\,n\,g\,(b^2 - a^2)}{2b}
$$

- **Slump.** In storage, the grain creeps down the case under its own weight. With the relaxed shear modulus $G$, the
  bore moves relative to the case by

$$
w(a) = \frac{\rho\,n\,g}{2G} \left[ \frac{b^2 - a^2}{2} - a^2 \ln\frac{b}{a} \right]
$$

  The slump grows with the square of the size. It is nothing for a small motor, and centimetres for a large motor that
  stands on its end.

## The end of the grain

When the grain cools, it also tries to shrink along its length. The case holds its outer surface, so the volume that
the propellant loses must go into the bore and along the axis. Relative to the case, a grain held at its outer surface
wants to shrink along the axis by

$$
\varepsilon_z = \frac{3\,\Delta\alpha\,\Delta T\,(1 + m^2)}{3 + m^2}
$$

which is 2.5 times the free shrinkage of a bar for $m = 3$. In the long cylinder, the bond stops this. At the ends it
cannot: the end of the grain slides toward the middle, and the bond near the end carries the force.

A **shear-lag** model, the same as for a bonded lap joint, gives the size of the end zone. The grain is a bar with the
axial stiffness $E(1 + m^2/3)\,A$ on a shear spring. The spring is the bond layer (thickness $h$, shear modulus $G_b$)
and the web of propellant itself, in series:

$$
\lambda^2 = \frac{2\pi b}{E\,(1 + m^2/3)\,A\,c}
\qquad
c = c_\text{web} + \frac{h}{G_b}
\qquad
u_\text{end} = \frac{|\varepsilon_z| \tanh(\lambda L/2)}{\lambda}
\qquad
\tau_\text{end} = \frac{u_\text{end}}{c}
$$

$u_\text{end}$ is how far the end moves relative to the case, and $\tau_\text{end}$ is the average shear on the bond at
the end. Two results follow:

- **The web is the soft spring, not the bond layer.** In the 300 mm grain, the web gives more than 90% of the
  compliance. A thicker bond layer changes the end shear only a little.
- **The end movement grows with the size of the grain,** as $1/\lambda$ does, but the end shear does not grow much. The
  end of a large grain moves by tens of millimetres.

The shear lag gives the average stress near the end. At the edge itself, where the bond meets the free face of the
grain, elastic theory gives a stress with no upper limit, like the stress at the tip of a crack (Bogy). Its value
depends on the angle between the bond and the free face, on the mesh of a finite element model, and on how the
propellant relaxes there. Get the stress near the edge from FEA of the real grain end, and compare it with bond tests
that have the same kind of edge. The calculator takes this as a concentration factor on the long-cylinder values.

## Stress-relief flaps

<BondFlap />

A stress-relief flap moves the end of the bond away from the propellant.

- **What it is.** A sheet of insulation, part of the dome insulation at its root. The propellant is bonded to its inside.
  A release layer stops the outside of the flap from bonding to the dome.
- **How it works.** When the grain shrinks, the flap opens. The propellant takes the shrinkage as a movement, not as a
  stress, and the bond ends at the root of the flap, in rubber. Rubber is much stronger and more flexible than the
  propellant.
- **Size the flap for the end movement.** At the coldest temperature, it must open by $u_\text{end}$, plus the shrinkage
  of the propellant along the flap itself, without tearing at its root. Give the root a large radius.
- **The gap is part of the chamber.** At ignition, gas fills it. The flap and the dome insulation must stand the heat
  there, and the gap must not let gas flow along the bondline.
- **The release layer must work, and stay where it is.** A release agent that moves into the bond weakens it. One that
  does not work leaves the flap bonded to the dome, and then the flap does nothing. Radiography of the motor at a cold
  temperature shows whether each flap opened.

## Ignition, cycling and ageing

- **Ignition** pushes the grain onto the case, so the bond in the cylinder is in compression. But at the ends of the
  grain and in the flap gaps, the gas pushes on surfaces that no bond holds, and it can force open a small separation
  at a termination. Treat a separation at a termination as a crack. The flow along the port also pushes the grain
  toward the nozzle. It acts like an acceleration, and the
  [Erosive burning page](../erosive-burning/#the-push-on-the-grain) gives its size in g.
- **Temperature cycling** works a separation at a termination like a crack in fatigue. It can grow a little with each
  cycle.
- **Ageing** changes the bond. Liquids move across the interfaces, and the propellant near the bond can get harder.
  Surveillance tests of bond samples and of motors that are cut up follow the bond strength through the storage life
  ([Storage & ageing](../../verify/storage-ageing/)).

## Inspection of the bondlines

- **Radiography and computed tomography** find separations between the liner and the propellant, and voids near the
  bond. A separation shows best when the X-ray beam runs along it (tangential radiography). Look at the terminations
  and the flaps from the angles where they line up with the beam.
- **Ultrasound** finds unbonded areas between a metal case and its insulation, from the outside of the case.
- **The acceptance criteria,** the size and the location of a separation that you can accept, come from your programme
  and its fracture analysis. A separation at a termination is worse than the same separation in the middle of the
  cylinder: it is open to the port at ignition
  ([Flaws in the grain](../../make/grain-flaws/#an-unbond-at-the-end-of-the-bond-is-the-worst)).
- See [NDT](../../make/ndt/) for the methods and their limits.

## Worked example: the bonds of the 300 mm grain

The grain is the HTPB grain of the [grain example](../propellant-grain/#worked-example-htpb-grain-in-the-d6ac-case): a
300 mm D6AC case with a 100 mm bore ($m = 3$), a relaxed modulus of 3.6 MPa, $\alpha_p = 100$ and
$\alpha_c = 12.3$ µm/m·K, stress-free at 60 °C and coldest at −40 °C. It is 980 mm long. The bond layer is 3 mm thick,
with $G_b = 1.5$ MPa, and the density is 1800 kg/m³ (illustrative). The criteria are **for this example only**: a bond
strength of 0.6 MPa in tension and 0.5 MPa in shear at the cold end, a factor of safety of 1.5, and a concentration of
3 at the termination of a grain end without a flap. Push **Load worked example**.

1. **Long cylinder.** The radial tension is $3.6 \times 8 \times 87.7 \times 10^{-6} \times 100 = 0.25$ MPa, as on the
   grain page. MS = $0.6 / (1.5 \times 0.25) - 1 = +0.58$.
2. **Free axial shrinkage.** $3 \times 87.7 \times 10^{-6} \times 100 \times 10/12 = 2.19\%$ relative to the case, 2.5
   times that of a free bar.
3. **End zone.** The axial modulus is $3.6 \times 4 = 14.4$ MPa. The web gives a compliance of 25.6 mm/MPa and the bond
   layer $3 / 1.5 = 2.0$ mm/MPa, so $1/\lambda = 163$ mm.
4. **End movement and shear.** $u_\text{end} = 2.19\% \times 163 = 3.55$ mm, and $\tau_\text{end} = 3.55 / 27.6 = 0.129$
   MPa.
5. **At the termination,** with the concentration of 3, the tension is 0.76 MPa and the shear is 0.39 MPa. MS = −0.47 in
   tension and −0.14 in shear. **The bond fails at the end of the grain, and passes in the cylinder.**
6. **With a flap,** the end of the bond moves into the insulation, and the end of the propellant can move. The flap
   must open by 3.6 mm at −40 °C, plus the shrinkage of the propellant along the flap.
7. **Acceleration.** The bond carries 1.18 kPa for each g: 0.018 MPa at 15 g. Even at the termination, the shear
   margin is +5.3. The bond carries the 111 kg web in flight with no difficulty.
8. **Slump.** Standing on its end in storage, the bore moves 0.05 mm: nothing. A grain 3.7 m across with a 1 m bore
   and the same propellant slumps 9.3 mm.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the example shows">
  The same bond passes in the cylinder and fails at the end of the grain, by the concentration factor alone. Every
  number that you can check by hand is comfortable. The risk is at the termination, and only FEA and tests can size
  it. This is why case-bonded grains have flaps, and why the inspection of a motor looks hardest at the ends of the
  grain.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Long-cylinder and one-dimensional values">
  These relations size the bond away from its edges and give the scale of the end zone. They do not give the stress at
  a termination, which needs FEA of the real grain end and bond tests with the same kind of edge.
</Aside>

- **The edge of the bond:** the stress at the termination, and fracture mechanics for a separation there.
- **The shape of the grain end:** the dome, the end face and the flap change the stress field.
- **Viscoelastic history:** the rate of the cool-down, temperature cycling and the damage that they add up.
- **Pressure at the terminations** during ignition, and the gas in the flap gaps.
- **Ageing** of the liner, the bond and the propellant near the bond.
- **Cartridge-loaded grains** have no case bond. Their supports and inhibitors have their own problems.

## On the drawing and in the specifications

- **The bond system:** the insulation, the liner and the adhesive by their specifications, with the surface
  preparation and the times between the steps (for example, the cure state of the liner when the propellant is cast).
- **The flaps:** their geometry, the root radius, the release layer and where it stops.
- **The bond tests** on samples cast with each motor: the rates, the temperatures, and the failure mode that you
  require.
- **The inspection of the bondlines:** the method, the views, and the acceptance criteria for separations by location.
- **The temperature limits** of storage and transport, which set $\Delta T$.
