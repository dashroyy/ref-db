## Why this matters

A guided vehicle steers by turning its thrust. A solid motor cannot be throttled, so the usual way is to turn the
whole nozzle through a few degrees about a pivot. For the 300 mm motor, 5° gives a side force of 2.7 kN. The side
force is there from the moment of ignition, when the vehicle is too slow for fins to steer.

The nozzle joint then has more jobs than a fixed joint. It must carry the blow-off load, seal the chamber, turn on
command, and stand the heat. The usual solution, the **flexible bearing**, does all four with rubber. The rubber sets
the torque that the actuators must give, and that torque can double at the cold end of the temperature range.

## The intuition: a bridge bearing

<Analogy title="The rubber pads under a bridge">
  A bridge beam sits on pads of rubber with thin steel plates moulded in. The pads carry the weight of the bridge with
  almost no movement, but they let the beam tilt and slide as it grows in the sun. The plates stop the rubber from
  bulging out, so the pad is stiff in compression. Nothing stops the rubber from shearing, so the pad stays soft in
  shear. A flexible bearing is the same idea, wrapped onto a sphere round the pivot of the nozzle.
</Analogy>

## Ways to turn the thrust

| Method | How it works | Gives | Costs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Movable nozzle on a flexible bearing | Laminated rubber and shims let the whole nozzle turn about a pivot | No thrust lost in the exhaust; large angles; no sliding seal | Actuators and their power; a torque that rises in the cold; the bearing is part of the pressure boundary |
| Movable nozzle on a ball and socket | A sliding spherical joint with a seal | A low torque at low pressure | Friction that grows with the pressure; a sliding seal in the heat |
| Fluid injection into the exit cone | Gas or liquid injected through the wall of the exit cone makes a shock that turns the flow | No moving nozzle | A tank, valves and the mass of the fluid; small angles only |
| Jet vanes or jetavators | Vanes or a ring in the exhaust turn part of the flow | A fast response; vanes also give roll | Thrust lost while they are in the jet; they erode, so the burn must be short |

The rest of this page is about the flexible bearing, which most large movable nozzles use.

## Side force and what it costs

Turned through $\delta$, the thrust $F$ gives

$$
F_\text{side} = F \sin\delta
\qquad
M = F L \sin\delta
\qquad
\Delta F_\text{axial} = F\,(1 - \cos\delta)
$$

where $L$ is the distance from the pivot to the centre of gravity of the vehicle. The axial loss is small: 0.38% at
5°, and only while the nozzle is turned.

Part of the angle is not available to steer. The vehicle must also hold itself against the misalignment of the motor.
A thrust line offset by $d_m$ at the centre of gravity, and a lateral offset $y_{cg}$ of the centre of gravity, need

$$
\delta_\text{trim} = \arcsin \frac{d_m + y_{cg}}{L}
$$

for the whole burn. The alignment stack on the [Tolerance stack](../../drawing/tolerance-stack/#the-nozzle-alignment)
page gives $d_m$. One movable nozzle also cannot control roll: roll needs two nozzles, vanes, or a separate system.

## The flexible bearing

<MovableNozzle />

The bearing is a stack of thin rubber pads and metal or composite shims, bonded together in a mould. Each layer is part
of a sphere centred on the pivot, which is on the motor axis. One end ring bolts to the case and the other to the
nozzle. The nozzle turns about the pivot because each pad shears a little.

### How it carries the load

The bearing carries the blow-off load of the nozzle. It comes from the same momentum balance as on the
[Nozzle](../nozzle/#loads-on-the-nozzle) page, with the pressure taken to act inside the middle radius $r_m$ of the
bearing:

$$
F_{ax} = (p_c - p_a)\,\pi r_m^2 - F
$$

A pressure test with the throat plugged has no thrust, so the bearing then carries the full pressure load. The bearing
must stay in compression at all times, because the bond of rubber to metal is weak in tension. Over the band, the pads
carry a pressure

$$
\sigma = \frac{F_{ax}}{\pi\,(r_o^2 - r_i^2)}
$$

in the middle of the stack. A thin rubber layer bonded on both faces cannot bulge much. Its **shape factor** $S$, the
loaded area divided by the free area at its edges, sets how stiff it is in compression. For a long strip (Gent):

$$
E_c = 4G\,(1 + S^2)
$$

in series with the bulk modulus of the rubber. A pad 1 mm thick and 30 mm wide has $S = 15$, and it is about 900
times stiffer in compression than in shear. The bulge also shears the rubber at the bonded edges:

$$
\gamma_c = \frac{1.5\,S\,\sigma}{G\,(1 + S^2)} \approx \frac{1.5\,\sigma}{G\,S}
$$

### How it turns

Each pad turns through its share of $\delta$. With $n$ pads of thickness $t_e$ at a radius $R$ from the pivot, the
shear strain from the turn is

$$
\gamma_r = \frac{R\,\delta}{n\,t_e}
$$

in the plane of the turn. The torque comes from the same shear. For one pad between the polar angles $\theta_1$ and
$\theta_2$, with $c = \cos\theta$, the stiffness is

$$
k = \frac{\pi G R^4}{t_e} \left[ (c_1 - c_2) + \frac{c_1^3 - c_2^3}{3} \right]
$$

and the pads act in series: $1/K = \sum 1/k$.

**The pad nearest the pivot works hardest.** All the pads carry the same load and the same torque, but the innermost
pad is the smallest. It carries the highest pressure with the lowest shape factor, and it turns the most, because
$k$ grows as $R^4$. The calculator takes each pad at its own radius.

### The shims

The shims stop the rubber from bulging, so the rubber pulls them toward its free edges. In a long strip, each face of a
shim adds an in-plane stress of about

$$
\sigma_s \approx 0.75\,\sigma\,\frac{t_e}{t_s}
$$

in tension at the middle of the band. Near the inner edge, the same pull makes the circumference of the shim shorter,
so there the shim is in hoop compression. Codes for bridge bearings size their steel plates with the same physics
(EN 1337-3). FEA of the real bearing finds the peak.

### Three numbers set the design

- **The total rubber thickness** $n\,t_e$ sets the strain from the turn and the spring torque. More rubber gives less
  strain and less torque, but more axial movement under the load.
- **The pad thickness** $t_e$ sets the shape factor. For the same total rubber, thinner pads cut the bulge strain and
  the axial movement, and the stiffness does not change. They need more shims.
- **The radius** $R$ and the width of the band give the area that carries the load, and the torque grows as $R^4$.

## The torque budget

The actuator must turn the nozzle against these torques (NASA SP-8114 gives the full list):

| Torque | From | Grows with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spring | The shear of the rubber: $K\delta$ | The angle, and $G$, so the cold |
| Centre of pressure | The blow-off load acts on the nozzle axis at a distance $e$ from the pivot: $F_{ax}\,e\,\sin\delta$ | The angle and the pressure |
| Acceleration | The mass $m$ of the moving parts, with its centre of gravity $d$ aft of the pivot, under the acceleration $a$ of the vehicle: $m\,a\,d\,\sin\delta$ | The angle and the acceleration |
| Inertia | The moving parts: $I\,\ddot\delta$ | How fast the command changes |
| Friction and hysteresis | Losses in the rubber, or friction in a sliding bearing | The direction of motion |

A positive $e$ or $d$ (aft of the pivot) pulls the nozzle back to the middle. Forward of the pivot, the term pushes
the nozzle out, and the actuator must hold it. The actuator force is the total torque divided by the actuator arm
$a_r$. The stroke, $2\,a_r \sin\delta$ for $\pm\delta$, and the rate for the required slew set its power.

**Size the actuator at the cold end.** Rubber gets stiffer as it cools toward its glass transition
([Viscoelasticity](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/)). Natural rubber also stiffens if it crystallises during a long
cold soak. The spring torque follows $G$. Keep the bearing warm, or size the actuator for the cold torque.

## Heat and the boot

The rubber of the bearing must stay cool. A **boot** of flexible insulation covers the inner edge of the bearing and the
gap to the nozzle, and it moves with them. After burnout, heat soaks back from the hot nozzle into the bearing
([Transient thermal](../transient-thermal/#soak-back)). Check that the rubber and its bonds stay below their limits for
as long as the bearing must work.

## How a bearing is qualified

- **Torque tests at pressure.** The bearing turns through the duty cycle in a fixture filled with fluid at the chamber
  pressure, at the hot and the cold temperature. The tests give the spring torque, the hysteresis, the centre of
  pressure and the movement of the pivot.
- **Proof pressure,** at the middle position and turned, with an inspection of the bonds after it.
- **Inspection of the bonds** after moulding: ultrasound or radiography for voids and unbonded areas between the pads
  and the shims.
- **Static firing** with a vector duty cycle ([Static firing](../../verify/static-firing/)): the bearing, the boot and
  the actuators together, in real heat and pressure.
- **Ageing** of the rubber and its bonds over the storage life ([Storage & ageing](../../verify/storage-ageing/)).

## Worked example: a flexible bearing for the 300 mm motor

The motor is the 300 mm motor of the [nozzle example](../nozzle/#worked-example-the-300-mm-motors-nozzle) at its MEOP
of 10 MPa, with 31.5 kN of thrust. Its nozzle gets a flexible bearing for ±5°. All the values of the bearing are
illustrative: pads between the radii 55 and 85 mm, centred 130 mm from the pivot, and a rubber with $G$ = 0.6 MPa warm
and 1.2 MPa at the cold end. The criteria (an allowable shear strain of 3.0, a factor of 1.5 on the yield of the 4130
shims, and a 12 kN actuator) are **for this example only**. Push **Load worked example**: it loads the first try.

1. **Blow-off load.** $(10 - 0.101) \times \pi \times 70^2 - 31\,500 = 120.9$ kN in firing, and 152.4 kN in a pressure
   test.
2. **First try: 12 pads of 1.4 mm with 1.0 mm shims.** The pad nearest the pivot is at 116.8 mm. It carries 11.35 MPa
   with $S = 11.6$. The bulge shears it by 2.43, and the turn of 5° adds 0.89. The total is **3.33, over the allowable
   of 3.0 (MS = −0.10)**. The turn is not the problem: the bulge is.
3. **Second try: 16 pads of 1.05 mm with 0.8 mm shims.** Change these three inputs. The rubber is the same 16.8 mm
   thick, so the stiffness hardly changes (135.5 N·m/°). $S$ goes up to 15.4, the bulge strain goes down to 1.86, and
   the total is **2.77: MS = +0.08**. The nozzle moves aft by 0.36 mm instead of 0.57 mm, and the stack is 28.8 mm
   high, 1 mm more than before.
4. **Shims.** The bulge gives 22 MPa in firing and 28 MPa in the pressure test, against $435 / 1.5 = 290$ MPa. The
   stress does not set the thickness of these shims. Their shape after moulding does.
5. **Torque at 5°.** The spring gives 678 N·m warm and 1355 N·m cold. The centre of pressure, 4 mm aft of the pivot,
   adds 42 N·m. The 9 kg of moving parts at 10 g add 8.5 N·m, the inertia for 1° at 6 Hz adds 4.0 N·m, and friction
   adds 50 N·m. The total is 1460 N·m cold.
6. **Actuator.** On a 150 mm arm, the force is 9.73 kN cold and 5.22 kN warm: MS = +0.23 against 12 kN. The stroke is
   ±13.1 mm, the rate is 79 mm/s at 30°/s, and the peak power is 0.76 kW.
7. **Authority.** At 5°, the side force is 2.75 kN, a moment of 1.92 kN·m about the centre of gravity 700 mm forward.
   The 0.45 mm of the alignment stack and a 1 mm offset of the centre of gravity need 0.12° of trim: 2.4% of the
   angle.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the example shows">
  The limit of the rubber came from the load, not from the turn. Thinner pads fixed it, and the torque did not change.
  The actuator is sized at the cold end, where the spring torque is twice the warm value. The misalignment that the
  tolerance stack holds to 0.45 mm uses only 2.4% of the angle here. A vehicle without thrust vector control cannot
  trim at all, and on such a vehicle the same 0.45 mm goes straight into its dispersion.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="A screen for the first layout">
  These are the first-order relations of a flexible bearing. They size a layout to test and to analyse. They do not
  replace either.
</Aside>

- **Spring torque and pressure.** The spring torque of a real bearing changes with the pressure, and the pivot moves as
  the stack compresses. Torque tests at pressure give both.
- **The stress analysis of the bearing.** The shims near their inner edge, the end rings, the bonds and the rubber at
  the edges need FEA with a hyperelastic model of the rubber ([FEA cards](../../verify/fea-cards/)).
- **Fatigue and ageing** of the rubber and its bonds, over the duty cycle and the storage life.
- **The boot and the heat** in the bearing ([Transient thermal](../transient-thermal/)).
- **The servo:** the stiffness of the actuator and its mount, the load resonance and the control loop.
- **The other methods** (fluid injection, vanes, a ball and socket) have their own design rules. NASA SP-8114 covers
  them.

## On the drawing

- **The pivot point and the sphere radii** as basic dimensions from the datums of the case and the nozzle; the middle
  (null) position of the nozzle and its alignment.
- **The rubber compound** by its specification, its shear modulus at the temperature limits, and the tests of each
  batch.
- **The shims:** the material, the thickness and its tolerance, and the surface preparation for the bond.
- **The bond system** (primer and adhesive), and the bond tests on samples moulded with each bearing.
- **The bearing tests:** proof, the torque test at pressure and temperature, and the inspection of the bonds.
- **The actuator attachment:** the brackets, the arm, the stroke and its stops.
