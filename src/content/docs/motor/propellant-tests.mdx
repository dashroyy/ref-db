## Why this matters

- **Every structural check of a grain compares an analysis with a test.** The bore at a cold ignition, the bond at a
  termination and a crack at the bore are each compared with a capability from tests
  ([Propellant grain](../propellant-grain/#part-2-structural-integrity), [Bondlines](../bondlines/),
  [Flaws in the grain](../../make/grain-flaws/)).
- **There is no single capability.** It changes with the rate, the temperature, the stress state, the pressure and the
  age of the propellant.
- **The margins of a grain are often small,** so the steps from a test result to the number in the margin decide the
  answer.
- **The [Propellant page](../propellant/#part-2-mechanical-properties) explains why** the properties behave as they do.
  This page gives the test programme, and the steps from the results to an allowable.

## The intuition: designing for the weather

<Analogy title="Designing for the weather">
  One thermometer reading does not tell you the climate. You collect readings over the seasons and the hours, put them
  on one chart, and design the house for the cold extreme, not for the average. Propellant capability is the same:
  tests over rates and temperatures, put on one master curve, and the design uses its lower bound at the condition of
  each load.
</Analogy>

## The test programme

| Test | What it gives | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uniaxial tension at a constant rate, over a matrix of temperatures and rates | $\sigma_m$, $\varepsilon_m$, $\varepsilon_b$ and the initial modulus | The capability master curve and the failure envelope; the margins of the bore |
| Stress relaxation at several temperatures | The relaxation modulus and the shift factor $a_T$ | The relaxed and the ignition moduli ([Propellant](../propellant/#explorer)) |
| Tension under superimposed pressure | The capability with dewetting held back | A credit at ignition, where the programme accepts one |
| Strip biaxial | The capability under strain in two directions | The correction from uniaxial tests to the bore |
| Poker chip: a thin disc bonded between two plates | Failure under triaxial tension | The grain near the bond and at terminations |
| Dilatometer | The bulk modulus and the thermal expansion | The pressure and cool-down strains |
| Bond tests: tension, peel and shear | Bond strength and the failure mode | [Bondlines](../bondlines/#how-a-bond-is-tested) |
| Fracture tests | The fracture energy and the rate of crack growth | [Flaws in the grain](../../make/grain-flaws/#a-crack-at-the-bore-grows) |
| Cyclic and constant-strain tests | Damage, and the time to failure under a held strain | [Thermal cycles and cumulative damage](../../verify/thermal-cycles/) |
| Aged specimens | The change of each property with time | End-of-life margins ([Storage & ageing](../../verify/storage-ageing/)) |
| Hardness and density | Quick checks of a batch | Batch acceptance |

- **The specimens come from the propellant of the motor:** blocks cast with the motor batch, or pieces cut from a
  motor that is taken apart. They are machined and tested under the explosive-safety rules of the facility.
- **Condition each specimen** at the test temperature until it is soaked through, and control the humidity. A composite
  propellant can absorb moisture, and moisture changes its properties.
- **Test replicates:** at least three to five at each condition, and more where the margin is small.

## The tensile test

- **The specimen.** The JANNAF test methods use a dogbone: wide tabs for the grips and a narrow gauge section
  (CPIA Publication 21). Their quick Class C method takes the strain as the crosshead travel over an effective gauge
  length of 68.5 mm; the more accurate methods measure the strain in the gauge section.
- **The strain rate** is the crosshead speed over the effective gauge length: 50 mm/min over 68.5 mm is 0.012 per
  second.
- **The results:** the maximum stress $\sigma_m$, the strain $\varepsilon_m$ at it, the strain at break
  $\varepsilon_b$, and the initial modulus. The programme states which strain its margins use.
- **The test matrix** goes from the coldest to the hottest condition of the motor, and from slow rates near those of a
  cool-down to fast rates near those of ignition. A cold ignition is both fast and cold, so it needs fast tests at cold
  temperatures.

## From the tests to a master curve

- **Shift each test to its reduced rate:** $\dot\varepsilon_r = \dot\varepsilon\,a_T$, with the WLF shift
  $\log_{10} a_T = -C_1\,(T - T_s)/(C_2 + T - T_s)$. Cold and fast have the same effect, so a cold test moves to a
  higher reduced rate.
- **Plot the capability against the log of the reduced rate.** For a composite propellant, the strain at maximum
  stress follows a broad curve: low at very slow rates, where the binder is soft and weak, highest in the middle, and
  low again at fast rates, where the binder becomes glassy.
- **Fit a smooth curve through the tests,** and measure the scatter about it.
- **Read each load at its own reduced rate:** a cool-down over hours, a daily cycle, and an ignition over tens of
  milliseconds, each at its own temperature.
- **Stay inside the data.** A fit is a poor guide outside the rates of the tests, and the cold ignition is often near
  the edge.
- **Stress has its own master curve.** The stress and the strain at failure together form the failure envelope
  (Smith, 1963), which programmes with stress criteria use.

## From the curve to an allowable

- **Take a statistical lower bound:** the curve less $k$ times the scatter, with $k$ for the fraction and the
  confidence that your programme specifies, as for the A- and B-basis values of metals
  ([Allowables](../../materials/allowables/)). For a fitted curve the bound is wider away from the centre of the data,
  so a load at the edge of the tests gets a larger $k$.
- **Correct for the stress state.** The bore is strained in two directions, and the biaxial capability is lower than the
  uniaxial. Strip-biaxial tests give the ratio.
- **Correct for the age.** Use the capability at the end of life, from accelerated and real-time ageing and from
  surveillance.
- **Take a credit for pressure only with evidence.** Pressure holds dewetting back, so the capability at ignition is
  higher than in a test at one atmosphere. Take the credit only from tests under pressure that your programme accepts.
- **Then the margin** against the analysis strain, with your factor of safety:

$$
\text{MS} = \frac{\varepsilon_\text{allowable}}{\text{SF}\;\varepsilon_\text{analysis}} - 1
$$

## Worked example: an allowable for the 300 mm grain

The tests are the eight illustrative tensile tests of the calculator, from 74 °C to −54 °C and from 0.5 to 500 mm/min.
The shift uses $T_s = -20$ °C and the general WLF constants, as on the Propellant page. The loads are those of the
[grain example](../propellant-grain/#worked-example-htpb-grain-in-the-d6ac-case): 16.1% at a cold ignition at −40 °C,
with a strain rate of about 2 per second, and 10.4% from the cool-down to −40 °C in storage over eight hours,
$3.6 \times 10^{-6}$ per second. The values are **for this example only**: a biaxial ratio of 0.85, 75% of the
capability left at the end of life (as on the [Storage & ageing](../../verify/storage-ageing/) page), no pressure
credit, a factor of safety of 1.5, and the B-basis bound. Push **Load worked example**.

1. **The shift.** At −40 °C, $\log_{10} a_T = 8.86 \times 20 / 81.6 = 2.17$, so the cold ignition is at a reduced rate of
   $2 \times 10^{2.17} = 297$ per second. The test at −40 °C and 500 mm/min is at $10^{1.26}$, and the fastest test, at
   −54 °C, at $10^{3.54}$.
2. **The fit.** The quadratic through the eight tests peaks at 43.9% at a reduced rate of $10^{-1.13}$ per second, with a
   scatter of 1.15% about the curve (5 degrees of freedom).
3. **The cold ignition.** The curve gives 39.3%: almost the 39.6% that the grain page assumed. On this mean, the margin
   is +0.63.
4. **The lower bound.** The cold ignition is near the fast end of the data, so $k = 3.36$, against 2.58 for a plain
   sample of eight. The B-basis bound is 35.5%.
5. **The corrections.** × 0.85 for the biaxial bore gives 30.2%, and × 0.75 for the end of life 22.6%. The analysis needs
   $1.5 \times 16.1 = 24.2\%$. **MS = −0.06: the grain fails at a cold ignition.**
6. **Cold storage passes.** The cool-down is at a reduced rate of $10^{-3.27}$, near the middle of the data: 42.3% on the
   curve, 38.7% as the bound, and 24.7% after the corrections, against 15.6%. MS = +0.58.
7. **The ways back.** A credit of 1.10 for the pressure of ignition, from tests under pressure, gives +0.03. Tests near
   the ignition rate would make the bound tighter. A warmer minimum firing temperature lowers both the strain and the
   reduced rate of the ignition.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the example shows">
  The grain that passes at +0.64 on the grain page fails at −0.06 here, with the same strain and almost the same mean
  capability. Nothing is wrong with either analysis. The statistical bound, the biaxial state and the end of life each
  take a modest share, and together they take 42% of the capability. Know which of these steps your programme applies
  before you report a margin.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="A screen of the method">
  The calculator shows the method on a small set of illustrative tests. A real allowable comes from a full
  characterisation of your propellant, with the methods and the statistics that your programme specifies.
</Aside>

- **One scatter for all rates.** Real scatter changes with the rate and the temperature.
- **The WLF constants.** Fit them to your relaxation tests: the general constants are only a start, and ageing can
  move them.
- **Strain criteria only.** Stress criteria and the failure envelope are not included. For the cumulative damage of
  temperature cycles and long storage, see [Thermal cycles and cumulative damage](../../verify/thermal-cycles/).
- **A specimen is not the grain.** The cure, the residual stress and the size of a specimen from a block differ from
  those in a grain. Correlate with motors that are taken apart.
- **The form of the fit.** A quadratic is a convenient fit, not a law: use the form that your data supports.

## On the drawing and in the specifications

- **The propellant specification:** the acceptance tests of each batch (for example, $\sigma_m$ and
  $\varepsilon_m$ at one condition, the density and the hardness), with their limits.
- **The characterisation:** the test matrix, the methods, the number of replicates, and the report.
- **The allowables:** the basis, the corrections, and how the grain analysis applies them.
- **The surveillance plan:** the specimens that are kept with the motors, and the schedule of their tests.
