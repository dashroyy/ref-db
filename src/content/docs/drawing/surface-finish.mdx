## Why this matters

The surface is where a part touches its environment. A seal face that is too rough leaks. A seal face with a scratch
across it leaks, even if its Ra is correct. A fatigue-critical fillet with tool marks across the load direction cracks
sooner. A press fit loses part of its interference when the roughness peaks flatten. A bond on a surface that is too
smooth, or not clean, peels.

A finer finish is not always better. It costs more, it can need an additional process, and some functions need a
rough surface. Adhesives and insulation bond better to a blasted surface. A very smooth static seal face can let the
ring slide. Thus the drawing must give the correct finish, not the finest finish.

<Analogy title="Sandpaper grades">
  You can feel the difference between coarse sandpaper and fine sandpaper, but your finger cannot give a number. Two
  sheets can also feel the same and still be different: one has a few sharp grains, and the other has many round
  grains. Surface parameters do the same work as your finger, with numbers. Ra is the average height of the grains. Rz
  and Rmr tell you about the tallest grains, which decide how the surface scratches, seals or wears.
</Analogy>

## The profile and its parameters

A stylus instrument moves a diamond tip, usually with a 2 µm radius, across the surface and records the heights. The
result is the **primary profile**. It contains the form error, the waviness and the roughness together. A filter
separates them. The **Gaussian filter** (ISO 16610-21) calculates a mean line, which is the waviness. The difference
between the profile and the mean line is the **roughness profile**. The **cutoff** λc is the wavelength where the
filter passes 50%. Shorter waves are roughness. Longer waves are waviness.

<SurfaceProfile />

The parameters are calculated on the roughness profile, over an evaluation length that is usually five cutoffs:

$$
Ra = \frac{1}{l}\int_0^l |z(x)|\,dx
$$

$$
Rq = \sqrt{\frac{1}{l}\int_0^l z^2(x)\,dx}
$$

| Parameter | What it is | What it tells |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ra** | The mean distance of the profile from its mean line | The usual control value. It does not show the shape: a few deep scratches and many small peaks can give the same Ra |
| **Rq** | The root-mean-square height | Approximately 1.25 Ra for a random surface |
| **Rz** | The mean of the peak-to-valley heights of the five sampling lengths (ISO 4287) | Sensitive to the peaks and valleys. Approximately 4 to 7 Ra for machined surfaces. The old DIN Rz and the old ISO "ten-point" Rz are different parameters |
| **Rt** | The peak-to-valley height over the full evaluation length | The largest single feature, for example one scratch |
| **Rsk** | The skewness of the height distribution | Negative: plateaus with valleys, good for bearing and sealing. Positive: sharp peaks |
| **Rmr(c)** | The material ratio: the fraction of the length that is solid at a depth c below the highest peak | How much of the surface carries load or seals after the peaks are worn or flattened |

For a turned surface, the tool geometry gives the smallest possible roughness. With a feed $f$ and a tool nose radius
$r$:

$$
Rt \approx \frac{f^2}{8r}
\qquad
Ra \approx 0.0321\,\frac{f^2}{r}
$$

Tool wear, vibration and built-up edge make the real surface rougher, usually by 1.5 to 3 times at fine feeds.

### Equivalent values on older drawings

Older drawings and other countries use other names for the same Ra. CLA (centre-line average, UK) and AA (arithmetic
average, US) are Ra. Microinches are µm × 39.37. The N grades of the old ISO 1302 double at each step. RMS is Rq, which
is approximately 1.1 to 1.25 × Ra. Rz cannot be converted exactly: approximately 4 to 7 × Ra on machined surfaces.

## Measuring it

- **Measure across the lay.** The stylus must move perpendicular to the machining marks. Along the marks, the result
  is much too low.
- **Select the cutoff.** ISO 4288 gives the cutoff from the expected value for a non-periodic profile. A cutoff that
  is too long counts waviness as roughness. A cutoff that is too short removes some of the roughness.

| Expected Ra (µm) | Expected Rz (µm) | Cutoff λc (mm) | Evaluation length (mm) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| over 0.006 to 0.02 | over 0.025 to 0.1 | 0.08 | 0.4 |
| over 0.02 to 0.1 | over 0.1 to 0.5 | 0.25 | 1.25 |
| over 0.1 to 2 | over 0.5 to 10 | 0.8 | 4 |
| over 2 to 10 | over 10 to 50 | 2.5 | 12.5 |
| over 10 to 80 | over 50 to 200 | 8 | 40 |

- **The comparison rule.** ISO 1302 and ISO 4288 use the **16% rule** as the default: the surface passes if no more
  than 16% of the measured values are above the upper limit. The **max rule** ("Ra max 0.4") accepts no value above the
  limit. ISO 21920, which replaced ISO 1302, ISO 4287 and ISO 4288, changed some defaults. It also selects the default
  cutoff from the tolerance value. Thus write the rule, and the cutoff where it is important, on the drawing. Do not
  depend on a default that the supplier can read differently.
- **Other methods.** Optical areal methods (ISO 25178, parameters such as Sa and Sz) measure an area and do not touch
  the surface. Comparison specimens are sufficient for a quick check on the shop floor, but not for acceptance of a
  critical surface. Replica compounds let you measure inside a bore that the instrument cannot get into.

## The finish that each function needs

The values below are typical screening values. The seal maker, the bearing maker or the requirements of your programme
give the real limits.

| Function on motor hardware | Typical Ra (µm) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Static O-ring face, gas or vacuum | 0.2–0.4 | Circular lay (C). No radial scratch across the seal path |
| Static O-ring face, liquid | 0.4–0.8 | Circular lay. A finer face is not always better |
| Dynamic seal counterface | 0.1–0.4 | Also limit the peaks, for example Rmr at least 50–70% near the top |
| O-ring groove walls and root | 0.8–1.6 | Up to Ra 3.2 on the side walls of static grooves |
| Rolling-bearing seat on a shaft | 0.4–0.8 | Housing bores Ra 1.6. Precision bearings need the values of the bearing maker |
| Press fit or shrink fit | 0.8–1.6 | The peaks flatten on assembly and decrease the interference |
| Fatigue-critical fillet | 0.2–0.8 | Lay along the principal stress. See [Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/) |
| Bonded or insulated surface | 1.6–6.3 | Grit blasted and clean. The process specification is more important than the Ra |
| Bolt bearing face | 0.8–3.2 | Rougher faces embed more and lose preload ([Bolted joints](../../joints/bolted-joints/)) |
| General machined surface | 1.6–3.2 | The usual default in the title block |

### Seal faces

A static O-ring fills the small valleys of the surface. Thus the typical limit is Ra 0.8 µm for liquids and Ra 0.4 µm
for gases. The **lay** is as important as the Ra. A circular lay on a face seal, or a lay around the bore of a radial
seal, makes no path across the seal. A radial scratch is a leak path that Ra does not show, because one scratch has
almost no effect on an average. Specify the lay, specify "no radial scratches", and inspect the face visually.

### Bearing seats and fits

On a fit, the measured size includes the peaks. When the parts are assembled, the peaks flatten. DIN 7190-1 estimates
the loss of interference as:

$$
\Delta U = 0.8\,(Rz_{inner} + Rz_{outer})
$$

On a small fit with a fine tolerance, this loss can be a large part of the interference. On a clearance fit, the peaks
wear in the first hours of operation, and the clearance increases. See [Fits & limits](../fits-and-limits/).

### Fatigue-critical fillets

Fatigue cracks usually start at the surface. Machining marks are small notches. The surface factor of Marin
([Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/)) decreases from approximately 0.86 for a ground surface to 0.68 for a machined
surface on a 1280 MPa steel. The stronger the steel, the larger the effect. Put the lay along the principal stress, so
that the marks are not across the load. Shot peening adds compressive residual stress
([Residual stress](../../materials/residual-stress/)), but it also makes the surface rougher. Peen after the last
machining operation.

### Bonded and insulated surfaces

Adhesives, liners and insulation need a surface that is clean, chemically active and rough enough to grip. Grit
blasting gives Ra of approximately 1.6 to 6.3 µm. The Ra alone does not make a good bond. The process specification
controls the media, the pressure, the cleaning, and the maximum time between preparation and bonding. Measure blasted
surfaces with a 2.5 mm cutoff, because their Ra is usually above 2 µm.

## What each process can give

The calculator plots these ranges. The usual range is what a shop gives without special effort. The best values need
sharp tools, fine feeds, rigid setups and more time.

| Process | Usual Ra (µm) | Best Ra (µm) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sand casting | 12.5–25 | 6.3 |
| Forging | 3.2–12.5 | 1.6 |
| Investment casting | 1.6–3.2 | 0.8 |
| Powder-bed AM, as built | 6.3–12.5 | 3.2 |
| Milling | 0.8–6.3 | 0.4 |
| Turning and boring | 0.4–3.2 | 0.2 |
| Reaming | 0.4–1.6 | 0.2 |
| Grinding | 0.1–1.6 | 0.05 |
| Honing | 0.05–0.4 | 0.025 |
| Roller burnishing | 0.05–0.4 | 0.025 |
| Lapping and polishing | 0.025–0.2 | 0.0125 |

Tolerance, quantity and cost by process are on [Process & AM](../../make/process-and-am/). Each step to a finer finish usually needs an additional operation. Thus specify the fine finish only on the features
that need it, and use the general default for all other surfaces.

## The callout

<SurfaceSymbol />

- **The symbol** points to the surface, or to an extension line from it. The bar means that material removal is
  required (MRR). The circle means that material removal is not permitted (NMR).
- **Position a** gives the requirement: the parameter, the rule if it is not the default, and the value. "Ra max 0.4"
  uses the max rule. "Ra 0.4" uses the default rule of the standard on the drawing.
- **An upper and a lower limit** use U and L: "U Ra 0.8" and "L Ra 0.2".
- **The filter** can be given before the parameter, for example "0.0025-0.8/Ra 0.4" for λs 2.5 µm and λc 0.8 mm.
- **Position c** gives the process (for example "ground"), and **position d** gives the lay: = parallel to the plane
  of the view, ⊥ perpendicular, X crossed, M multidirectional, C circular, R radial, P particulate (no direction).
- **The default** for all surfaces without a symbol goes in the title block or in a general note, for example
  "All surfaces Ra 3.2 unless otherwise stated".
- **Standards.** ISO 21920-1 (which replaced ISO 1302) or ASME Y14.36 for the symbol. ISO 21920-2 and -3 (which
  replaced ISO 4287 and ISO 4288) or ASME B46.1 for the parameters and the measurement. The drawing must tell which
  standard applies.

## Worked example: the surfaces of the 300 mm motor

The starting values in each tab of the calculator are the values of this example. The limit of Ra 0.4 µm is **for this
example only**. Push **Load worked example** to apply it.

1. **The nozzle seal bore.** The radial seal of the [O-ring page](../../seals/o-ring-design/#worked-example-a-nozzle-to-case-radial-seal)
   seals combustion gas at 10 MPa. The typical finish for a static gas seal is Ra 0.2–0.4 µm with a circular lay. The
   bore is finish-turned with a feed of 0.08 mm/rev and a 0.8 mm nose radius. The kinematic roughness is Ra 0.26 µm
   (Rt 1.0 µm). The simulated surface gives Ra 0.26 µm and Rz 1.24 µm. Real tool wear can increase this to
   approximately Ra 0.4 µm. Thus the supplier must control the tool, or hone the bore. The callout is
   "MRR turned · Ra max 0.4 · lay C", plus the note "no radial scratches in the seal path".
2. **The cutoff.** The bore also has 2 µm of waviness with a 3 mm wavelength. With the correct 0.8 mm cutoff, the
   instrument gives Ra 0.26 µm. If the inspector uses a 2.5 mm cutoff, the filter keeps part of the waviness, and the
   result is **Ra 0.52 µm**. A good part fails a 0.4 µm limit. Write the cutoff on the drawing or in the inspection
   plan.
3. **The acceptance rule.** Twelve readings around the bore are 0.28 to 0.42 µm, and one reading is above 0.4 µm. The
   first reading (0.31 µm) is more than 70% of the limit, so the inspector must take more readings. One of twelve is
   8%. The **16% rule accepts** the bore, and the **max rule rejects** it. Both decisions are correct for their rule.
   For a gas seal, a local rough area can leak. Thus this example uses the max rule, and writes it on the drawing.
4. **The actuator bracket fillet.** The 4340 bracket of the
   [Fatigue page](../../fundamentals/fatigue/#worked-example-an-actuator-bracket) lasts 6.2 service lives as machined.
   If the fillet is ground to Ra 0.4 µm, the surface factor increases from 0.68 to 0.86, and the life increases to
   **14.8 service lives**. The callout is "MRR ground · Ra 0.4 · lay =", with the lay along the bracket, parallel to the
   bending stress.
5. **The aluminium ring on the steel attach ring.** This is the ⌀310 H7/s6 fit of the
   [Fits page](../fits-and-limits/#worked-example-four-fits-on-the-motor), with an interference of 118–202 µm at 20 °C.
   The two parts are turned to Ra 1.6 µm, which gives Rz of approximately 6.3 µm. On assembly, **10 µm** of the
   interference is lost (8.5% of the smallest value), so the effective range is 108–192 µm. At +60 °C the fit was
   already −20 to +64 µm. With the smoothing loss, it becomes −30 to +54 µm, and it is more often loose. A finer finish
   (Rz 3.2 µm) loses only 5 µm. The conclusion of the Fits page does not change: pins or bolts must carry the load.
6. **The bonded skirt.** The aft skirt of the [Load paths page](../../joints/load-paths/) is bonded to the case through a
   shear ply. The case surface is grit blasted to the bonding process specification, which typically gives Ra 1.6 to
   6.3 µm. Measure it with a 2.5 mm cutoff. The drawing refers to the process specification. The Ra is a check, not the
   requirement.

## Where this stops

- **Simulated profiles.** The calculator makes typical profiles for each process. Real surfaces depend on the tool,
  the machine and the material. Use measured values for acceptance.
- **Profile parameters only.** Areal parameters (ISO 25178) and the functional parameters of ISO 13565-2 (Rk, Rpk,
  Rvk) describe some surfaces better, especially plateau-honed and run-in surfaces.
- **Typical values for functions.** They are screening values. The seal maker, the bearing maker, the adhesive
  specification and your programme give the real limits.
- **Standards change.** ISO 21920 replaced ISO 1302, ISO 4287 and ISO 4288, and some defaults changed. Check the
  edition that your drawing refers to.

## On the drawing

- **A requirement on each functional surface,** with the parameter, the rule and the value, and a general default in
  the title block.
- **The lay** where the direction is important: seal faces and fatigue-critical fillets.
- **The cutoff** where the default can be misunderstood, and the standard that applies.
- **The process** only where the function needs it, for example "ground" on a fatigue-critical fillet, or a reference
  to the surface preparation specification for a bond.
- **Notes for defects that Ra cannot see:** "no radial scratches in the seal path", "no tool marks across the
  fillet".
- **The order of operations** where it is important: finish before plating or after it, and peen after the last
  machining.
