## Why this matters

All the drawings of a motor can be correct and the assembly can still be incorrect. An O-ring groove, a bore and a pilot
can each be in tolerance, and the ring can still have too little squeeze. A case, a closure and a nozzle can each pass
inspection, and the thrust can still point away from the centre of gravity. The individual drawings do not show this.
The stack shows it. A stack is the chain of dimensions that sets one gap.

A stack answers three questions:

- What values can the gap have?
- Which tolerances have the largest effect on it?
- Must the result be correct for all assemblies, or only for almost all of them?

<Analogy title="Books on a shelf">
  Each book on a shelf is a little thicker or thinner than the catalogue says. If you take books at random, most of the
  errors cancel and the row ends near where you expect. The row is too long only if all the books are thick ones. This
  is possible, but rare. If someone sorted the books, or if all of them came from one print run, the errors add and the
  rare result occurs each time. A tolerance stack does the same three things: it adds the worst case, it adds the usual
  case, and it tells you which one your parts permit.
</Analogy>

## The loop diagram

1. **Name the result and its requirement.** The result is a gap, a squeeze or an offset. The requirement is the limit
   that it must meet.
2. **Start at one side of the gap.** Go to the other side through the parts that touch, with one arrow for each
   dimension. Select a positive direction. Arrows in the opposite direction are negative.
3. **Go through datums and contact points.** The dimensions of each part start at its datums, and parts meet where they
   touch. Thus the loop goes from datum to datum. If the drawing gives a dimension from a different feature, the loop
   must go a longer way and it collects more tolerance. This usually shows that the datums are not correct
   ([Datums & position](../datums-and-position/)).
4. **Decide the position of each clearance.** If a fit lets a part move, push the part in the direction that is worst
   for this result. The loop then goes through the contact point.
5. **Change each dimension to a mean ± an equal half-tolerance.** Give it a sensitivity: ±1 for a length in the loop,
   ±½ for a diameter whose radius is in the loop, and $\cos\theta$ for a dimension at an angle.

The [O-ring page](../../seals/o-ring-design/#worked-example-a-nozzle-to-case-radial-seal) has a nozzle seal. Push the
plug onto the far wall of the bore, and let the groove move by its runout $e$. The deepest gland is then:

$$
g = D_{bore} - \tfrac{1}{2}D_{pilot} - \tfrac{1}{2}D_{root} + e
$$

The squeeze is the ring section minus $g$. On the tight side, the bore diameter is not in the stack, because the plug
sits on the bore.

## Worst case and RSS

The result is $y = \sum s_i x_i$. Each contributor has a half-tolerance $t_i$ and a sensitivity $s_i$:

$$
t_{WC} = \sum |s_i|\,t_i
$$

$$
t_{RSS} = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum (s_i t_i)^2}
$$

- **Worst case.** The result is correct for all assemblies of in-tolerance parts, for all distributions. It increases
  in proportion to the number of contributors.
- **RSS.** This method assumes that each part is independent, normal and centred, and that its tolerance is ±3σ. It
  increases only as the square root of the number of contributors. Thus it is much smaller on long stacks. Even when all
  the assumptions are correct, approximately 0.27% of assemblies are outside the RSS band.
- **Which method to use.** This is a decision. Use the worst case when one failure is not acceptable and the assembly
  check will not find it, when the quantity is small, or when you do not have process data for the statistics. A
  statistical stack is applicable to many contributors, to large quantities, and to failures that an inspection finds
  or that are acceptable at the predicted rate.

## GD&T and fits in a stack

| On the drawing | In the stack |
| --- | --- |
| Size ⌀a–b, radius in the loop | mean $(a+b)/4$ ± $(b-a)/4$; sensitivity ½ on the diameter |
| Position ⌀t at RFS, coaxiality ⌀t, runout t | an offset of 0 ± t/2 in the direction of the loop |
| Position ⌀t at MMC, to the surface of a hole | size and position together. The surface is between the virtual and resultant conditions, so the radius is $(VC + RC)/4$ ± $(RC - VC)/4$ |
| Profile of a surface, total width t | 0 ± t/2 (equal bilateral), or the offset of the zone if it is unequal |
| Flatness, parallelism or perpendicularity t | 0 to t in a gap. As a tilt: t over the length that it acts on, moved to the lever arm |
| A clearance fit | an assembly shift. The loop goes through the contact point (diameters at ±½), or uses a shift of up to $c_{max}/2$ |
| A datum feature at MMB | an added shift, up to half the difference between its boundary and its actual size |
| Temperature | the growth of each part, $\alpha L \Delta T$, as an added arrow. Check all temperatures, not only 20 °C |

When a material condition modifier connects the size and the geometric tolerance of a feature, the two are not
independent. Use the boundaries ([GD&T](../gdt/#mmc-bonus-tolerance-and-virtual-condition)), not two rows.

## Statistical stacks: Cpk and Monte Carlo

**Cpk-based.** Real processes do not all have a Cpk of 1. For a part made at a capability $C_{pk}$, a conservative
value is $\sigma_i = t_i / (3\,C_{pk,i})$. The σ and the capability of the assembly follow, where μ is its mean:

$$
\sigma_y = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum (s_i \sigma_i)^2}
$$

$$
C_{pk,y} = \frac{\min(USL - \mu,\ \mu - LSL)}{3\,\sigma_y}
$$

Plain RSS is the special case where each $C_{pk}$ is 1. If a process is off-centre ($C_{pk} < C_p$), this method
treats the shift as random. If the shift is the same on all parts, add it as a worst case. Some parts are uniform
across their tolerance, for example because of tool wear between resets, or because nobody measures the feature. For
these parts, $\sigma = t/\sqrt{3}$, which is 1.7 times the normal value. If you do not know the capability of the
parts, a traditional allowance multiplies the RSS by 1.5 (Bender).

**Monte Carlo.** Take a sample of each part from its distribution. Calculate the result with the real function, also
if it is nonlinear (for example a squeeze as a percentage of the sampled section, or a vector sum). Then count the
failures. This method does not need a normal assumption, but it needs sufficient samples. To see a failure rate of 1 in
10,000, use some hundred thousand samples. The result is only as good as the distributions that you use.

**Correlation.** RSS and Monte Carlo assume that the parts are independent. Two features made in one setup, by one tool
or from one batch, change together. Put them in a group and add them as a worst case. The calculator does this when you
give the rows the same group letter.

## Allocating tolerances

A stack can also go in the opposite direction: from the requirement on the result to the tolerance of each part. Start
with a target $T$ on the result. Rows that you cannot change (a bought O-ring, a standard bearing) are **fixed**, and
they use their part of the target first. Then share the remainder among the other rows. Three usual methods are:

| Method | Weight of each row | Use it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scale | The present tolerance | The present balance is correct and only the size of the tolerances must change |
| By size | The ISO 286 tolerance factor $i = 0.45\sqrt[3]{D} + 0.001D$ of the size $D$ | The rows are made by similar processes. Each row gets about the same IT grade, so each process works equally hard |
| Equal effect | $1/|s_i|$ | Each row must have the same effect on the result |

With weights $w_i$, the worst-case allocation is $t_i = k\,w_i$ with $k = T_\text{free} / \sum |s_i| w_i$. The RSS
allocation uses $k = T_\text{free} / \sqrt{\sum (s_i w_i)^2}$. Then compare each new tolerance with the IT grades: IT6 and
finer needs grinding or honing, IT7 to IT8 needs fine turning or boring, and IT9 to IT11 is normal machining.

## Radial stacks

<StackRadial />

Coaxiality, position and clearance shifts are offsets in a plane, in any direction. They add as vectors:

- **The worst case** is $\sum r_i$, with all offsets in one line.
- **The RSS of the radii,** $\sqrt{\sum r_i^2}$, is not a limit. It treats each offset as a ±3σ dimension. A clearance
  that is fully taken up in a random direction is very different. With two equal clearances, half of all assemblies
  are more than this value.
- **Many random offsets** approach a Rayleigh distribution, with $\sigma^2 = \sum \sigma_i^2$ in each direction. Use
  $r^2/4$ for an offset anywhere in a zone, and $r^2/2$ for a clearance that is fully taken up. The radius that contains
  a fraction $p$ is then $\sigma\sqrt{-2\ln(1-p)}$. With few offsets, use Monte Carlo.
- **A common direction** makes random offsets add arithmetically. On a horizontal assembly stand, gravity takes up each
  clearance in the downward direction. A side load during bolt tightening has the same effect.
- **Tilts are also offsets.** A tolerance $t$ over a length $l$ tilts an axis by $t/l$. At a lever arm $L$, this moves
  the axis by $tL/l$.

For a nozzle, the important result is the **offset $d$ of the thrust line from the centre of gravity**. This offset
gives a disturbing moment $M = F d$, which the vehicle control system must correct. It also combines the lateral offset
and the tilt of the nozzle into one value.

## Calculator

The calculator has four tabs:

- **Linear stack.** Enter one row for each arrow, as the drawing gives the dimension: equal ±, unequal + and −, limits,
  unilateral, or a GD&T control. The calculator changes each row to a mean ± an equal half, and shows the change. A
  diametral position zone acts by its radius, and a position at MMC can take its bonus. Each row can have a sensitivity,
  a normal or uniform distribution, a correlation group and a material from the
  [material library](../../materials/selection/) for thermal growth. The results are the worst case, RSS, Bender's 1.5 ×
  RSS, the Cpk-based band and a Monte Carlo, with a table of the contribution of each row. You can also paste the rows
  as text, for example from a spreadsheet.
- **Allocate.** Shares a target on the result among the rows, by one of the three methods above, and writes the new
  tolerances back to the rows.
- **Radial stack.** Offsets that add as vectors, as described above.
- **Fasteners.** The floating and fixed fastener formulae of ASME Y14.5, also without a projected tolerance zone (see
  [GD&T](../gdt/)).

## Worked example: seal squeeze and nozzle alignment

The starting values of the calculator are the values of this example. The part Cpk of 1.33 and the two requirements are
**for this example only**. Push **Load worked example** to apply them.

### The nozzle seal

This is the radial seal of the [O-ring page](../../seals/o-ring-design/#worked-example-a-nozzle-to-case-radial-seal),
with the groove runout that the [GD&T page](../gdt/#worked-example-the-aft-closure-its-bolts-and-the-nozzle-seal-groove)
added to the drawing:

| Arrow | Limits (mm) | Sensitivity | Mean ± half |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| O-ring section | 5.20–5.46 | +1 | 5.330 ± 0.130 |
| Case bore ⌀ (H8) | 150.000–150.063 | −1 | 150.0315 ± 0.0315 |
| Plug pilot ⌀ | 149.860–149.900 | +½ | 149.880 ± 0.020 |
| Groove root ⌀ | 141.45–141.55 | +½ | 141.500 ± 0.050 |
| Root runout 0.05 to the pilot | (none) | −1 | 0 ± 0.025 |

1. **Nominal and worst case.** The nominal squeeze is 0.989 mm. The worst case is **0.767 mm**, which is 14.75% of the
   smallest ring and agrees with the GD&T page. The requirement in this example is 0.74 mm. At this value, the stretch,
   the −40 °C temperature and the 20% compression set of the O-ring page leave its 10% minimum. The worst case passes,
   by 0.027 mm.
2. **RSS.** The band is ±0.139 mm, so the low end is **0.850 mm**. The section of the O-ring itself is **88% of the
   variance**. If you halve all four machined tolerances, the result changes by less than 0.01 mm. A ring with a section
   tolerance of ±0.08 changes it by approximately 0.05 mm. The machining does not control this stack.
3. **Cpk.** With all parts at Cpk 1.33, σ is 0.035 mm. The Cpk of the assembly against the 0.74 mm minimum is 2.38. None
   of the 100,000 Monte Carlo assemblies is below the minimum.
4. **A possible cost saving.** Change the runout to the general tolerance of 0.2. The worst case is then **0.692 mm**.
   This is 9.2% at the end of life, which is below the minimum. RSS gives 0.819 mm, which passes. Nobody measures a
   general tolerance, thus use a uniform distribution for the runout (“-0.1, 0.1, u”). The runout is then 74% of the
   variance and the Cpk of the assembly decreases to 1.23. The Monte Carlo finds 1 assembly in 100,000 below the minimum.
   The normal estimate gives 1 in 10,000. A uniform part has no tails, and the normal approximation does not show this.
5. **Decision: use the worst case and keep the 0.05.** At assembly, you cannot see a seal that has too little squeeze.
   Its failure causes a burn-through, and the quantity made is small. The statistics would accept a risk that the
   programme must not accept. The runout has no cost if the groove and the pilot are machined in one setup.
6. **How much tolerance is there?** In the **Allocate** tab, the target is the distance from the nominal to the
   minimum: $0.9885 - 0.74 = 0.2485$ mm. The O-ring section is fixed, because it is a bought part. It uses 0.130 mm of
   the worst case, and 0.1185 mm is left for the machined rows. **Scale** gives a factor of **1.30**: every machined
   tolerance can open by 30% and the worst case still passes. **By size** gives about IT8 on the bore, the pilot and the
   groove root (± 0.042, ± 0.042 and ± 0.041 mm), and a runout of 0.07. The present drawing is tighter than necessary on
   the pilot (± 0.020) and looser on the groove root (± 0.050). This is a cost decision for the supplier and the designer to make together.

### The nozzle alignment

The result is the offset of the thrust line at the centre of gravity of the vehicle. At ignition, this point is 700 mm
forward of the aft joint (illustrative value). The thrust is 29.5 kN, from the
[load-path page](../../joints/load-paths/#worked-example-the-300-mm-motors-load-path). The closure pilot fit is from the
[datums page](../datums-and-position/#worked-example-the-closure-the-cmm-rejected). The other tolerances are
illustrative. The requirement is that 99.73% of motors are within 0.45 mm.

| Offset | Tolerance | Kind | At the CG (mm) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Case flange bore to the case axis | coaxiality ⌀0.10 | zone | 0.050 |
| Case flange face | perpendicularity 0.05 over ⌀300 | zone, tilt | 0.117 |
| Closure pilot in the flange bore | 0.10–0.25 clearance | shift | 0.125 |
| Nozzle bore in the closure | position ⌀0.10 | zone | 0.050 |
| Nozzle seat face | perpendicularity 0.03 over ⌀180 | zone, tilt | 0.117 |
| Nozzle pilot in its bore | 0.100–0.203 clearance | shift | 0.102 |
| Throat to the nozzle pilot | coaxiality ⌀0.10 | zone | 0.050 |

1. **Worst case: 0.610 mm.** This gives 18.0 N·m, which is the same as a thrust tilt of 0.050°. It does not meet
   0.45 mm. The two squareness tilts and the closure clearance are 58% of the total.
2. **RSS of the radii: 0.246 mm.** This seems to pass easily, but **26% of motors are more than this value**. Do not
   use it.
3. **Monte Carlo, vertical assembly.** The two clearance shifts are in random directions. 99.73% of motors are within
   **0.442 mm** (13.1 N·m). This passes by a small margin: 0.20% are more than 0.45 mm.
4. **Horizontal assembly.** The nozzle and the closure move to the bottom of their clearances before the bolts are
   torqued. Change the two shifts to “fixed”. The 99.73% value increases to **0.474 mm**, and 0.81% of motors are more
   than the limit. The stack fails, but no part has changed.
5. **The solution.** Change the nozzle pilot to H7/g6 ([Fits & limits](../fits-and-limits/)), which gives a 0.0395 mm
   shift. Then 99.73% of motors are within **0.415 mm**, also with horizontal assembly. Another solution is to centre
   the nozzle in a fixture during torquing. After the change, do the seal stack again, because the plug pilot is one
   of its arrows. Also measure the nozzle alignment of each motor. Inspection is the best way to show that a statistical
   requirement is met.

## Where this stops

- **Independent contributors.** You must put correlated features (one setup, one tool, one batch) in groups yourself.
- **Linear sensitivities** in the linear tab. Nonlinear results need their own function. The Monte Carlo in the library
  permits this.
- **One lever arm for all tilts** in the radial tab. Tilts about points far apart need their own lever arms. Also, the
  centre of gravity moves as the propellant burns.
- **Geometry only.** Loads, pressure, deflection and temperature also change gaps. See the joint opening on the O-ring
  page, and [Differential expansion](../../joints/differential-expansion/) for temperature. Thrust misalignment also
  comes from the flow and from nozzle erosion. Tolerances do not include these.
- **Normal and uniform parts only.** Real distributions can be skewed, cut off by inspection, or have two peaks from two
  machines. Use a separate Monte Carlo with measured data for these.

## On the drawing

- **Datums that make the loop short.** Give each critical dimension from the datum that the stack uses.
- **All the tolerances that the stack uses.** Do not leave one to the general tolerances by accident, as could occur
  with the groove runout.
- **Assembly instructions** where the stack assumes a position: centring, orientation and the tightening sequence.
- **The stack** in the design record: the loop, the method, the distributions that you assumed and the data for them.
- **Inspection of the assembled result** where the requirement is statistical: measure or gauge the alignment, the gap
  or the squeeze.
