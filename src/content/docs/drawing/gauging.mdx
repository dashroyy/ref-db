## Why this matters

A drawing gives limits. An inspector gives a number and a decision. Between the two, there is the measurement, and no
measurement is exact. When a part is near a limit, the measurement error decides if the part passes:

- **A bad part can pass.** A seal bore that is 5 µm too large, measured with an error of −6 µm, is accepted. The seal
  then has less squeeze than the design assumed.
- **A good part can fail.** The same error in the other direction scraps a good part, or starts a nonconformance report
  that costs more than the part.
- **Two inspectors can disagree.** The supplier and the customer measure the same part with different gauges, at
  different temperatures. Both are correct within their uncertainty, and the part is in dispute.

Thus a measurement is complete only with its uncertainty, and a decision is complete only with its rule.

<Analogy title="A bathroom scale at the limit">
  A suitcase must be under 23 kg. Your scale reads 22.8 kg, but it is only good to ±0.5 kg. The suitcase can be 23.3 kg.
  At the airport, the scale is better, and you pay. If the scale reads 21 kg, the uncertainty does not matter. The
  uncertainty only matters near the limit, and that is where the arguments are.
</Analogy>

## Uncertainty: the budget

The uncertainty of a measurement comes from several sources. Write each one in a list, the **uncertainty budget**,
and convert each one to a standard uncertainty $u_i$ (one standard deviation):

| How the value is given | Standard uncertainty |
| --- | --- |
| A standard deviation from repeated measurements | The value |
| An expanded uncertainty from a calibration certificate, $k = 2$ | Value ÷ 2 |
| A limit $\pm a$ with no other information (a temperature range, a specification of the instrument) | $a/\sqrt{3}$ |
| The resolution $d$ of a digital display | $d/\sqrt{12}$ |

For uncorrelated sources, the combined standard uncertainty and the expanded uncertainty are (GUM, JCGM 100):

$$
u_c = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum u_i^2}
\qquad
U = k\,u_c,\quad k = 2 \ (\text{approximately 95\%})
$$

The squares make the largest source dominate. If one source is two thirds of the variance, improve that source. An
improvement to the others has almost no effect.

**Temperature** is often the largest source on large parts. ISO 1 gives all dimensions at 20 °C. A part and its
reference (a gauge, a setting ring, a scale) at different temperatures give a length error:

$$
\Delta L = L\,\big[\alpha_\text{part}(T_\text{part} - 20) - \alpha_\text{ref}(T_\text{ref} - 20)\big]
$$

On a 150 mm steel bore, a difference of 2 K between the part and a steel setting ring gives 3.7 µm. This is 6% of an
H8 tolerance. A part that comes from the machine warm, or from a cold store, must soak to the temperature of the room
before it is measured.

**Other usual sources:** the calibration of the reference, the repeatability of the instrument, the resolution, the
alignment and centring of the gauge in the feature, the form error of the part (a two-point gauge in an oval bore gives
a different result in each direction), and the operator.

## Decision rules and guard bands

The **test uncertainty ratio** compares the tolerance with the uncertainty:

$$
\text{TUR} = \frac{USL - LSL}{2U}
$$

A usual minimum is 4:1, and 10:1 is better. Many quality systems give their own value. Below 4:1, a large part of the
tolerance is uncertain.

The **decision rule** tells how a measured value becomes a decision. ISO 14253-1 gives the default for proving
conformance:

<GuardBand />

- **Guarded acceptance.** The acceptance limits move inside the tolerance by $U$. A part is accepted only if the
  measured value is in the conformance zone. The supplier carries the risk, and the customer gets a low false-accept
  rate. The cost is a smaller tolerance for manufacture.
- **Simple acceptance.** The acceptance limits are the tolerance limits. The two parties share the risk at the limits.
  It is acceptable when the TUR is high and the decision rule is agreed.
- **Guarded rejection.** The customer moves the limits outside the tolerance by $U$ to prove that a part does not
  conform.

Agree the rule in the contract or the inspection plan (ISO/TR 14253-6, ASME B89.7.3.1), before the first part is measured.

**False accept and false reject.** For a process with a normal distribution and a gauge with a normal error, the
probabilities follow from the process capability and the uncertainty. A capable process (small spread, centred) has
few parts near the limits, so the decision rule has a small effect. A process that is not capable puts many parts
near the limits, and the rule decides how many bad parts go to the customer and how many good parts go to scrap.

## Gauge repeatability and reproducibility

The uncertainty budget is an analysis. A **gauge R&R study** is a test of the complete measurement system on real
parts: two or three operators measure the same parts two or three times each, without seeing their previous results.
The average and range method of the AIAG *Measurement Systems Analysis* manual separates:

- **Repeatability (EV):** the scatter when one operator measures one part again. It comes from the gauge, the fixture
  and the part (form, roughness).
- **Reproducibility (AV):** the difference between operators. It comes from the method, the training and the
  technique.
- **Part variation (PV):** the real difference between the parts.

$$
GRR = \sqrt{EV^2 + AV^2}
\qquad
TV = \sqrt{GRR^2 + PV^2}
$$

The result is a percentage. **%GRR of the tolerance** ($6\,GRR$ ÷ tolerance) tells if the gauge can accept and reject
parts. **%GRR of the total variation** tells if the gauge can see the process for SPC. The usual AIAG guidance is: under
10% is acceptable, 10% to 30% can be acceptable for the application, and over 30% is not acceptable. The **number of
distinct categories**, $1.41\,PV/GRR$, must be at least 5 for process control.

Select parts that cover the range of the process. If the parts in the study are all almost the same, PV is small, and
the gauge looks worse than it is.

## Limit gauges

A **plain limit gauge** gives a decision, not a number. A GO gauge must enter (or pass over) the feature. A NOGO gauge
must not. Limit gauges are fast, robust and need little training. They do not give the data for SPC, and they cannot
tell how near to a limit the part is.

<TaylorGauges />

**Taylor's principle.** The GO gauge represents the mating part. It has the full form and the full length of the
feature, so it checks the envelope: size and form together. The NOGO gauge checks one local size at a time, so it
must be a two-point gauge (a flat pin or a snap gauge), and it is used in more than one direction.

**The gauge tolerances use part of the work tolerance.** The gauge-maker needs a tolerance, and a GO gauge wears, so it
needs a wear allowance. If both are inside the work tolerance, a gauge in tolerance never accepts a bad part. But a
good part near a limit can be rejected. With a gauge tolerance of 10% and a wear allowance of 5% of the work tolerance,
only 75% of the tolerance is accepted by all gauges. ISO 1938-1 and ASME B89.1.5 give the gauge tolerances by size and
by class (in US practice, the gauge-maker classes XX, X, Y, Z and ZZ).

**Functional gauges** check the position of a pattern of features at MMC. Each pin of the gauge is at the virtual
condition of its hole: the MMC size minus the position tolerance. A part that goes onto the gauge will assemble with
its mating part. With absolute tolerancing (ASME Y14.43), the gauge tolerance goes on the product side, so the gauge
never accepts a bad part. A functional gauge does not check the size of the holes. Use GO and NOGO plugs for that. See
[Datums & position](../datums-and-position/).

## Measuring motor hardware

| Feature | Usual method | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seal bores and spigots | Two-point or three-point bore gauge set on a ring, or CMM | Temperature; form error (oval, taper) on thin walls; measure at several heights and angles |
| O-ring groove depth and width | Groove gauge, CMM, or a cast replica | The squeeze depends on the depth: see [O-ring design](../../seals/o-ring-design/) |
| Case wall thickness | Ultrasonic thickness gauge; mechanical gauge at open ends | Velocity calibration on the same material; coatings; minimum thickness of the probe |
| Threads | GO and NOGO thread plug and ring gauges (ISO 1502, ASME B1.2) | A thread gauge checks function, not the pitch diameter directly |
| Bolt-hole patterns | Functional gauge or CMM | The datum reference frame of the drawing |
| Position and profile | CMM to the datum reference frame; functional gauge for position at MMC | The fitting method and the datum simulation of the CMM program |
| Flatness of seal and flange faces | CMM with minimum-zone evaluation, or surface plate and indicator | Free state or restrained: a thin flange can be flat only when bolted ([GD&T](../gdt/)) |
| Roundness and cylindricity | Roundness machine; CMM with many points | Few points on a CMM miss lobing; the filter changes the result |
| Runout | Part on centres or on its datum features, with an indicator | Full indicator movement includes the form error; measure where the drawing places the datum |
| Surface finish | Stylus instrument | The cutoff and the rule: see [Surface finish](../surface-finish/) |
| Large diameters | Pi tape, CMM, laser tracker | Temperature over a large part; the part can be out of round when it is not supported |

**A thin case is flexible.** A 300 mm case with a 1.4 mm wall can be several tenths of a millimetre oval when it lies
on a bench, and round when it is pressurised. Measure on a defined support, or measure the circumference (pi tape) and
the form separately. The drawing must say in which condition the part is measured (ISO 10579, the "free state" notes of
ASME Y14.5).

**Ultrasonic thickness** is the time of flight multiplied by the sound velocity, divided by two. An error of 1% in the
velocity is an error of 1% in the thickness. Calibrate the gauge on a step block of the same material and heat
treatment, at the same temperature. Thin walls need a high-frequency probe with a delay line.

**CMM.** A coordinate measuring machine can measure size, form and position in one setup, but its result depends on
the number of points, the filter and the fitting algorithm (least squares, minimum zone, maximum inscribed). Give the
evaluation method in the inspection plan, because two machines with different settings give different results on the
same part.

## Worked example: the nozzle seal bore

The bore of the radial seal on the [O-ring page](../../seals/o-ring-design/#worked-example-a-nozzle-to-case-radial-seal)
is ⌀150 H8: 150.000 to 150.063 mm, a tolerance of 63 µm ([Fits & limits](../fits-and-limits/#worked-example-four-fits-on-the-motor)).
The starting values in each tab of the calculator are the values of this example. The criteria (TUR at least 4, %GRR
at most 20% of the tolerance) are **for this example only**. Push **Load worked example** to apply them.

1. **The budget.** The bore is measured with a two-point bore gauge, set on a ring with a certificate of ±1.5 µm
   ($k = 2$). The repeatability is 1.2 µm, the display resolution is 1 µm, and alignment is ±1 µm. The part can be up
   to 2 K warmer than the ring: $150\,000 \times 12.3\times10^{-6} \times 2 = 3.7$ µm. Thus $u_c = 2.64$ µm,
   **U = 5.3 µm** and **TUR = 6.0**. The TUR is above 4, but the temperature is 65% of the variance.
2. **The decision.** With guarded acceptance, the bore is accepted from 150.0053 to 150.0577 mm. The process is
   centred at 31.5 µm with a standard deviation of 9 µm ($C_p = 1.17$). With simple acceptance, **119 ppm** of the bores
   are bad but accepted. With the guard bands, this decreases to **3 ppm**, and 0.47% of the good bores are rejected.
   For a gas seal, the guarded rule is the correct choice.
3. **Improve the largest source.** Soak the part next to the ring until the two are within 0.5 K. The temperature
   contribution decreases to 0.9 µm, U decreases to **3.3 µm**, the TUR increases to 9.6, and the false rejects
   decrease to 0.16%. This costs time on the bench, not a new gauge.
4. **The gauge R&R study.** Three operators measure ten bores three times each (the readings are in the calculator).
   %GRR is **16.3% of the tolerance** and 12.9% of the total variation, with 10 distinct categories. This passes the 20%
   limit of the example. It is in the "can be acceptable" band of AIAG, so the inspection plan keeps it under review.
   Repeatability (1.26 µm) and reproducibility (1.16 µm) are almost equal: the method and the gauge need the same
   attention.
5. **Limit gauges for the shop floor.** With a gauge tolerance of 10% and a wear allowance of 5%, the GO plug is
   150.0032 to 150.0095 mm when new and is scrapped at 150.000 mm. The NOGO plug is 150.0567 to 150.0630 mm. All
   gauges in tolerance accept 150.0095 to 150.0567 mm: **75% of the tolerance**. The bore gauge with guard bands keeps
   83%, and it gives the numbers for SPC. Thus the plugs are a quick check at the machine, and the bore gauge gives the
   acceptance.
6. **The case wall.** The minimum wall of the [case](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example) is 1.377 mm. An
   ultrasonic gauge with a velocity error of 1% gives an error of 0.014 mm. Against a minimum wall, this is a large
   uncertainty. Calibrate the gauge on a step block of D6AC in the same heat-treated condition, and include the
   calibration in the budget.

## Where this stops

- **Normal distributions.** The risk calculation assumes a normal process and a normal gauge error. Real processes can
  be skewed, drift, or have two populations (two machines, two tools).
- **Uncorrelated sources.** Correlated sources (for example, the temperature of the part and of the ring when they
  change together) need the covariance terms of the GUM.
- **Average and range method.** The ANOVA method of the AIAG manual also gives the operator-part interaction, and it is
  better for small studies.
- **Simplified gauge tolerances.** The percentages for limit gauges are a simplification. Use ISO 1938-1, ASME B89.1.5
  or your gauge standard for the real values.
- **Screening guidance.** TUR and %GRR limits come from your quality system and your customer.

## On the drawing and in the inspection plan

- **The decision rule** (guarded or simple acceptance), and the standard that defines it.
- **The measurement temperature,** or the soak time before measurement, for features with fine tolerances.
- **The condition of the part:** free state or restrained, and how it is supported.
- **The method for critical features:** the instrument, the number and position of the measurements, and for a CMM
  the fitting algorithm.
- **The gauge** for features that a limit gauge or a functional gauge accepts, with its identity and calibration.
- **The data to keep:** the values, not only "accept", for features that SPC or a later investigation needs. See
  [Proof testing](../../verify/proof-testing/) for the records of a test.
