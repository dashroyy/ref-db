## Why this matters

A performance index ranks materials on one question ([Material selection](../selection/)). A real choice has
several: mass, damage tolerance, cost, heritage, supply, schedule. A weighted trade study puts them on one page and
gives each option a total.

The total looks objective, but it comes from choices: which criteria, which scales and which weights. Change the
weights and the winner can change. That is not a fault of the method; it is the information the method gives. The
weights, not the totals, are what a design review should question.

<Analogy title="Buying a car">
  Score three cars on price, economy, boot space and comfort, weight each criterion, and add. One car wins by two
  points. Then your partner says comfort matters more than you thought, and another car wins. The sheet did not tell
  you which car is best; it told you that the decision depends on how much comfort matters, and that is the question
  to settle.
</Analogy>

## Musts and wants

<TradeFunnel />

- **Musts are screens.** A requirement that must be met (a service temperature, weldability, a mass limit, an export
  restriction) is pass or fail. An option that fails it is out. Never give a must a weight: a weighted sum lets a
  strength in one criterion pay for a failure in another.
- **Wants are scored.** Everything that is better when larger or smaller, but has no hard limit, goes into the matrix
  with a weight.

## Criteria and scales

- **Independent criteria.** If two criteria measure the same thing (strength-to-weight and wall mass), the property
  counts twice. Merge them or drop one.
- **Measured where you can, judged where you must.** Use the property value for mass, cost and strength. For judged
  criteria (heritage, supply risk, manufacturability), write the scale with anchors: what a 1, a 3 and a 5 mean.
- **Scale the values to 0–1.** Min–max scaling gives the best option 1 and the worst 0. It makes the criteria
  comparable, but it stretches small differences: two options 1% apart get 0 and 1.

The weighted total of option $i$ is:

$$
T_i = 100\,\frac{\sum_k w_k\,s_{ik}}{\sum_k w_k}
$$

Here $w_k$ is the weight of criterion $k$ and $s_{ik}$ the scaled score of option $i$ on it.

## Weights

Weights express how much each criterion matters to this decision. Set them before you score, with the people who own
the requirement, and write the reason for each. Methods that help:

- **Swing weighting:** for each criterion, ask how much it is worth to move from the worst option to the best. The
  criterion with the largest swing gets the largest weight. This ties the weights to the options, not to an abstract
  importance.
- **Pairwise comparison** (as in the analytic hierarchy process): compare the criteria two at a time, and derive the
  weights from the comparisons. It shows when the judgements are not consistent.

## Rank reversal

Because the scores are scaled across the options, adding or removing an option changes every score.

<RankReversal />

The order of two options can change although nothing about them changed. A matrix ranks the options it holds, not
options in general. Fix the set of options before the final scoring, and screen out options that fail a must.

## Sensitivity

Hold all weights but one, and find the weight $w_c$ at which another option $b$ ties with the leader $a$:

$$
w_c^{*} = -\,\frac{\sum_{k \ne c} w_k\,(s_{ak} - s_{bk})}{s_{ac} - s_{bc}}
$$

If a small change of one weight (less than approximately 25%) changes the leader, the weights cannot support the
decision: no one knows them that well. If no single weight within reason changes it, the decision is robust.

## Uncertainty

Scores are uncertain too: a cost estimate, a judged heritage, a mass index from a typical property. A Monte Carlo
moves each score and each weight at random inside its uncertainty and counts how often each option wins. An option
that wins in 95% of the trials is a clear choice. Two options that share the wins evenly are a tie, whatever their
totals say.

## Pugh matrix for concepts

Early in a design, scores are too uncertain for a weighted sum. The Pugh method compares each concept with a datum
concept, criterion by criterion: better (+), the same (0) or worse (−). It does not give a winner. It shows where
each concept is strong and weak, and it suggests hybrids that take the strengths of two.

## Worked example: the case-material shortlist

The [material-selection example](../selection/#worked-example-choosing-a-case-material) ranked the metals for the
300 mm case. The programme now asks whether a filament-wound carbon/epoxy case is worth it. The shortlist is D6AC,
maraging 250, Ti-6Al-4V ELI and carbon/epoxy. The judged scores, the laminate mass index and the weights are **for
this example only**. Push **Load worked example** in the calculator to load them.

1. **Musts.** All four meet the service temperature and have a qualified joining or winding route. They pass the
   screen.
2. **Scores.** Mass index $\sigma_y/\rho$: 185, 211 and 187 kN·m/kg for the metals, and an illustrative 350 for a
   filament-wound wall. Damage tolerance, judged 1–5 from the crack at yield (0.59, 0.89 and 3.68 mm) and the impact
   sensitivity of the composite: 2, 3, 5, 2. Relative cost: 1, 3, 4, 3.5. Heritage: 5, 4, 3, 4. Supply risk: 2, 4, 4,
   3.
3. **Weights and totals.** Mass 30, damage tolerance 25, cost 20, heritage 15, supply 10. Carbon/epoxy **45.8**,
   D6AC **45.0**, maraging 27.2, titanium 25.4. Carbon/epoxy leads by 0.8 points.
4. **Sensitivity.** The mass weight falling from 30 to **29.2** (−3%) makes D6AC tie. So does the cost weight rising
   from 20 to 21 (+5%). For titanium to tie, the damage-tolerance weight must nearly double, to 45.5. The first two are
   a tie; the other two are clearly behind.
5. **Uncertainty.** With ±0.15 on each scaled score and ±30% on each weight, carbon/epoxy wins **51%** of 4000 trials
   and D6AC **49%**. Maraging and titanium almost never win.
6. **Adding an option.** Add 4130 steel (mass index 130, cheap and well known). It "wins" with **61.7**, although it
   cannot meet the mass of the case: it should have failed a must. And D6AC (47.9) now ranks **above** carbon/epoxy
   (44.0), although nothing about either changed.

The matrix says clearly that maraging and titanium are not the answer for these weights, and that it cannot choose
between D6AC and carbon/epoxy. The choice between them rests on what the matrix leaves out: qualification cost and
schedule, the facilities, and the risk of a first composite case. Record that reasoning with the matrix.

## Where this stops

- **A weighted sum is compensatory.** A high score on one criterion pays for a low score on another. Use screens for
  anything that must not be traded.
- **Min–max scaling** depends on the options in the set and stretches small differences. Other scales (ratio to the
  best, fixed ranges) behave differently; state which one you use.
- **Judged scores are opinions** with an anchor. Two people should score them independently, and the differences be
  discussed.
- **Uniform random variation** in the Monte Carlo is a simple model. Correlated uncertainties (cost and schedule, for
  example) can change the result.
- **The matrix does not decide.** People decide, with the matrix as evidence.

## On the drawing

A trade study does not go on the drawing, but its result does:

- **The material specification and condition** that the study chose ([Material specs](../specification/)).
- **A reference to the trade study** in the design file or the design justification, so that a later change can see
  why the choice was made.
- **The musts** that screened the options, as requirements in the specification, so that a later substitution must
  meet them.
