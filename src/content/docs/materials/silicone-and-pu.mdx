## Why this matters

Silicone and polyurethane are in almost every motor programme: silicone in seals, sealants, potting, conformal
coatings, cable boots and thermal insulation; polyurethane in potting, cable jackets, wear pads, bump stops and
handling equipment. They are cheap, easy to cast and forgiving, so they get little design attention.

Their failures are quiet. A silicone that never cures where it touched polyurethane looks like a bad batch. A bond
that will not hold looks like poor surface preparation, when silicone oil from the next bench is the cause. A potted
unit that passed its vibration test cracks its solder joints at the cold limit. Each of these comes from the
chemistry, and each is easy to prevent once you know it.

<Analogy title="Oil and water">
  Silicone and polyurethane are like oil and water in a kitchen: both are useful, and neither is a problem on its own.
  The trouble is a trace of one where you need the other. A drop of oil on a clean glass stops the water from wetting
  it; a trace of amine in a silicone mould stops the cure.
</Analogy>

## Two chains, opposite properties

- **Silicone (polydimethylsiloxane)** has an inorganic Si–O–Si backbone, the bond of quartz: 452 kJ/mol against
  348 kJ/mol for carbon–carbon. The Si–O–Si angle opens to about 143°, and the chain rotates almost freely. Strong
  bond, floppy chain: silicone works from about −55 °C to 200 °C (phenyl grades to −100 °C), but its open coils let gases
  through and its tear strength is low. The methyl groups on the outside give a surface energy of about 21 mN/m, so
  almost nothing wets it or sticks to it.
- **Polyurethane** forms when an isocyanate (–N=C=O) reacts with a polyol (–OH). The urethane link carries an N–H that
  hydrogen-bonds to the carbonyl of the next chain, and pulls the hard segments into stiff domains in a soft matrix.
  This two-phase structure gives its abrasion and tear resistance. The same link is hydrolysable: hot water reverts it,
  polyester grades much faster than polyether grades. Its heat limit is about 80–120 °C.

They fail in opposite environments. Silicone takes heat, ozone, UV and oxidation, and is destroyed by fuels, oils and
abrasion. Polyurethane takes abrasion, fuels and oils, and is destroyed by heat and hot water. **Choose by the
dominant threat.** Neither is the general-purpose answer.

## Where they fight

| Problem | Mechanism | Result | Prevention |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Platinum poisoning** | Addition-cure silicone cures on a platinum catalyst at a few ppm. Amines, tin and sulphur, all in polyurethane chemistry, take the platinum sites | A tacky, uncured layer at the interface. Parts per million are enough | Separate storage, tools and gloves; cure the urethane fully first; test a coupon |
| **Silicone contamination** | Surface energy of about 21 mN/m: silicone oil spreads, and moves on gloves and dust | Paint fisheyes, adhesives do not wet, bonds fail. Hard to clean off | No silicone in or near bonding and painting areas |
| **Acetoxy cure** | One-part RTV that releases acetic acid as it cures | Corrodes copper and brass, attacks the urethane link | Specify a neutral-cure (alkoxy or oxime) silicone near metal or urethane |
| **Isocyanate and water** | Free isocyanate and water give an amine and CO₂. Silicone passes water vapour to the urethane | Bubbles and voids in the potting; the amine then poisons platinum silicone | Dry parts and materials; do not cure urethane beside fresh silicone |
| **No bond between them** | Nothing wets silicone, and its surface has no groups that react | A joint that opens on the first thermal cycle | Mechanical interlock or a dedicated primer, and a test |

The condensation-cure silicones are cured by tin. **A tin-cured RTV can itself poison a platinum silicone.** Keep the
two silicone families apart too.

## Potting at the cold limit

A potting compound cures at room temperature, then cools with the unit. It shrinks much more than the parts and the
housing (silicone expands at about 280 × 10⁻⁶/K, aluminium at 24 × 10⁻⁶/K), so it pulls on everything it is bonded to:

- **With a free surface,** the stress depends on the modulus: $\sigma = \frac{E}{1-\nu}\,\Delta\alpha\,\Delta T$. A
  soft silicone (E of about 2 MPa) puts almost no load on solder joints and glass-bodied parts. A rigid epoxy (about
  3000 MPa) puts a thousand times more.
- **Fully confined** (a closed box filled to the lid), the potting cannot shrink at all, and the stress depends on the
  bulk modulus, not on E: $\sigma = 3K\,\Delta\alpha\,\Delta T$. Rubber is nearly incompressible: K is about 1 GPa for
  silicone. **A confined rubber is not soft.** It tears, pulls away from a wall, or forms voids.
- **Below its glass transition** a polyurethane potting becomes many times stiffer. Choose a grade whose Tg is below the
  coldest temperature.

## Worked example: a potted junction box

A junction box on the aft skirt has a 6061-T6 aluminium housing and is potted at 25 °C. For this example only, its
cold limit is −40 °C. Select **Load worked example**.

1. **Silicone, with a free surface:** 0.065 MPa. The potting can hardly load the parts.
2. **The same silicone in a box filled to the lid:** **50 MPa** of hydrostatic tension, far above the 3–10 MPa that
   the silicone can carry. It tears or pulls away from the wall. Leave a free surface, or an air space under the lid.
3. **Polyurethane potting:** 0.22 MPa free, 86 MPa confined. Its Tg (about −55 °C) is below −40 °C, so it stays
   rubbery; a grade with a Tg of −30 °C would be rigid at the cold limit.
4. **Epoxy:** **10.9 MPa** on the parts with a free surface: enough to crack glass diodes and solder joints. Use it
   only with stress-relief buffers.
5. **Poisoning.** The silicone has 10 ppm of platinum. The same gloves handled the polyurethane potting of the next
   unit, and left 5 ppm of amine in the silicone at the interface. That is 0.87 amine molecules for each platinum atom:
   only 13% of the catalyst is still active, and **the silicone does not cure** there. 5.75 ppm of amine stops it
   completely. Of a tin catalyst, 32 ppm would do the same.

## Where this stops

- **Typical values for each class.** Grades differ widely: hardness, temperature range, outgassing and adhesion come
  from the product datasheet.
- **The poisoning model is a teaching model.** Inhibition depends on the poison and the formulation. Test the actual
  combination: cast the silicone against the actual cured urethane, from the actual batch.
- **No viscoelasticity.** The potting modulus changes with rate and temperature
  ([Viscoelasticity](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/)); near Tg it changes by orders of magnitude.
- **Outgassing** for space or vacuum use comes from ASTM E595 data for the cured product ([Plastics](../plastics/#vacuum-outgassing-and-radiation)).

## On the drawing

- **The product and the cure,** not only "silicone": the specification, the mix ratio, the cure schedule, and the
  primer.
- **The free surface or air space** that a potting must keep, and the fill level.
- **Segregation:** no silicone in bonding or painting areas; separate tools and gloves for silicone and urethane.
- **A cure-compatibility test** for any silicone that is cast against a urethane or near one.
