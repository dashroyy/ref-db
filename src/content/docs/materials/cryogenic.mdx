## Why this matters

Cold changes three things at once. Parts shrink by different amounts, so fits, joints and seals that work on the
bench open or close. Some metals lose their toughness and break like glass. And the fluids bring hazards of their own:
liquid oxygen ignites materials that are safe in air, and hydrogen embrittles some metals and leaks through gaps that
hold other gases.

Most mistakes come from treating the cold as a larger temperature change. It is not. The expansion coefficient falls
as the temperature falls, the toughness of some metals drops suddenly, and in oxygen the question is ignition, not
strength.

<Analogy title="A crowd that stops fidgeting">
  Atoms in a solid vibrate about their places, like people in a crowd who fidget. Fidgeting takes room, so a warm crowd
  takes more space. Calm the crowd and it shrinks, but not in a straight line: when most people already stand still,
  more calming makes almost no difference. Below about 40 K most metals have almost stopped shrinking. A
  room-temperature expansion coefficient assumes that the crowd keeps calming at the same rate, so it over-predicts
  the movement.
</Analogy>

## The cryogens

| Fluid | Boils at 1 atm | Liquid density | Latent heat | Gas from 1 L of liquid | Main concern |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Liquid methane (LCH4) | 111.7 K (−161 °C) | 422 kg/m³ | 511 kJ/kg | 633 L | A fuel. It freezes at 90.7 K, near LOX temperature |
| Liquid oxygen (LOX) | 90.2 K (−183 °C) | 1141 kg/m³ | 213 kJ/kg | 858 L | Ignition and burning of the materials it touches |
| Liquid nitrogen (LN2) | 77.4 K (−196 °C) | 806 kg/m³ | 199 kJ/kg | 692 L | Asphyxiation. The usual test and chill-down fluid |
| Liquid hydrogen (LH2) | 20.3 K (−253 °C) | 70.8 kg/m³ | 446 kJ/kg | 845 L | Embrittlement, permeation, leaks, wide flammability limits |
| Liquid helium (LHe) | 4.2 K (−269 °C) | 125 kg/m³ | 20.6 kJ/kg | 749 L | A very small latent heat: one watt boils 1.4 L an hour |

Values from the NIST WebBook at 1 atm, hydrogen as parahydrogen. Gas at 20 °C and 1 atm.

The gas column gives a rule: **every volume that can trap liquid needs a relief path.** Liquid that is caught between
two closed valves warms, boils and tries to expand several hundred times. The pressure rises until something fails. A
relief valve or a burst disc on each such volume is part of the design.

## Crystal structure decides toughness

<CrystalDbtt />

| Structure | Metals | In the cold | Cryogenic use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FCC | Austenitic stainless steel, aluminium, copper, nickel alloys, Invar | No ductile-to-brittle transition. Strength rises and toughness stays high | To 4 K |
| BCC | Carbon and low-alloy steels, martensitic and PH stainless steel, the ferrite in duplex, Mo, W | A ductile-to-brittle transition, usually between −70 °C and room temperature | None, except 9% Ni steel to 77 K |
| HCP | Titanium, magnesium, beryllium | Few slip systems. Toughness depends on the interstitials (oxygen, nitrogen, iron) | Titanium ELI grades to 20 K |

FCC metals have close-packed slip planes in many directions, so dislocations keep moving at any temperature
([Metallurgy](../../fundamentals/metallurgy/#crystals-grains-and-dislocations)). In BCC metals, dislocations need
thermal energy to move. Take the energy away and the metal cleaves instead of yielding: a carbon steel that is tough in
the workshop can break like glass at −100 °C.

Two exceptions to the simple rule:

- **9% nickel steel** is BCC, but the nickel lowers its transition far enough for LNG and liquid nitrogen tanks, down
  to −196 °C. It is not used colder.
- **304L** is FCC but metastable: cold deformation changes part of it to martensite. It stays tough to 4 K, but the
  martensite makes it sensitive to hydrogen. 316L, with more nickel, is the stable choice.

Choose on structure first and on strength second. The [Material selection](../selection/) chart ranks by strength and
mass. It does not know the temperature or the fluid.

## Contraction: a constant α is wrong

| Material | To 77 K (LN2) | To 20 K (LH2) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aluminium alloys (6061-T6, 5083) | 0.389% | 0.415% |
| Copper (OFHC) | 0.304% | 0.325% |
| Austenitic stainless steel (304, 316) | 0.280% | 0.300% |
| Nickel superalloys (Inconel 718) | 0.224% | 0.239% |
| Ferritic steels (Fe-2.25Ni to Fe-9Ni) | 0.191% | 0.210% |
| Ti-6Al-4V | 0.162% | 0.174% |
| Invar 36 | 0.040% | 0.040% |
| G-10CR glass/epoxy, in plane | 0.210% | 0.238% |
| PTFE | 1.940% | 2.115% |

Integrated contraction from 293 K, (L293 − LT)/L293, from the NIST curve fits.

1. **Most of the movement happens above 77 K.** Austenitic stainless steel has done 93% of its total contraction by
   77 K. A test in LN2 shows almost all the movement of LH2 service.
2. **A constant room-temperature α over-predicts.** For 6061-T6 to 20 K, the library α (23.6 µm/m·K) × 273 K gives
   0.644%: 55% more than the real 0.415%. Above about 200 K a constant α is within about 15%, and the
   [Differential expansion](../../joints/differential-expansion/) tools are good enough, for example for a −54 °C cold
   soak.
3. **The differences cause the trouble.** Aluminium shrinks about 0.1% more than stainless steel. On a 40 mm fit at
   LOX temperature that is 42 µm: as much as the largest interference of an H7/p6 fit.

## Fits and joints change on cooldown

- **An aluminium housing on a steel shaft tightens:** the housing shrinks more. A steel housing on an aluminium part
  loosens, and the part can move or leak.
- **An aluminium flange clamped by stainless steel or A-286 bolts loses preload:** over the grip, the flange shrinks
  more than the bolt. In the worked example the joint loses 29% of its preload.
- **To keep the preload,** use conical spring (Belleville) washers; bolts that shrink as much as the flange; a
  low-expansion spacer under the nut, sized so that the stack and the bolt shrink by the same amount; or a higher
  assembly preload, checked against the bolt and the warm case.
- **Size each fit at both temperatures.** The room-temperature limits come from
  [Fits & limits](../../drawing/fits-and-limits/), and the movement comes from here. The warm case governs assembly;
  the cold case governs service.

## Restraint and chilldown

A part that cannot shrink takes the strain as stress: σ = E × contraction. A 316L line held between two anchors at LOX
temperature would see 193 GPa × 0.27% = 521 MPa, far past its yield. Cryogenic lines need a way to move: bellows,
expansion loops or sliding supports.

Chilldown adds stress of its own. The wetted surface cools first, a thick flange lags a thin wall, and the first liquid
into a warm line boils and surges. Chill down with cold gas or a small flow first, and give thick parts time to follow.

## Oxygen: ignition, not strength

In LOX the question is whether a material can ignite and then keep burning. The ignition sources are:

- **Particle impact** at bends, valves and places where the flow impinges.
- **Adiabatic compression:** a valve that opens fast onto a dead-end volume heats the gas, and can ignite a polymer
  seat.
- **Friction and mechanical impact:** rubbing parts, a dropped tool.
- **Contamination:** oil, grease or cutting fluid, which ignite far more easily than the metal.

| Material | LOX in tanks and lines | Oxygen at pressure or speed | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Austenitic stainless steel (304L, 316L) | Yes | Check | The default for LOX lines and valves |
| Aluminium alloys | Yes | No | Standard for LOX tanks. Keep it out of valves and impingement points |
| Copper alloys, Monel, nickel 200 | Yes | Yes | The most burn-resistant: used where the flow impinges |
| Nickel superalloys (718, 625) | Yes | Check | Between Monel and stainless steel |
| **Titanium alloys** | **No: prohibited** | **No: prohibited** | Ignites on impact or at a fresh scratch, and burns violently |
| Magnesium alloys | No | No | Ignition risk |
| PTFE, PCTFE | Yes, after impact tests | Check | The standard seats and seals |
| Hydrocarbon polymers, oils and greases | No | No | Use PTFE, PCTFE and fluorinated (PFPE) greases |
| Composites (epoxy matrix) | Check | No | Need a LOX-compatible resin or a liner, and impact tests |

**The titanium trap.** Titanium alloys have among the highest specific strengths of the metals, and a selection chart
ranks Ti-6Al-4V high for a pressure vessel. If the vessel holds oxygen, that answer is wrong: titanium can ignite from
an impact or a fresh scratch, and then it burns. No performance index sees this, so screen for the fluid before you
rank.

**Cleanliness is a design requirement.** Clean LOX hardware to a stated particle and non-volatile residue level
(ASTM G93), assemble it with oxygen-compatible lubricants only, and keep it sealed until use. A well-designed part
that carries cutting fluid is a hazard. The metals and the polymers are assessed by ASTM G94 and ASTM G63; LOX impact
by ASTM G86 or NASA-STD-6001 Test 13A.

## Hydrogen: two problems at once

LH2 brings the cold and hydrogen together.

- **Embrittlement.** Hydrogen enters some metals and lowers their ductility. It is worst in warm, high-pressure gas,
  not in the liquid: at 20 K hydrogen hardly diffuses. In austenitic stainless steel the effect peaks near 200 K, so
  chilldown and warm-up count, and the number of cycles counts as much as the time cold.
- **Which metals.** Aluminium alloys, copper and stable austenitic stainless steel (316L, 21-6-9, A-286) resist it.
  Metastable 304L, the nickel superalloys (718, 625, Monel K-500) and high-strength steels are sensitive. Titanium
  absorbs hydrogen and forms brittle hydrides when warm. Hydrogen piping follows ASME B31.12, and ANSI/AIAA G-095A
  covers hydrogen systems as a whole.
- **Permeation and leaks.** Hydrogen leaks through gaps and seals that hold other gases, and permeates polymers and
  microcracked composites.
- **Wide flammability.** Hydrogen burns in air from 4% to 75%, and its flame is almost invisible in daylight. Vent it
  safely, and detect it.

## Seals and polymers in the cold

Elastomers go glassy between about −20 °C (FKM) and −120 °C (silicone), so no elastomer O-ring seals at 77 K.
Cryogenic seals are:

- **Spring-energised PTFE seals:** the spring keeps the load on as the PTFE shrinks.
- **Metal seals:** C-rings and E-rings, often with a soft plating (silver, gold, indium) on the seal face.
- **PCTFE or PTFE seats** in valves, held by metal so that the shrinkage closes on the seat, not away from it.

Polymers shrink 3 to 7 times as much as metals: PTFE 2.1% against 0.30% for stainless steel to 20 K. A PTFE seat or
seal in a metal body loosens unless the design keeps it loaded. See [Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/)
and [Plastics](../plastics/).

## Composites microcrack before they break

Carbon/epoxy gains strength and stiffness when cold. But the resin shrinks much more than the fibre, and the mismatch
drives transverse microcracks in each ply. The cracks join into leak paths long before any structural failure. In the
X-33 composite LH2 tank (1999), microcracks in the inner skin let hydrogen into the honeycomb core; when the tank
warmed, the gas debonded the outer skin. Toughened resins, thin plies and liners are the answers. Where a composite
meets a metal fitting, the difference in contraction loads the bond: see the bonded overlap in
[Differential expansion](../../joints/differential-expansion/).

## Heat leak through supports

A support conducts Q = (A/L) × ∫k dT: its section over its length, times the conductivity integral of its material
between the two temperatures.

| Material | 77 K to 293 K | 20 K to 293 K |
| --- | --- | --- |
| G-10CR glass/epoxy, in plane | 137 W/m | 154 W/m |
| PTFE | 56 W/m | 68 W/m |
| Ti-6Al-4V | 1160 W/m | 1300 W/m |
| Inconel 718 | 1820 W/m | 2100 W/m |
| Stainless steel (304, 316) | 2600 W/m | 2910 W/m |
| Al 6061-T6 | 27 600 W/m | 31 000 W/m |
| Copper, OFHC (RRR 100) | 91 000 W/m | 164 000 W/m |

Conductivity integrals from the NIST curve fits.

For the same section and length, G-10CR conducts about one twentieth as much as stainless steel, and Ti-6Al-4V less
than half. Aluminium and copper suit thermal straps, not supports. Every watt boils fluid: one watt boils 22 mL of LN2
an hour, and 1.4 L of LHe.

## Worked example: a LOX valve on a test stand

A LOX valve on an engine test stand has a 6061-T6 body with a 316L seat sleeve pressed into it. Eight M8 A-286 bolts
clamp a 6061-T6 flange with a 20 mm grip, at 12 kN each; the seal needs at least 6 kN a bolt. A 2 m 316L line joins
the valve between two anchors. A bracket in Ti-6Al-4V was proposed to save mass. The valve stands on four G-10CR legs,
each 10 × 10 mm and 100 mm long, on a frame at 293 K. Service is LOX at 90.2 K. These are the default values of the
calculator.

1. **Screen the bracket.** Ti-6Al-4V in LOX: prohibited. Make the bracket in 316L or Inconel 718.
2. **The line.** 316L shrinks 0.270% to 90.2 K, so the 2 m line gets 5.40 mm shorter. Held at both anchors it would
   carry 521 MPa, far past its 220 MPa yield. Add a bellows or a loop.
3. **The sleeve.** The fit is H7/p6 on 40 mm: 1 to 42 µm of interference at 20 °C. The aluminium body shrinks
   0.376% and the sleeve 0.270%, so the fit tightens by 42.5 µm, to 44 to 85 µm. At the largest interference the
   contact pressure is 35.5 MPa and the hoop stress in the body 92 MPa, against 275 MPa yield. The fit holds; check
   the press force at assembly with the warm limits.
4. **The flange.** Over the 20 mm grip the aluminium shrinks 21 µm more than the A-286 bolt. The preload falls from
   12 kN to 8.54 kN, a loss of 29%: still above the 6 kN that the seal needs. An Invar spacer of 9.2 mm under each nut,
   with longer bolts, would balance the contraction; conical spring washers would hold the preload instead.
5. **The legs.** Four G-10CR legs conduct 0.53 W from 293 K to 90.2 K: about 0.19 L of LOX a day. The same legs in
   304 stainless steel would conduct 9.95 W, 19 times as much.

## Where this stops

- **Screening data.** Many alloys use the curve of their class, and the ratings are general practice. For a final
  design, take alloy data at temperature from NIST, from MMPDS where it covers the temperature, or from test.
- **Strength in the cold.** FCC metals get stronger as they cool, so room-temperature allowables are conservative for
  strength. They say nothing about toughness: test welds, castings and anything BCC at temperature.
- **Not covered here:** two-phase flow, geysering and pressure surge in lines; cryogenic insulation (multilayer
  insulation, foams); propellant conditioning.

## On the drawing

- **The service fluid and the temperature range** in the notes, for example "LOX service, 90 K to 323 K".
- **The material and its condition:** 316L, Ti-6Al-4V ELI, never an open "stainless steel".
- **Cleanliness for oxygen service:** "Clean for oxygen service to ASTM G93, level …", with the level that your
  programme sets.
- **Fits and preloads stated for assembly at room temperature,** with the cold check recorded in the analysis.
- **No titanium, magnesium or hydrocarbon lubricant** on parts wetted by oxygen.
