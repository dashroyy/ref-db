## Why this matters

A solid rocket motor has more plastic in it than its drawings suggest: back-up rings and seats, insulating bushes and
spacers, closures that keep moisture out in storage, and much of the ground equipment that handles the motor. These
parts are small and cheap, so they get less attention than the case. Then a back-up ring cracks on a cold morning, a
bush falls out of its housing, or a guide made of nylon grows until it binds.

The causes are almost always the same five: the cold end, creep, water, the vacuum, and expansion against metal.

<Analogy title="Butter">
  Butter from the fridge is hard and snaps. On the counter it is soft and takes the shape of the knife. Put a weight on
  it and it slowly spreads, even when the weight is small. A plastic is the same: its glass transition is the fridge
  door, and creep is the slow spread.
</Analogy>

## The cold end kills more seals than the hot end

Below its glass transition temperature (Tg) an amorphous polymer, or the amorphous part of a semi-crystalline one, is
hard and brittle, with almost no elastic recovery. Standard FKM stiffens near −20 °C, which is why low-temperature
grades exist. Silicone and PTFE stay useful much colder than almost anything else.

- **Read the minimum temperature, not only the maximum.** A seal that works at 200 °C can leak at −30 °C.
- **A Tg inside the service window** means that the stiffness changes a lot across the window. Nylon (Tg about 60 °C,
  lower when wet) is a common example.
- **Semi-crystalline polymers** (POM, PP, PE, PEEK, PTFE) keep their crystals above Tg, so they stay useful there. The
  amorphous ones (PC, PSU, PEI) soften sharply as they approach Tg.

## Creep and relaxation

A plastic under constant load creeps; held at constant strain, it relaxes. Both are faster when hot, and the rate
rises steeply near the service limit.

- **The service temperatures in this page are continuous-service guides,** not limits under load. A loaded part needs
  creep data at its temperature (ISO 899-1, ASTM D2990).
- **An interference fit loses its pressure with time.** Do not rely on it alone to carry load or to retain a part.
- **PTFE cold-flows** at room temperature. A PTFE seat or back-up ring needs a groove that contains it.

## Water moves dimensions

Nylon absorbs up to about 1.8% water in 24 hours, and more to saturation, and grows as it does. The water also acts
as a plasticiser: stiffness and strength fall, and toughness rises. For precision parts, use POM or PET, which absorb
little. PA11 and PA12 absorb much less than PA6 and PA66.

## Vacuum, outgassing and radiation

- **Outgassing:** in a vacuum, volatiles leave the polymer and condense on optics, sensors and cold surfaces. The
  screening test is ASTM E595, with the usual limits of 1.0% total mass loss (TML) and 0.10% collected volatile
  condensable material (CVCM). Fillers, plasticisers and the cure change the result more than the base polymer does.
  Check the actual compound in the NASA outgassing database.
- **Radiation:** PTFE and the perfluoropolymers are among the worst materials under ionising radiation. PEEK, PEI, PI
  and PPS are the radiation-tolerant choices.

## Plastics against metal

Plastics expand five to ten times more than steel or titanium, and two to five times more than aluminium. Where a
plastic part fits to a metal part, the fit changes a lot with temperature:

- **A plastic bush in a metal bore** is tight when hot and loose when cold.
- **A plastic ring on a metal shaft** is tight when cold and loose when hot.
- **A pressed-in bush bore closes** by about the interference. Machine the bore after pressing, or size it for the
  closure.

The physics is the same as for metal fits ([Differential expansion](../../joints/differential-expansion/#fits-that-change-with-temperature)),
with much larger numbers.

## Worked example: a guide bush in the handling fixture

The handling fixture of the 300 mm motor has a guide bush, ⌀30 mm outside and ⌀25 mm bore, pressed into an
aluminium 6061-T6 bracket (⌀50 mm boss) with 0.05 mm interference at 20 °C. The fixture is stored and used outdoors,
and goes into a vacuum chamber with the motor. Select **Load worked example**. The service window, −54 to +71 °C, is
for this example only.

**1. Screen.** With water absorption of 0.3% at most and low outgassing, 10 of the 37 polymers pass: UHMWPE, PSU,
PEI, PEEK, PEKK, PAI, PTFE, PFA/FEP, ETFE and PCTFE. POM, the usual bush material, fails twice: it is rated only to
−40 °C, and its outgassing is moderate. Nylon fails three times: cold, water and outgassing.

**2. Fit.** Select **In a metal part**. PEEK expands at 47 µm/m·K against 23.6 for the aluminium:

| Temperature | Fit | Contact pressure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| −54 °C | 0.002 mm clearance: **the bush is loose** | 0 |
| 20 °C | 0.050 mm interference | 1.1 MPa |
| +71 °C | 0.086 mm interference | 2.0 MPa |

The 0.05 mm interference is all used up by the cold end. The comparison lists the plastics rated for the window:
PBI keeps 0.049 mm of interference at −54 °C and PAI (Torlon, 31 µm/m·K) keeps 0.034 mm, but PEKK keeps only
0.002 mm, which is no margin. POM, which is not rated for the window, would have 0.142 mm of clearance.

**3. The bore.** Pressing the PEEK bush in closes its bore by 0.052 mm at 20 °C, and by 0.089 mm at +71 °C. Machine the
bore after pressing, and check the running clearance on the guide pin at the hot end.

**4. The decision.** Do not rely on the interference: retain the bush with a shoulder and a retaining ring, or change
to PAI. More interference would keep PEEK tight at −54 °C, but it raises the stress and the relaxation at +71 °C.

## Where this stops

- **Typical, unfilled values at room temperature.** Glass and carbon fill change the modulus, the expansion and the
  strength a lot, and in different directions along and across the flow. Use the grade's datasheet.
- **No creep and no relaxation** in the fit. The pressure at 20 °C after a year at +71 °C is lower than the tool shows.
- **The modulus falls with temperature,** most sharply near Tg. The tool uses the room-temperature value.
- **Ratings are a screen.** Outgassing, radiation and chemical resistance depend on the compound; test or use data
  for the actual grade.

## On the drawing

- **The grade and the form,** not only the polymer: for example "PEEK, unfilled, extruded rod, annealed", with the
  specification or the classification (ASTM D4000) your programme uses.
- **The conditioning before inspection,** for any polymer that absorbs water: measure after conditioning, at 20 °C.
- **Tolerances that the material can hold.** Plastic parts move with temperature and water. Moulded parts follow
  ISO 20457.
- **Retention that does not depend on interference** for any part that must stay in place across the service window.
