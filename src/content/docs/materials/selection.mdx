## Why this matters

The material selection usually has the largest single effect on mass, cost and risk. It is made early, when a change
costs little. It is also the most frequent source of design errors, for a simple reason: designs are sized with the
incorrect number. The *typical* room-temperature yield on a datasheet is not the *minimum* strength at the service
temperature, in the load direction, for the product form that you can buy. This page is about how to make the correct
comparison first. The [specification page](../specification/) is about how to fix the result on a drawing.

## The idea: a performance index

<Analogy title="Choosing a minibus by fuel used per passenger">
  If you must move eight people, the car with the best fuel consumption is not the correct selection. A two-seat sports
  car would win on that value. The important value is the fuel consumption *for each seat*: the combination of
  properties that the function needs. A performance index is the same idea for materials. For a light, strong pressure
  vessel, it is strength divided by density, not strength alone.
</Analogy>

The Ashby method changes a design requirement into that index in four steps:

1. **Function:** what does the part do? (Contain pressure, carry a bending load, conduct heat.)
2. **Objective:** what do you minimise or maximise? (Usually mass, sometimes cost or deflection.)
3. **Constraints:** what must the part always do? (Not yield at proof, not burst, survive 150 °C, be weldable.)
4. **Free variables:** what can you change? (Wall thickness, section size, the material.)

Write the objective. Use the constraint to remove the free variable. The remaining term is a group of material
properties. Maximise that group.

### Worked derivation: a light motor case

A thin cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$ holds a pressure $p$. Its mass is $m = 2\pi r L\,t\,\rho$. The wall must
carry the hoop stress without exceeding the allowable $\sigma$: $t = p\,r/\sigma$. Substitute the free variable $t$:

$$
m = 2\pi r^2 L\,p\;\cdot\;\frac{\rho}{\sigma}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\text{maximise}\;\; M = \frac{\sigma}{\rho}
$$

The design sets all the terms before the dot. Only $\rho/\sigma$ is a property of the material. This is the specific
strength, which is the same index as for a light tie in tension. This is why the
[case-sizing page](../../motor/case-sizing/) gives $p_bV/W$.

### Shape changes the index

<IndexShapes />

For a beam of square section with a stiffness target, the deflection changes as $1/(E\,I)$, with $I \propto A^2$. When
you remove the area, you get $m \propto \rho/E^{1/2}$. For a panel of fixed width, $I \propto t^3$, so
$m \propto \rho/E^{1/3}$. With logarithms, each index becomes a straight line on a log–log property chart:

$$
M = \frac{E^{1/2}}{\rho} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \log E = 2\log\rho + 2\log M
$$

This is a line with a slope of 2. All the materials on that line have the same performance for the function. All the
materials above it are better. This is the dashed guideline on the chart below.

<Analogy title="Contour lines on a map">
  A property chart is a map of all materials. The families are countries: metals in one area, polymers lower down, and
  composites in a long thin island. A performance index is a set of contour lines across the full map. If you move
  along one line, the material changes but the performance does not. If you move uphill, perpendicular to the lines,
  the part becomes better.
</Analogy>

### Fracture needs its own index

The strength-for-weight index gives the best score to the strongest alloys. But toughness usually decreases as
strength increases. Two fracture indices show the other side:

- **Yield before break,** $K_{IC}/\sigma_y$. The critical crack size at yield changes with its square:
  $a_c = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_{IC}}{Y\sigma_y}\right)^2$. A large value is more tolerant.
- **Leak before break,** $K_{IC}^2/\sigma_y$. This gives the largest wall that a pressure vessel can have, and still
  leak before it breaks (see the [leak-before-break screen](../../motor/case-sizing/#leak-before-break)).

Alone, these indices select soft, tough metals that are not suitable for a light case. When you use them **together**
with the mass index, they show the real trade: how much toughness you lose for each kilogram that you save.

## The chart

Select the item that you design, select families, and apply the necessary properties. Then click a point (or a table
row) to make it the reference for the guideline. Hover to see the values.

- **Find a material** marks the materials whose names contain the text, also when the filters dim them.
- **Colour by index value** colours each passing material on a scale from the lowest index (blue) to the highest (red).
  Materials that fail a filter are grey.
- **Swap X and Y** exchanges the axes.
- **Service conditions** move the comparison away from room temperature:
  - **Design temperature.** Yield and ultimate strength are scaled with a family-typical retention curve, and materials
    whose maximum service temperature is lower are removed. Alloys in one family can differ a lot: 2219 aluminium
    keeps its strength much better than 7075. Use the curve to screen, and the allowable at temperature to design.
  - **Fatigue.** For metals, an estimated fatigue strength from the ultimate, with the Marin factors for surface,
    loading, size and reliability ([Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/)), and a Goodman reduction for a mean stress.
    The two fatigue indices rank materials for parts that cycle many times. These are mean estimates, not allowables.

## Worked example: choosing a case material

**Function:** contain the chamber pressure. **Objective:** minimum mass. **Constraints:** weldable, a service
temperature of at least 150 °C, and metallic (for this example). **Free variable:** wall thickness.

1. **Rank by** $\sigma_y/\rho$ (the default of the chart). The first eight, in kN·m/kg:

   | # | Material | $\sigma_y/\rho$ | $K_{IC}/\sigma_y$ (√m) | Crack at yield, $Y = 1.12$ |
   | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
   | 1 | Maraging 18Ni-300 | 233 | 0.043 | 0.47 mm |
   | 2 | Ti-6Al-2Sn-4Zr-2Mo | 218 | 0.066 | 1.09 mm |
   | 3 | AerMet 100 | 218 | 0.067 | 1.13 mm |
   | 4 | Maraging 18Ni-250 | 211 | 0.059 | 0.89 mm |
   | 5 | Ti-6Al-4V | 199 | 0.085 | 1.84 mm |
   | 6 | Al-Li 2195 | 194 | 0.057 | 0.83 mm |
   | 7 | Ti-6Al-4V ELI | 187 | 0.120 | 3.68 mm |
   | 8 | D6AC | 185 | 0.048 | 0.59 mm |

2. **Check fracture.** The lightest selection, maraging 300, tolerates a crack of only approximately half a millimetre
   at yield. This is near the limit of reliable inspection. Maraging 250 loses 10% of the mass index, but it tolerates
   a crack almost two times larger. Ti-6Al-4V ELI loses 20% of the mass index against maraging 300, but it tolerates a
   crack almost eight times deeper. These numbers show the yield-before-break trade.
3. **Check what the chart cannot show.** D6AC is eighth on mass and low on toughness, but it was used on many large
   motors. The reasons are not on the chart. Large forgings and plate are available, the heat treatment and welding are
   well known, the indicative price in the database is approximately a quarter to a third of that of maraging steel, and
   many years of test data support it. Titanium has cost and availability problems in large sections. Maraging steels
   have price and supply risk from cobalt.
4. **Composites.** Select the composites family. Filament-wound carbon/epoxy is far above all the metals, but this is
   the strength in the *fibre direction*. A real filament-wound case is a laminate, and the strength of its wall is a
   fraction of that number. It also has its own failure modes (matrix cracking, impact damage, stress rupture). But it
   usually gives the lowest mass. This is why many modern cases are composite.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the example shows">
  An index ranks. It does not decide. Use the mass index to find the candidates, and a second index to show their
  cost in other properties. Use engineering judgement for all the items that the chart cannot plot: supply, process,
  cost, compatibility and previous use.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Screening data, not allowables">
  The chart uses typical room-temperature values from REF·DB v1. Designs are sized with statistically based allowables
  (for example MMPDS A-basis or B-basis) for the specific product form, thickness, grain direction and temperature, from
  the approved data of your organisation.
</Aside>

- **Temperature.** The strength and toughness at the service temperature, also after any thermal exposure, decide the
  selection. The maximum-temperature filter is only a screen.
- **Product form and direction.** Plate, forging and extrusion are different. The short-transverse direction of thick
  plate can be much weaker and less tough than the rolling direction. See
  [specifying materials](../specification/#product-form-and-grain-direction).
- **Environment.** Stress-corrosion cracking, hydrogen embrittlement, and compatibility with propellant, fluids and
  coatings remove candidates that the chart ranks high.
- **Cost and availability** are real constraints that you must consider from the start. They also change.
