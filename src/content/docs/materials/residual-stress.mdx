## Why this matters

Each weld, each machined or ground surface, and each quenched or straightened part has stresses that no load analysis
shows. They can be as large as yield. They add to the service stress at each point, and they are always present:

- **Fatigue.** Residual tension decreases the life. Residual compression from peening or rolling increases it, often
  by several times.
- **Fracture.** Residual tension decreases the flaw size that a part can tolerate.
- **Stress-corrosion cracking.** This needs only sustained tension, and residual stress is always sustained. A
  high-strength steel part can crack in storage with no load on it.
- **Distortion.** If you machine away a stressed layer, the part moves.

On a solid motor, residual stress is in the welds of a high-strength steel case, the ground surfaces near them, rolled
threads, peened fillets, and quenched aluminium skirts and closures. There are also thermal residual stresses in
composite cases, bonded joints and the propellant grain.

## What it is

A residual stress is the elastic response to a **misfit**. A region has yielded, transformed or shrunk, so that it does
not fit the material around it. The material around it forces it to fit. With no external load, the stresses must
balance across all cuts through the part, with no net force and no net moment:

$$
\int_A \sigma_{res}\,dA = 0
$$

$$
\int_A \sigma_{res}\,y\,dA = 0
$$

Thus tension in one location always has compression in a different location. When machining removes part of the
section, the stresses in the remaining material balance again, and the part distorts.

<Analogy title="Toughened glass">
  To make toughened glass, you heat a glass panel and blow cold air on its surfaces. The surfaces become solid first.
  The hot core then cools and shrinks. It pulls the surfaces into compression, and the core stays in tension. A scratch
  in the surface must overcome that compression before it can grow, so the glass is several times stronger. But the
  stored energy is still there. If a crack gets through to the core, the full panel breaks into small pieces. A Prince
  Rupert's drop works in the same way, in one drop of glass.
</Analogy>

## Where it comes from

| Process | Where | Sign and size | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Welding | Weld and heat-affected zone | Tension near yield along the weld; compression away from it | Lower across the weld. Phase changes in steels change the pattern |
| Machining | A thin surface layer, up to some tenths of a millimetre | Compression from sharp tools and light cuts; tension from heat | Abusive grinding leaves tension and a burned, hardened-again layer |
| Quenching | Through the section | Non-transforming alloys: surface compression, core tension | Thick aluminium sections distort when machined; transforming steels can have the opposite pattern |
| Shot peening, deep rolling, thread rolling, cold expansion of holes | Surface, approximately 0.1–1 mm deep | Compression, a large fraction of yield | Balanced by a small tension below; lost if machined off after |
| Straightening and forming | Where it bent | Tension on one side | For this reason, straightening of hardened high-strength steel is restricted |
| Autofrettage | Bore of a thick cylinder | Hoop compression at the bore | Yield the inner wall one time, then unload |
| Thermal mismatch | Composites at cure, bonded joints, the propellant grain | Either sign | See [Differential expansion](../../joints/differential-expansion/) and [Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/) |

Assembly stresses have the same effect. An interference-fit bush or an over-tightened fitting keeps a sustained tension
that no service load explains.

## How it adds to service stress

While all the material is elastic, the residual stress and the service stress add. This has three results.

**The static strength of a ductile part almost does not change.** The residual stress balances itself, so it yields
away as the part approaches collapse. This is not true for brittle parts, buckling and distortion. In these cases the
residual stress stays.

**Yield sets a limit.** If the service stress plus the residual stress is more than yield, the excess yields away, and
less residual stress stays after the load:

$$
\sigma'_{res} = \min\left(\sigma_{res},\; F_{ty} - \sigma_{max}\right)
$$

There is the same limit in compression. Thus one large load (a proof test or an overload) is a mechanical stress
relief, and it can leave compression at notches that yielded. Also, large fatigue cycles remove residual stress. Thus
residual stress has the largest effect in high-cycle fatigue.

**In fatigue it is a mean stress.** At the surface where cracks start, the residual stress adds to the service mean:

$$
\sigma_{m,eff} = \sigma_m + \sigma'_{res}
$$

Then use Goodman or a different mean-stress rule, as on the [Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/) page. Tension moves
the cycle towards failure. Compression moves it away. This page, as the fatigue page, gives credit to compression only
until it cancels the tensile mean stress.

**In fracture it is a crack-driving stress, with or without a load:**

$$
K = Y\,(\sigma + \sigma_{res})\sqrt{\pi a}
$$

The critical flaw depth changes as $1/(\sigma + \sigma_{res})^2$. Thus even a small residual stress causes a large
decrease in the tolerable flaw size ([Leak before break](../../verify/leak-before-break/)). Fitness-for-service
procedures such as BS 7910 and API 579-1 give residual stresses where they are not measured: up to yield in as-welded
joints, and a fraction of yield after post-weld heat treatment.

**In stress corrosion it is often the only cause.** Stress-corrosion cracking needs a susceptible material, an
environment and sustained tension. Residual tension always gives the tension. A flaw grows when $K$ from the residual
stress alone is more than $K_{ISCC}$. Typical examples are high-strength steels, and 7075-T6 aluminium loaded across its
short-transverse grain. This is why over-aged tempers such as T73 and T7351 exist.

## Autofrettage

If you pressurise a thick cylinder above its first-yield pressure, it yields from the bore outwards to a radius $c$.
When you remove the pressure, the elastic outer wall squeezes the yielded inner wall, and the bore stays in hoop
compression. For a bore of radius $a$ and an outside radius $b$ (Hill, Tresca):

$$
p_A = F_{ty}\left[\ln\frac{c}{a} + \frac{b^2 - c^2}{2b^2}\right]
$$

This goes from first yield at $F_{ty}(b^2-a^2)/2b^2$ to full overstrain at $F_{ty}\ln(b/a)$. In service, the pressure
cycles the bore around a compressive mean. Thus cracks at the bore almost do not open, and the peak stress moves out to
the edge of the yielded zone. Too much overstrain on a thick wall causes **reverse** yield of the bore when you remove
the pressure. In real steels this occurs sooner, because the reverse yield strength decreases after overstrain (the
Bauschinger effect). Autofrettage is used on gun tubes, high-pressure pumps and tubing, and on the metal liners of
composite-overwrapped pressure vessels. The liner yields one time. After that it operates elastically, in compression
at zero pressure.

## Measuring it

| Method | Depth | Part survives? | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| X-ray diffraction | Tens of micrometres | Yes | Surface lattice strain; profiles by removing layers with electropolishing (EN 15305) |
| Hole drilling | To approximately 1–2 mm | Small hole | Strain-gauge rosette around a drilled hole (ASTM E837) |
| Neutron diffraction | Centimetres | Yes | At a neutron source; through-section maps (ISO 21432) |
| Contour method | Whole cut plane | No | Cut the part, and measure how the cut face deforms |
| Slitting, ring-core, deep-hole drilling | Through thickness | Partly or no | Strain released by a cut or core |
| Barkhausen noise, temper etch | Surface | Yes | Screening for grinding burns, not values |

Almen strips control the shot-peening intensity (SAE J442 and J443). They measure the process, not the part. Each
measurement gives an average over its own gauge volume. Steep gradients near the surface or a weld need a method that
is applicable to them.

## Relieving and controlling it

- **Thermal stress relief.** At temperature, the yield strength decreases and creep relaxes the stress. Slow cooling
  prevents its return. Stay below the tempering temperature of quenched-and-tempered steels, or they become softer.
  The temperature and time come from the material specification.
- **Weld, then heat-treat.** High-strength steel cases are usually welded before their final hardening and tempering,
  which also relieves the welds. A repair weld after heat treatment causes near-yield tension again and an untempered
  heat-affected zone. Thus it needs the full heat treatment again, or it is not permitted.
- **Mechanical relief.** After quenching, aluminium plate and forgings are stretched or compressed by a few per cent
  (the T651, T7351 and T652 tempers), because heat would over-age the alloy. A proof test relieves tensile residual
  stress where it causes yield.
- **Process control.** Control the welding sequence and heat input ([Welding](../../make/welding/#shrinkage-and-distortion)).
  Use sharp tools and light finishing cuts. Grind
  with controlled feeds and coolant, and inspect for burns. For parts that easily distort: machine roughly, relieve the
  stress, then finish.
- **Put compression where cracks start.** Use shot peening, laser peening, deep rolling, threads rolled after heat
  treatment, and cold-expanded holes. Peen after the last machining operation. Check at temperature, because peened
  layers relax when hot.

## Worked example: residual stress in the 300 mm motor's hardware

The starting values in each tab of the calculator are the values of this example. The residual stresses here are
**assumed for the example**. On real hardware, you measure them or your assessment procedure gives them.

1. **The case weld.** At MEOP, the D6AC case wall has 1094 MPa of hoop stress, and $K_{IC} = 70$ MPa√m
   ([Leak before break](../../verify/leak-before-break/)). A long, shallow surface flaw along a longitudinal weld
   ($Y = 1.12$) becomes critical at $\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{70}{1.12 \times 1094}\right)^2 = 1.04$ mm depth. Assume
   that 290 MPa of residual stress (20% of yield) stays across the weld after the final heat treatment. The critical
   depth decreases to **0.65 mm**. The 11 MPa proof test helps. The proof stress plus the residual stress,
   $1204 + 290$, is more than the 1450 MPa yield. Thus the residual stress relaxes to $1450 - 1204 = 246$ MPa, and the
   critical depth increases again to **0.69 mm**. A repair weld after heat treatment would cause near-yield tension
   again, in an untempered heat-affected zone.
2. **The case in storage.** When the case has no pressure, the 246 MPa still acts. At a 0.5 mm flaw, it gives
   $K = 1.12 \times 246 \times \sqrt{\pi \times 0.0005} = 10.9$ MPa√m, permanently. For high-strength steels in moist or
   salty air, $K_{ISCC}$ can be a small fraction of $K_{IC}$. If it is less than 10.9 MPa√m for this steel, heat
   treatment and storage environment, that flaw grows with no pressure in the case. Enter your $K_{ISCC}$ in the
   **Fracture & SCC** tab to see the deepest flaw that is safe in storage.
3. **The actuator bracket.** The 4340 bracket from the [Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/#worked-example-an-actuator-bracket)
   page lasts 6.2 service lives as machined. Its fillet has local cycles of 444 on 444 MPa in handling, and 320 on
   107 MPa in firings.
   - **Shot peened,** with −400 MPa at the surface. The handling mean decreases to 44 MPa, the equivalent amplitude
     decreases from 680 to 460 MPa, and the handling life increases from 16,000 to 126,000 cycles. Firings get less
     benefit, because their mean becomes compressive and compression gets no credit. The bracket lasts **25 service
     lives, 4.1 times longer**.
   - **Abusively ground,** with +300 MPa. The first handling cycle takes the surface to yield, so the residual stress
     relaxes to 292 MPa and stays there. The handling life decreases to 1,670 cycles, and the bracket lasts **0.73
     service lives**. It fails in service, because of a surface treatment that no drawing shows.
4. **Autofrettage.** Motor cases at 10 MPa never need it, but high-pressure test equipment does. A steel tube has a
   10 mm bore and a 25 mm outside diameter, with $F_{ty} = 1000$ MPa. It operates at 300 MPa. Without autofrettage, its
   bore has 714 MPa of Tresca stress. Autofrettage at 650 MPa yields the inner 23% of the wall, and leaves **−548 MPa**
   of hoop stress at the bore. At working pressure, the bore is still in compression (−133 MPa). The peak stress is
   537 MPa at the edge of the yielded zone, which is **25% lower**. Pressure cycles at the bore now go between −548 and
   −133 MPa, so a crack there almost does not open. 650 MPa is almost the best value for this tube. More overstrain
   increases the peak again.

## Where this stops

- **Elastic–perfectly plastic, uniaxial.** Real relaxation depends on the cyclic stress–strain curve, multiaxial
  stress and the number of cycles. Residual stresses also decrease at temperature.
- **Uniform residual stress across a flaw.** Real profiles change quickly with depth. Fitness-for-service procedures
  integrate them through the crack.
- **Autofrettage without the Bauschinger effect,** with Tresca and elastic unloading. Codes such as ASME VIII Division 3
  use corrected methods.
- **Values come from measurement or your assessment procedure,** not from typical numbers such as the numbers in this
  example.

## On the drawing

- **Stress relief.** Heat treatment after welding, forming or straightening, with the temperature and time of the
  material specification. Also tell if straightening or repair welding is permitted.
- **Shot peening.** Areas and masking, intensity (Almen), coverage and media to the applicable specification (for
  example SAE AMS 2430). Peening must follow the last machining operation.
- **Grinding.** Inspection for grinding burns on high-strength steel, and limits on stock removal.
- **Thread rolling after heat treatment** for fatigue-critical fasteners. See [Threads](../../joints/threads/).
- **Stress-relieved tempers** (T651, T7351) for aluminium parts machined from thick stock.
- **Autofrettage pressure,** and the verification after it, for autofrettaged parts.
