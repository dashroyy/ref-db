## Why this matters

A datasheet says that an alloy has a yield strength of 460 MPa. This is a **typical** value, the middle of the test
results. Approximately half of all production material is weaker. If you use it in a margin calculation, the margin is
not real. A **design allowable** is a lower limit on the strength of the material that will be built, at the condition
that it will be in. If you use a typical value in a margin, the design review will find the error.

## The bases

| Basis | Meaning | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A-basis** | 99% of the population is above it, with 95% confidence | Single load paths, where one failure is not contained |
| **B-basis** | 90% of the population is above it, with 95% confidence | Redundant structure, where the load moves to other paths (if your rules permit it) |
| **S-basis** | The minimum of the specification, with no statistical support of its own | Often treated as A-basis. Check the rules of your programme |
| **Typical** | The mean of the test data | Comparison of materials. Never for a margin |

The definitions of A-basis and B-basis come from aerospace practice (MMPDS for metals, CMH-17 for composites). Civil
aircraft rules (14 CFR 25.613) tell when to use each. Most space and defence programmes use the same logic.

## The statistics behind them

A basis value is a **one-sided tolerance limit**: the mean of the tests, minus a multiple of their standard deviation:

$$
x_\text{basis} = \bar{x} - k\,s
$$

There are two percentages in the definition. The **proportion** (99% or 90%) tells how much of the population must be
above the value. The **confidence** (95%) covers the fact that $\bar{x}$ and $s$ are only estimates from a sample. With
infinite data, $k$ would be the value of the normal distribution: 2.326 for 99% and 1.282 for 90%. With a finite sample,
it is larger. It comes from the noncentral $t$-distribution:

$$
k = \frac{t'_{0.95}\!\left(n-1,\; z_p\sqrt{n}\right)}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

| Tests, $n$ | 5 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 100 | ∞ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $k$, B-basis | 3.407 | 2.355 | 1.926 | 1.778 | 1.527 | 1.282 |
| $k$, A-basis | 5.741 | 3.981 | 3.295 | 3.064 | 2.684 | 2.326 |

The calculator calculates these values exactly. Its tests compare them with the published tables. CMH-17 also gives
closed-form approximations, which agree within 1% for ten or more tests.

<Analogy title="Sizing a doorway for a whole town">
  You measure twenty people, and you want a doorway that 99 of each 100 people in the town can go through. The tallest
  of your twenty people is not sufficient, because the town has taller people that you did not measure. Thus you add an
  allowance. The fewer people you measure, the larger the allowance must be. A basis value is the same idea in the
  opposite direction: a strength that almost all of the material that you did not test will still be above.
</Analogy>

The statistics have assumptions, and these can be incorrect:

- **A normal distribution.** Real data can be skewed. Handbook practice tests the fit, and uses Weibull, lognormal or a
  non-parametric method when the normal distribution does not fit.
- **One population.** Strength changes more between material lots (heats, batches) than within one lot. Allowables
  come from several lots, and data are pooled only where the statistics permit it. One lot gives a scatter that is too
  small.
- **Data that represent production:** the same specification, process, supplier, thickness and orientation as the
  parts that will fly.

## Knockdowns: before the allowable is yours

A basis value applies to the condition of the test. To get to the design condition, multiply it by **knockdowns**.
These are property ratios from data, not safety factors:

| Knockdown | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Temperature, and time at temperature | Strength decreases with temperature; short-time and long-exposure allowables are different curves |
| Direction | In thick wrought products, the short-transverse (through-thickness) strength and ductility can be much lower than longitudinal |
| Thickness and product form | The quench rate decreases with thickness, so heavy sections do not get thin-section properties; plate, forging and extrusion are different |
| Environment | Moisture and heat ("hot-wet") for composites; corrosion and stress corrosion for metals |
| Joints and welds | The weld efficiency comes from the code and the procedure qualification, not from the parent metal |
| Process | Castings and additively manufactured parts have allowables related to the qualified process and supplier |

## How the allowable feeds the margin

$$
MS = \frac{\text{allowable}}{FS \times \text{applied stress}} - 1
$$

Use the allowable on the correct basis, at the design temperature, in the loaded direction, for the product form used.
Check yield and ultimate separately, each against its own load level and factor. The factor of safety comes from the
requirements of your programme. Each margin report must tell **which allowable, from which source, on which basis, at
which condition**. A value such as "σ = 880 MPa" alone will get questions at each review until somebody finds its
source.

## Worked example: a plate lot on a single load path

The data are illustrative: twenty yield-strength tests from one lot of aluminium plate, longitudinal, at room
temperature. The mean is 460.0 MPa, the standard deviation is 9.0 MPa (2.0% scatter), and the lowest result is
444 MPa. These are the starting values of the calculator.

1. **Factors for $n = 20$:** $k_B = 1.926$ and $k_A = 3.295$.
2. **Basis values:** B-basis $= 460.0 - 1.926 \times 9.0 = 442.6$ MPa, and A-basis $= 460.0 - 3.295 \times 9.0 =
   430.3$ MPa. The two values are below the lowest test, because they are limits for the population, not for the
   sample.
3. **To the design condition:** 100 °C (0.90 from the aluminium screening curve), and a load in the short-transverse
   direction. In this example, your own tests give 88% of the longitudinal value for that direction. A-basis:
   $430.3 \times 0.90 \times 0.88 = 340.8$ MPa. The same knockdowns give 350.6 MPa on B-basis and 364.3 MPa on the
   typical value.
4. **The check.** A single load path needs A-basis. Against an applied stress of 300 MPa, the reserve is
   $340.8/300 = 1.14$. The typical value would give 1.21. Any factor of safety between 1.14 and 1.21 passes with the
   typical value and fails with the real allowable.
5. **More tests give a higher allowable.** With only the first five of these tests, $k_A = 5.741$. The A-basis value
   decreases to 412.6 MPa, the design allowable to 326.8 MPa, and the reserve to 1.09.

## Where this stops

- **Normal data from one population.** Real allowables use several material lots and test the distribution. The
  calculator assumes a normal distribution and one sample.
- **Knockdowns are inputs.** The temperature curve is a screening estimate for the family. Use handbook data or test
  data for the alloy, temper, product form and exposure time.
- **Composites** follow CMH-17 practice, which includes environmental conditioning, lay-up and damage. Their
  allowables are made in a very different way.
- **The factor of safety** comes from your programme, not from this page.

## On the drawing and in the reports

- **Specify the material so that the allowable applies:** the specification, temper, product form and thickness range
  on the drawing. See [Specification](../specification/). If you change any of these, the allowable possibly does not
  apply.
- **Lot acceptance tests** show that each delivery meets its specification. They do not make an allowable.
- **In each margin table,** give the allowable value, source, basis, temperature, direction and product form, adjacent
  to the margin.
