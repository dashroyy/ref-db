## Why this matters

O-rings are the cheapest parts on a motor, and some of the most safety-critical. Case joints, the nozzle-to-case
joint, igniter bosses and closures all use them to hold hot gas at high pressure. If gas gets past a seal, the
combustion gas cuts through metal in seconds. The worst known example is the loss of the Space Shuttle *Challenger* in
1986. The cause was an O-ring in a booster field joint that could not seal on a cold morning.

## How an O-ring seals

<ORingStates />

<Analogy title="A plug-type aircraft door">
  The passenger door of an airliner is slightly larger than its frame, and it fits from the inside. Thus the cabin
  pressure pushes it harder into the frame as the aircraft climbs. An O-ring works in the same way. The squeeze from
  assembly gives it an initial seal. The system pressure then pushes it harder into the low-pressure corner of the
  groove. The seal becomes tighter as the pressure increases, until the pressure is sufficient to push rubber through
  the clearance gap.
</Analogy>

In bulk, rubber is similar to a very stiff liquid. In shape, it is similar to a very soft solid. It is easy to change
its shape, but its volume almost does not change.

<Analogy title="A water balloon in a box">
  Push down on a water balloon in a box, and it bulges to the sides. Its volume cannot change, only its shape. If the
  box is too small, the balloon has no space to go, and the pressure inside becomes very high. This is why the groove
  *volume* (gland fill) is as important as the depth. The ring must have space to change shape, and space to grow when
  it is hot or when it swells in a fluid.
</Analogy>

## The four numbers

For a ring with a cross-section $d$, in a gland with a depth $g$ (the radial or axial space where it is squeezed) and a
width $w$:

| Quantity | Definition | What goes wrong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Squeeze** | $s = \dfrac{d - g}{d}$ | Too little: no initial seal, especially when cold. Too much: overstress, set, high assembly force |
| **Gland fill** | $\phi = \dfrac{\pi d^2 / 4}{w\,g}$ | More than approximately 90%: no space to expand. Hydraulic lock, cracked groove lands, extrusion |
| **Stretch** | $\varepsilon = \dfrac{D_\text{groove} - ID}{ID}$ | Too much makes the section thinner and decreases the life; negative leaves the ring loose, and it can roll |
| **Extrusion gap** | Diametral clearance at the low-pressure side | Too wide for the pressure and hardness: the gap cuts small pieces from the rubber |

When you stretch a ring onto a groove, its cross-section becomes thinner. With constant volume, the stretched section
is:

$$
d' = \frac{d}{\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}}
$$

Thus a stretch of 2% decreases the section by approximately 1%, and it also decreases the squeeze by approximately 1%.

### Nominal is not enough

All of these numbers change across the tolerance band, and the extreme values cause the failures. A 5.33 mm section has
a tolerance of approximately ±0.13 mm. Alone, this changes the squeeze by approximately ±2.5 percentage points. Add the
tolerances of the groove, the bore and the piston, and let the parts sit off-centre (the gland is then shallow on one
side and deep on the other). A nominal squeeze of 19% can then be anywhere from approximately 13% to 25%.

Then add the changes that occur after assembly:

- **Temperature.** Elastomers expand approximately ten times more than steel (approximately $1.6\times10^{-4}$/K). Cold
  rings shrink and lose squeeze. Hot rings grow and fill the groove.
- **Fluid swell.** A volume increase of a few per cent in service is normal, and the groove must have space for it.
- **Compression set.** Over time and at temperature, the ring takes a permanent set, and it pushes back with less
  force. Include this in the minimum squeeze.
- **Joint opening.** Under pressure, case joints turn, bores expand and flanges lift. Thus the gap that the ring must
  fill becomes larger exactly when the pressure arrives. The bolt preload keeps a flanged gland closed. See
  [Bolted joints](../../joints/bolted-joints/). [Sealing strategy](../sealing-strategy/) tells where to put the seal
  so that the gap almost does not move, and how to back it up and do a leak check on it.

## The Challenger lesson: the seal must follow the joint

<Aside type="caution" title="A seal is a spring, and cold springs are slow">
  In the field joint of a Shuttle booster, the ignition pressure made the joint turn a little and open the gap that the
  O-rings sealed. A warm O-ring expands sufficiently fast to follow. On the cold morning of 28 January 1986
  (approximately 2 °C ambient, and the joint was colder), the fluoroelastomer rings recovered too slowly. Hot gas got
  past before they sealed, and the joint failed approximately one minute into the flight.
</Aside>

During the Rogers Commission hearings, Richard Feynman showed the effect with a sample of the O-ring rubber clamped in
iced water. When he released the clamp, the rubber stayed flat and did not spring back. Two design rules come directly
from this:

1. **Size the minimum squeeze with the joint open under pressure, at the coldest temperature.** The "joint opening"
   input of the calculator does this.
2. **Do not use the resilience of rubber to follow motion near its low-temperature limit.** Specify the compound by its
   low-temperature recovery (TR-10 and resilience tests), heat the joint, or design the joint so that it does not open.
   The redesigned booster joint added a capture feature to limit the rotation, a third O-ring and joint heaters. The
   [viscoelasticity page](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/#worked-example-a-fluoroelastomer-seal) shows why: when
   cold, all the recovery processes in the rubber are thousands of times slower.

Two more lessons about joints:

- **Redundancy is real only if the two seals work.** A primary and a secondary O-ring with a leak-check port between
  them is standard. Show that the secondary can still seal when the joint is open and the primary has already let gas
  past.
- **A leak check pushes the primary O-ring in the incorrect direction.** Pressure between two rings pushes the primary
  towards the motor side of its groove. Thus at ignition, it must first move across the groove before it can seal. The
  leak-check pressure and procedure are part of the seal design.

## Calculator

The calculator works in two steps:

1. **Design from the hardware.** Enter the bore and the plug (or the rod, or the groove diameter of a face seal), the
   pressure and the targets. **Design the ring and groove** selects the section from the diameter, a stock ring with
   1–5% stretch (or against the pressure side of a face groove), the groove for the target squeeze and fill, and back-up
   rings if the extrusion check needs them. It writes the result into the fields.
2. **Check the gland.** Change the minimum and maximum values to your real tolerances. The calculator checks the worst
   case at the tolerance corners and the temperature extremes, after compression set, and with the joint opening. It
   also shows:
   - the minimum and maximum squeeze across the full service temperature range;
   - a Monte Carlo of 40,000 assemblies at the coldest temperature after compression set, with each dimension normal
     across its limits. This tells how rare the worst case is;
   - a table of what goes on the groove drawing: ring, groove dimensions, corner radii, lead-in chamfer, surface finish
     and extrusion gap.

The housing material comes from the [material library](../../materials/selection/), so its expansion is the same as
in the other tools. For a ring that is not in the class A list, select **Other** and type the section and the ID.

## Worked example: a nozzle-to-case radial seal

These are the defaults of the calculator: a static radial seal in a piston-type closure, a 150 mm bore at 10 MPa, a
steel housing, and a service range of −40 to +60 °C. The ring has a 5.33 mm section and a 139.29 mm stock ID. The
groove root is Ø141.45–141.55, the width is 7.0–7.2 mm, the bore is Ø150.000–150.063, and the plug is
Ø149.860–149.900. The parts can sit off-centre.

1. **Nominal.** The gland depth is $(150.03 - 141.50)/2 = 4.27$ mm. The stretch onto the root is
   $(141.5 - 139.29)/139.29 = 1.6\%$, which makes the 5.33 section thinner, at 5.29 mm. The squeeze is
   $(5.29 - 4.27)/5.29 = 19.3\%$, and the fill is 73%. These values are well in the band.
2. **Worst conditions.** Fully off-centre, the gland is from 4.16 to 4.41 mm. The maximum squeeze (largest ring, hot,
   with 5% swell) is 25%. The minimum squeeze (smallest ring, most stretch, −40 °C) is 13.5%, and **10.8% after 20%
   compression set**. This is just above the 10% screening minimum.
3. **Extrusion.** The clearance can be up to 0.203 mm. At 10 MPa, a 70 Shore ring needs approximately 0.08 mm, and even
   a 90 Shore ring needs approximately 0.15 mm. **A back-up ring is necessary,** or a tighter fit and a harder compound.
4. **Compound.** Standard FKM usually has a limit of approximately −20 °C. At −40 °C, it does not pass the temperature
   check. Use a low-temperature fluoroelastomer grade or a different compound, and confirm the TR-10.
5. **Joint opening.** Enter 0.25 mm of radial opening under pressure. The minimum squeeze decreases to **6.9%**, and
   the cold-resilience check fails. This is the Challenger situation on a small scale. Make the joint stiffer. Do not
   change the seal.
6. **How rare is the worst case?** With the joint opening back at 0, the Monte Carlo puts the 3σ squeeze at 11.9%,
   and none of 40,000 assemblies is below 10%. The worst case of 10.8% needs the smallest ring, the most stretch, the
   deepest gland, full eccentricity and −40 °C together. Use the worst case for this seal (see
   [Tolerance stack](../../drawing/tolerance-stack/#the-nozzle-seal) for why), but the statistics show where the margin
   is.
7. **Redesign with a back-up ring.** Push **Design the ring and groove**. It keeps the 139.29 × 5.33 ring, moves the
   groove root to Ø141.52–141.62 for 20% nominal squeeze, and adds one back-up ring because of item 3: the groove width
   becomes 8.32–8.52 mm. The minimum squeeze after compression set increases to 11.4%, and the extrusion check passes.
   The compound check still fails until you select a low-temperature grade.

## Choosing the compound

Select the compound for the coldest, the hottest and the chemically worst conditions, in that sequence. The table in
the calculator checks each compound against your temperature range and medium. These errors are frequent, also for
experienced engineers:

- **EPDM with petroleum grease.** EPDM is very good in water, steam and phosphate-ester fluids. But it swells and fails
  in mineral oil, which includes the assembly lubricant. Specify the lubricant on the drawing, adjacent to the
  compound.
- **Standard FKM when cold.** It is very good when hot and in fuels, but standard grades become stiff at approximately
  −20 °C. Low-temperature grades are available. Specify one by its TR-10.
- **Silicone in dynamic duty.** It has a wide temperature range, but poor tear strength and abrasion strength. Use it
  for static seals only.
- **Oxygen service.** Usual elastomers can ignite in oxygen. LOX seals and high-pressure oxygen seals use specially
  tested materials and controlled cleanliness.
- **The brittle point is not the limit.** The seal fails at a temperature much higher than the brittle point. Use TR-10
  or resilience data.

## Groove details that decide the outcome

- **Surface finish.** Static sealing faces are usually approximately Ra 0.8 µm (finer for gases and vacuum), dynamic
  counterfaces Ra 0.2–0.4 µm, and groove walls approximately Ra 1.6–3.2 µm. The machining lay must not cross the seal.
  See [Surface finish](../../drawing/surface-finish/).
- **Lead-in chamfers** of 15–20°, deburred and blended, on all edges that the ring moves over. Assembly cuts more
  O-rings than service failures, and you cannot see a cut ring after it is installed.
- **Groove corner radii** sufficiently small for the ring to sit correctly, but never sharp.
- **Back-up rings** on the low-pressure side, or on the two sides if the pressure can reverse. Add their width to the
  groove.
- **Runout of the groove root to its pilot.** The calculator assumes that the groove is coaxial with the part that
  centres it. With a general tolerance, it is possibly not coaxial, and each 0.1 mm of runout decreases the squeeze by
  approximately one point. See
  [GD&T](../../drawing/gdt/#worked-example-the-aft-closure-its-bolts-and-the-nozzle-seal-groove), and
  [Tolerance stack](../../drawing/tolerance-stack/#the-nozzle-seal) for the tolerances that decide the squeeze.

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Screening, not a seal qualification">
  The squeeze bands, the fill limit and the extrusion table are typical guidance, not ISO 3601-2 dimensions. Real joint
  seals are qualified by test at temperature, with the joint deflecting as it does in service, and with a direct
  measurement of the low-temperature recovery of the compound.
</Aside>
